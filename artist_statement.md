I build instruments of attention. Portraits of artists I want to be in communion with. A digital portal to the sky my ancestors and I share across centuries. A sandbox of volcanic ash where visitors reshape a living ecosystem with their hands. A tarot deck for learning the programming language that governs how light appears on every modern screen. The forms are very different; the question underneath them is the same: what does it take for an artifact to develop presence?

A plotter drawing of a face is accurate, every structural interval measured, every proportion correct, and it addresses no one. What changes is the hand returning after, laying paint over the machine's skeleton: not correcting it, but completing it. Machine accuracy and human accumulation, in contact, develop what neither produces alone. A face that looks back. The same crossing recurs across every medium: satellite data becomes a planet that looks alive when its stripped atmospheric glow is restored. A celestial map becomes a connection across generations when it is tied to a specific family's migration and shared between two people in the same night sky. Volcanic ash becomes something that responds when it holds the consequence of every gesture made in it. The systems change, machine and hand, data and atmosphere, code and tarot, migration and sky, but the question does not: what can they produce together that neither holds alone?

My practice spans oil painting, interactive installation, real-time digital simulation, generative code, and physical drawing machines. Each medium follows the same logic: find what has been filtered from ordinary perception, build the thing that participates in restoring it, and stay long enough to discover what develops. City light erased the stars, so I render the accurate star field for the room where the work is shown. Satellite sensors stripped Earth's atmospheric glow to protect their optics, so I add it back. The plotter reduces a face to measurable geometry, so the hand can arrive after and bring what measurement cannot carry.

The personal stakes run through all of it. I made my first astronomical work for a child who knew the moon from a children's book but had never felt it as a living presence in the sky. I traced my own family's migration, Irish and Italian ancestors arriving in Argentina under unfamiliar stars, through a digital map that weaves genealogy, Milky Way, and shoreline into a south-up portrait of belonging. I make portraits of artists whose creative practice required inventing themselves against everything their families expected: a personal search for an alternative lineage made of makers.

The tools I build are also part of the work. Hypatia, my astronomical computation library, began as a way to calculate the moon's exact position for that child in Brooklyn. The plotter that draws my portraits follows the lineage of the camera obscura and the portable paint tube: instruments that liberated the hand by taking measurement off its hands. PixelSpirit, my shader tarot deck, was built from the conviction that the grammar of light should be learnable by anyone willing to begin at card zero.

At a moment when attention has become a resource systematically extracted for other people's profit, building things that redirect it toward what is slow, large, and indifferent to our economies feels necessary. The star is still there above the city. The plotter mark still asks whose presence was here. The hand in the ash still has to decide how much to reshape before watching what it has changed.
