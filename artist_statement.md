I build instruments of attention. A real-time astronomical library that calculates where every star is, for this location, at this hour. A wall plotter that draws the structural geometry of a face onto primed canvas before the brush arrives. A sandbox of volcanic ash where visitors reshape a living ecosystem with their hands. A tarot deck for learning the programming language that governs how light appears on every modern screen. The forms are very different; the question underneath them is the same: what does it take to feel something you already know is true?

You can know the stars are above every city ceiling and still never feel them. You can know your ancestors crossed an ocean under unfamiliar constellations and never feel their sky. You can know that a gesture as small as a hand moving through ash can collapse rivers, shift weather, and destroy what took years to grow, and still act as though the scale of consequence matches the scale of effort. The gap between what we know and what we feel is the distance my work tries to cross. Not by providing information. By building the instrument that makes the crossing possible.

My practice spans oil painting, interactive installation, real-time digital simulation, generative code, and physical drawing machines. Each medium follows the same logic: find what has been filtered from ordinary perception, and build the thing that restores it. City light erased the stars, so I render the accurate star field for the room where the work is shown. Satellite sensors stripped Earth's atmospheric glow to protect their optics, so I add it back. The plotter reduces a face to measurable geometry, so the hand can arrive after and bring what measurement cannot carry.

The personal stakes run through all of it. I made my first astronomical work for a child who knew the moon from a children's book but had never felt it as a living presence in the sky. I traced my own family's migration — Irish and Italian ancestors arriving in Argentina under unfamiliar stars — through a digital map that weaves genealogy, Milky Way, and shoreline into a south-up portrait of belonging. I make portraits of artists whose creative practice required inventing themselves against everything their families expected: a personal search for an alternative lineage made of makers.

The tools I build are also part of the work. Hypatia, my astronomical computation library, began as a way to calculate the moon's exact position for that child in Brooklyn. The plotter that draws my portraits follows the lineage of the camera obscura and the portable paint tube: instruments that liberated the hand by taking measurement off its hands. PixelSpirit, my shader tarot deck, was built from the conviction that the grammar of light should be learnable by anyone willing to begin at card zero.

At a moment when attention has become a resource systematically extracted for other people's profit, building things that redirect it toward what is slow, large, and indifferent to our economies feels necessary. The star is still there above the city. The plotter mark still asks whose presence was here. The hand in the ash still has to decide how much to reshape before watching what it has changed.
