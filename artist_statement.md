For me the creation of art is both a process of exploration and discovery, it's a gradual unfolding of an idea, like peeling back the layers of a sedimentary rock, revealing the history embedded within. The meaning is reveal from within, as we build up layers of meaning. 

In that process for me the tools are integral part, there is some knowledge I cannot access if I haven't built the instruments to reach it. Like a telescope, or a microscope, or a paintbrush, or a camera, or a pen. They are extensions of our senses, they allow us to see and understand the world in new ways. They all affect the way we see and understand the world. The way we construct meaning. 

Like the charcoal pens that sketch the first lines of a drawing, that later the brush completes. Some of my tools are digital, like Hypatia, my astronomical library, or Lygia, my graphical library, both respectively help me perceive and simulate the cosmos. Others bridge ancient symbolic knowledge with computational language — like the Pixel Spirit Tarot Deck (2017), fifty cards that translate an old system of divination into the grammar of shader code, an attempt to show that code and myth are both ways of encoding what the world feels like from the inside. They are the fertile ground for imagining artifacts, rituals and symbols that later materialize in artworks.

This impulse to build instruments that expand perception has roots in a practice older than the software. Before I built plotters I trained as a clinical psychologist and worked as an expressive arts therapist. The question I carried from that work is the same one I carry now: what does sustained attention make possible, in an individual and in a group? My earliest artworks were participatory systems. *Communitas* (2010) invited visitors to collaboratively construct a shared image through mutual observation and gesture — no single person controlled the result, and the work only existed in the space between them. *Efecto Mariposa* (2011), an ash-based interactive ecosystem shown at FILE Festival in São Paulo, simulated a world in which a visitor's smallest gesture cascaded into consequences they could not predict or fully contain. These works were instruments for making collective perception visible: not tools of measurement but of encounter.

The paintings in my recent series work the same way. Custom software reads the geometry of a portrait — its proportions, its structural intervals — and translates that structure into vector paths a plotter draws onto primed canvas. The machine lays down the scaffold with precision and without feeling. Then I return with oils, reconstructing the face from memory — not the photograph's memory, but my own. Layer by layer, the painted surface accumulates what the machine cannot carry: the weight of encounter, the residue of attention, the specific moment when a face stops being data and becomes someone.

The astronomical works follow the same logic. Hypatia, my astronomical library, was built because I needed an instrument to access a specific kind of knowledge — the position of stars and planets not as abstracted data but as felt presence, as something happening right now above whoever is looking. That library became the engine for LUNA, which mirrors the current phase of the moon in real time; for Orbitas, which traces the slow luminous paths planets have inscribed since the start of the year; for Weaver, which threads family migration routes — Irish ancestors crossing the Atlantic during the Famine, Italian grandparents sailing from Anacapri to Argentina — into the shared sky overhead. Like the sedimentary layers, these works hold time inside them. They make visible what passes unnoticed: the motion of the cosmos, the correspondence between a moment here and a moment somewhere else, the fact that people separated by oceans and centuries looked up at the same stars.

The personal stakes are not separate from the work. In my family's lineage I cannot trace a single artist. One great-grandmother reportedly burned all her poems upon marriage, as though creativity were a deviation to be corrected, a path to be relinquished in favor of duty. My most recent series, Santos, is a search for an alternative lineage: portraits of figures whose service to humanity was enacted through creation. Artist-saints. The plotter draws their faces; the brush completes them. Each one is a restoration of something that was erased before it could be passed on.

At the moment when machines can produce any image at speed and scale, I keep building instruments by hand and returning to the canvas with a brush. Not as a refusal of technology — I live inside it — but as an insistence on something it cannot replicate: the slow accumulation of attention, the risk of failure, the presence of a body in time. Human intelligence, for me, is fundamentally embodied. It emerges through sensation, intuition, and lived experience. That conviction shapes every tool I build and every mark I make.
