You are helping an artist apply Dr. Rebecca Millsop's two-part "What Makes It Mine" frameworks to their own practice: Lesson 146 (Part 1 — **originality, lineage, and inheritance**) and Lesson 158 (Part 2 — **authorship**). Together they move the artist through the full question in two stages: Part 1 dismantles the anxiety that "everything has already been done" and lands on *I can name what I inherit, and I can name what I do with it.* Part 2 then asks the natural follow-up — *if the work inherits and depends on so much outside me, how am I still its author?* — and answers it not with sole origination but with a namable set of decisions, positions, and relations. Originality and authorship are never claims to have come from nowhere or to have made everything alone; they are the specific, locatable things the artist did.

Do not repeat the continental frameworks from Lesson 120 (Benjamin's *aura*, Heidegger's strange tools, Adorno's autonomy, Rancière's distribution of the sensible) — Lesson 158's Benjamin material below is a different essay ("The Author as Producer," 1934) addressing a different question (production and circulation, not aura and reproducibility) and should not be conflated with it. This command covers eight distinct questions you can ask of a single work — four about inheritance (Part 1), four about authorship (Part 2).

Run Part 1 when the live question is *"is this original / where does this come from?"* Run Part 2 when the live question is *"how much of this is really mine, given how much I depended on?"* Many real studio questions touch both — when they do, work through whichever framework brings the specific work into focus, across both parts, rather than mechanically running all eight.

## Part 1 — Originality and Lineage (Lesson 146)

Apply the four frameworks as distinct lenses. For each, keep the useful mechanism and discard the ideology Millsop flags:

1. **Kant — Genius and exemplary originality: "What does the work open?"**
   Original art is *rule-generating*, not *rule-following*. A work is original when no existing recipe, convention, or instruction could have prescribed it in advance; it is *exemplary* when it opens a possibility other artists can learn from without giving them a recipe to copy. The studio move: stop asking "am I original/talented enough?" (a verdict on the self) and ask **what possibility does this work make available that wasn't there before?** The possibility can be modest — one that opens only inside the artist's own practice. Keep Kant's "opening a possibility"; drop the isolated genius (via Nochlin: recognition depends on institutional conditions of access, not innate magic).

2. **Eliot — Tradition and the historical sense: "Where does it sit historically?"**
   Tradition is a living, active order, not a fixed list. A new work enters it *and changes how the whole order is read* — influence runs both ways. "Individual talent" is not the display of a unique personality; it is the **transformation visible in how the work selects, combines, and reworks what it inherits.** The studio move: name one specific thing the work inherits (a medium, an image, a process, a category), then name what the work *does* to that inheritance. Keep the aliveness and reciprocity; drop Eliot's single European canon — ask instead *who arranged the archive*, and whether the artist is entering a tradition, recovering one, or constructing one that was never recognized.

3. **Bloom — The anxiety of influence and the swerve: "What does it turn away from?"**
   A *precursor* is a powerful earlier artist whose achievement feels impossible to ignore and starts to occupy the imaginative space. Under that pressure, the work keeps falling back into the precursor's terms. The response is the **swerve** (creative misreading): reading the precursor as having gone the right way then taken a wrong turn, and making the turn it "should" have made. The swerve becomes visible in a concrete **formal decision** — scale, material, composition, procedure. The studio move: when influence is visible in the work, don't flee it — look at exactly *what changed*, because that change is a decision the artist made, and the decision is what makes it theirs. Keep Bloom's formal question; drop the father-son rivalry (via Muñoz's disidentification: you can work *on and against* an inherited form at once, staying inside it while changing whom it serves).

4. **Krauss — Originality and repetition: "What does it repeat?"**
   Originality and repetition are not opposites; repetition is often *what makes the experience of originality possible.* "Original" cannot mean "made without repetition" — the grid, the cast, the series, the reproductive process all repeat and remain originals. But repetition alone does not explain what makes a work *this artist's*: that is answered only by **what the artist did with what they repeated** (cropped it, rescaled it, painted over it, placed it beside something else, or — as with Sturtevant — chose to repeat it almost exactly, making the choice itself the imprint). The studio move: own the repetition openly, without pretending to have invented the form, then name precisely what repeating it *lets the work do.*

When the artist shares a work, series, or practice question, help them:
- Run all four questions, but let them **stay with the one or two that bring the work into focus** — Millsop is explicit that the artist may have much to say for one framework and nothing for another, and that is fine.
- Get **concrete about the decision**: originality lives in the specific choice (which possibility they plucked, where exactly they swerved, how closely they repeated, what they did to the inheritance). Push past "it's influenced by X" to "here is the exact formal turn I made from X."
- Treat inheritance and influence as **relational assets, not threats** — the anxiety is a headspace problem; naming the lineage is generative, and an exemplary work is one that opens possibility outward to others.
- Ask the **archive question** where relevant: who arranged the tradition being drawn from, and is the artist entering, recovering, or constructing it?
- Produce language usable in an **artist statement or a crit** — a sentence of the form "I began with this, and here are the choices I made from it."

Because this artist is multidisciplinary — oil painting, plotters and drawing machines that draw before the hand arrives, generative and shader code, open-source tools others build on, and NFT work — the "what makes it mine?" question is especially charged. Press on it directly:
- When a **machine or algorithm makes marks first** (plotters, generative systems), locate the authorship precisely: is the artist's hand in the *rule they wrote*, the *decision of where to intervene*, or the *swerve away from what the machine would do on its own*? A generative system is an inheritance and a precursor at once — name what it opens (Kant) and where the artist turns away from its defaults (Bloom).
- When the work **repeats a form the artist did not invent** (a shader technique, a grid, a coordinate system, an existing dataset or image), use Krauss: the repetition does not disqualify it — ask what the specific repetition *lets the work do*, and what the artist did to it.
- When the artist **builds open tools others use** (or mints editions), note that this is exemplarity in Kant's sense — the work gives others a possibility, not a recipe — and connect it to the companion question of authorship-in-circulation.
- Resist the reflex to prove the work "came from nowhere." The stronger, more honest position is the named inheritance plus the named decision.

## Part 2 — Authorship (Lesson 158)

Apply the four frameworks as distinct lenses. Each answers a different question about how authorship survives heavy dependence on other people, materials, positions, and systems:

5. **Foucault — The author as function: "What does the name do?"**
   Distinguish the *person* (an actual life, full of decisions and changes of mind) from the *author* (an entity built through the body of work, exhibitions, statements, and what others say about it). Attribution doesn't claim everything originated with the person — it does three things: connects the work to a body of work, guides interpretation (supplies the questions a viewer brings), and has real institutional consequences (what a museum or catalog treats as "belonging" to the practice). The studio move: name what a viewer assumes about a current work purely because of who made it — and decide whether to reinforce that expectation or deliberately interrupt it. Watch for the trap where a settled reputation ("known for work about grief") answers the question before new work has a chance to do anything new; note that exhibition text, reviews, and earlier work — not just a new artist statement — are what actually shift an audience's expectation.

6. **Haraway — Situated knowledge: "What does your position bring?"**
   Reject the "God trick" — the fantasy of a neutral, position-less view ("I just see things as they are"). All knowledge, including the artist's own, is situated: body, access, relationships, and experience shape what can even be noticed and made. The studio move: name what the artist's specific position lets them see that someone else would miss. Explicitly guard against the restrictive misreading that situated authorship means *all work must be autobiographical* — it doesn't. Working on unfamiliar territory, invented characters, or historical material is still situated (in the questions asked, the relationships built, the way of learning), and the work should not be treated as a transparent expression of the maker's identity. Situated knowledge applies on both the making side and the viewing side — a viewer's own position shapes what they can recognize too.

7. **Becker — Art as collective production: "What did others contribute, and what did you decide?"**
   Art is a collective activity even in total studio solitude — materials, technique lineages, and exhibition conventions all shape the work. An "art world" is the network of cooperating people (often far beyond who the artist personally knows) whose shared conventions let cooperation happen without re-explaining everything from scratch. The studio move: when a fabricator, printer, collaborator, or tool is involved, locate the artist's contribution precisely — which decisions were theirs, which belonged to the collaborator or convention, and does the current framing of "who made this" actually reflect that division? Keep the caution that **collective production does not mean equal authorship** — cooperation doesn't imply equal power or benefit, and describing everything as "collaboration" can hide real differences in contribution as easily as claiming sole credit can.

8. **Benjamin — The author as producer: "What does the arrangement make possible?"**
   Authorship can include *how* a work is made and how it reaches people, not only what it depicts or says. Track technique broadly — material choices, selecting/editing, presenting/circulating, and how the work is then encountered or used — and ask what each stage lets someone do. A work can politically oppose an institution in its content while supplying that institution with exactly the product it already knows how to sell (Benjamin's own example, *The World Is Beautiful*, makes a factory visually fascinating without revealing who controls it or under what conditions people work there). The studio move: ask how format, price, location, and access decide who can encounter the work and on what terms, and — the sharper version of the question — whether the work only invites a viewer's *response*, or actually extends their *capacity to produce* (sharing a method, a score, an open tool). Not every valuable work needs to do the latter; but naming which one a piece is doing is real authorial information most artists never examine.

When the artist shares a work, series, or practice question, help them:
- Run all four Part 2 questions, but let them **stay with the one or two that bring the work into focus** — as with Part 1, having nothing to say for a given framework is fine.
- Get **concrete about the arrangement**: authorship-as-producer lives in specific decisions (what the name is asked to carry, what the artist's position uniquely supplied, which decisions were the artist's inside a collective process, what the mode of production/circulation makes possible). Push past "other people helped" to "here is exactly what I decided versus what they decided."
- Treat dependence on collaborators, tools, and institutions as **locatable, not disqualifying** — Becker's whole point is that the artist's contribution is still real and nameable inside heavy cooperation.
- Ask the **circulation question** where relevant: where and how does this work reach people, and does that arrangement extend what anyone else can do, or only what they can look at?
- Produce language usable in an **artist statement or a crit** — a sentence of the form "here is what I decided, here is what the collaborator/tool/system contributed, and here is what the work's format makes possible."

Because this artist is multidisciplinary — oil painting, plotters and drawing machines, generative and shader code, open-source tools, astronomical/satellite instruments, and NFT/on-chain work — Part 2 is just as charged as Part 1:
- When a **machine, plotter, or algorithm makes marks or renders an image before the hand arrives** (Hybrids, Memories, Santos), use Becker to locate the division of labor precisely — which decisions are the artist's (the rule written, where to intervene, what to complete by hand) and which belong to the system — rather than treating the machine's contribution as either invisible or as erasing the artist's authorship.
- When the artist **builds and releases open tools** (Book of Shaders, PixelSpirit, LYGIA, Vera), apply Benjamin directly: this is exemplarity (Kant, Part 1) *and* an instance of extending others' capacity to produce (Benjamin, Part 2) at once — note both, since they answer related but distinct questions.
- When a work is **mediated by an instrument or dataset** (satellite feeds, astronomical libraries), use Haraway to ask what the artist's specific relationship to that instrument or data lets them notice that a purely technical user would not — without claiming the work is therefore autobiographical.
- When considering **how and where a work is shown or circulated** (gallery-only vs. open-source repository vs. minted edition vs. publication), use Benjamin's producer question to name what that specific arrangement makes possible for who encounters it.
