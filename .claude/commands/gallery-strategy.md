## Step 3 — Distinguish curator from gallerist/dealer

This changes everything about what to show, what to say, and what to ask for. Do not sort by job title — sort by function.

### Functioning mainly as a curator
- Organizes exhibitions around themes, questions, or research
- Works for museums, nonprofits, project spaces, biennials, Kunsthalls, or independently
- Writes or speaks about artists in contextual/historical terms
- Relationship to artists is often project-based, not ongoing
- Selecting work for a conversation — not building inventory
- Not primarily responsible for selling the work

**What they ask during a studio visit:** Does this work fit my exhibition idea or research question? Does this artist help me build a conversation? Is there a meaningful conceptual reason to include this practice now?

**What to show them:** Breadth of practice, the journey, the through-line of ideas and questions across bodies of work. Show older work alongside new if it illuminates the arc. Talk about process, influences, what you're exploring.

### Functioning mainly as a gallerist/dealer
- Runs or represents a gallery; maintains a roster
- Attends fairs, cultivates collectors, talks about editions and sales
- Relationship with artists is longer-term and business-oriented
- Helping create a market and place work — not framing it intellectually

**What they ask during a studio visit:** Can I sell or place this work? Is there consistency and growth that makes this worth a long-term investment? Is this artist professional, communicative, and reliable? Is there enough unsold work available?

**What to show them:** Recent, unsold work only. Strong edit. Don't show old favorites that aren't available. Don't show work that's already sold and waiting to ship — it's disappointing. Be professional, reliable, communicative in how you present yourself.

Note: many small galleries and project spaces blend both roles. The question is their center of gravity.

## Step 5 — Craft the introduction strategy

### In-person presence (the foundation)
Everything starts here. Before any email, Patricio should be a thoughtful and recurring presence: go to exhibitions, attend curator talks, follow up after open studio days, introduce himself at openings. Repeated encounters matter more than a single email.

**How to introduce yourself:**
- Be specific, not generic. Not "I'm an artist" — say the medium and one or two phrases that pique curiosity.
- Patricio's practice spans oil painting and generative/real-time software. Both are conversation-starters. Use whichever fits the context and the person.
- Limit to one to two things. People will only remember one to two things. Choose what you're promoting in this moment.
- Speak in one to three sentences, then stop. Don't monologue. Give phrases a curious person will respond to with "tell me more."
- Workshop these sentences. The only way to know what works is to test it on real people in real situations — like a stand-up comedian on the road.
- Examples to workshop:
  - "I make oil paintings and generative software that both explore the same questions about memory and landscape."
  - "I work with two mediums that seem opposite — oil on canvas and real-time software — but they're both trying to capture things that don't hold still."
  - "I make paintings and code them both — same questions about time and place, different surfaces."

**At openings:** Join a circle where you know at least one person. Let them introduce you. Don't monopolize anyone's time. Don't make it about you. Follow up when there's a real reason.

### Warm introduction by a third party (highest success rate)
When possible, ask a mutual connection to make the introduction by email. A strong warm intro:
- Has a point of reference ("great seeing you at X")
- Names the artist clearly and repeats the name
- Shows specific knowledge of the curator's work ("especially the [show] you did last year")
- Gives one concrete detail about the work ("abstracts in oil on cardboard")
- Includes one image
- Creates two off-ramps: the curator can ask for more, or can suggest meeting in person

### Building warmth over time (before the ask)
No ask should be the first contact. Build familiarity through:
- After a talk: "Hi [name], I really enjoyed the talk — especially [specific thing]. Do you have a reading recommendation on that?" (No ask. Just curiosity.)
- After social media engagement: "Thanks for the kind words on the work this week. I haven't posted this yet, but I thought you might enjoy seeing the next piece in the series." (No ask. Just sharing. Making them feel like an insider.)
- After showing up repeatedly: reference a prior meeting when you follow up.

## Step 6 — Write the outreach email

An outreach email has one goal: create enough interest that a next conversation becomes possible. Not to close the deal. Not to get the show. Just to get to the next conversation.

**Anatomy of a strong outreach email:**

1. **Subject line** — clear and specific; no mystery, no clickbait
2. **Opening** — one sentence showing why you're reaching out to *this specific person*. Reference a show they curated, an artist they represent, something they wrote. Show that you see them clearly.
3. **1–3 sentences about the practice** — concrete, specific, not jargon-filled. Pull from the sentences Patricio has workshopped. Sound like a person, not a press release.
4. **A gentle, appropriate ask** — matched to the warmth of the connection:
   - Cold/new: "Would it be okay if I sent a small portfolio PDF?"
   - Warm: "Would you be open to a studio visit when your schedule allows?"
   - Adjacent: "I'd be curious if you know any curators exploring [theme] whose work this might resonate with."
5. **Link to website or selected work**
6. **Short, professional close**

**Tone:** Professional, specific, warm but not overly familiar. Confident but not self-inflated. Not dripping in flattery. Not academic or jargon-heavy. Not needy.

**Emails that fail:**
- Contacting people with no research or alignment ("dear curator, I love your work")
- Form letters that could go to anyone
- Too much biography, too many attachments, no context for what's attached
- Over-explaining (signals insecurity in the work)
- Ask too big too soon (representation, solo show as first contact)
- Weak subject lines that require guessing

## Step 8 — Studio visit preparation

A studio visit is a conversation, not a test. The curator or gallerist is there to gather information and to root for you to be the solution to their problem. They came because they were interested — they are on your side.

### Before the visit
- Decide what work to show based on who is coming (see Step 3 — curator vs. gallerist)
- Remove work and clutter that would create distracting tangents or reveal unfinished, unsold work you don't want them to see
- Plan the arc: what you'll show first, second, third — and why that order
- Prepare sentences about: recent work, process, influences, current questions the work is exploring
- Anticipate their likely questions based on their curatorial interests and past shows

### Physical logistics (do not overlook)
- Have drinks available
- Have a place to sit — plan when you'll move from standing to seated
- If you have a printed portfolio, place it on the coffee table where they can pick it up organically — don't hand it over
- Account for neighborhood noise (school dismissal, street noise, nearby bars)
- Comfortable temperature
- Postcards, available works list, and a printed portfolio copy available but not forced

### Leave things undone on purpose
Save something for follow-up. Don't hand them everything on the way out. An unfinished work they noticed ("I'll send you an image when it's done") creates a natural, low-pressure reason to be back in touch.

### During the visit
- Make space for their looking — don't over-explain before they've had time to see
- Don't apologize for work
- Don't become defensive when questions arise — look for the kernel of truth
- Don't push for an immediate commitment — every meeting is different; let them process
- Pay as much attention to their reactions as to your prepared sentences; improvise in response

### Post-visit follow-up
- Same day or next day: brief, warm thank-you note; mention one or two specific things from the conversation
- A few days later: send any promised or requested documents or links
- Leave something to send later rather than sending everything at once
- The goal is multiple touch points, not one fire-hose delivery

## Step 9 — Follow-up strategy

**Three types of follow-up:**

1. **Administrative** — confirming details, sending what was requested, replying when invited. Always prompt.

2. **Relational** — thanking someone after a real exchange, acknowledging something meaningful from a conversation, sharing something clearly connected to prior discussions. This is what makes you memorable.

3. **Speculative** — gently reappearing after cold outreach with no response. Brief, respectful. Use sparingly. After two unanswered messages, give it a very long rest (months or more) unless you run into them in person.

**What good follow-up looks like:** it has a reason to exist. It adds context, information, or continuity. It does not ask whether they saw your last email. Examples:
- "Here's that finished piece I mentioned — thought you'd enjoy seeing how it resolved."
- "I came across this essay about [topic you discussed] and thought of our conversation."
- "I'm giving a talk next month in case you'd be interested — thought you might enjoy the poster I made."

**What bad follow-up looks like:** repeated nudges with no new context, sounding impatient or hurt, making the recipient manage your anxiety, escalating because of silence. Silence may mean no, not now, buried, or still deciding. Over-reading silence is a trap.

## Step 10 — Red flags and discernment

Not every opportunity is worth pursuing. Artists need discernment as much as ambition. You are evaluating them as much as they are evaluating you.

**Red flags:**
- Vague promises without specifics or timelines
- Pressure tactics of any kind
- Poor communication (slow or non-response to factual questions about timelines or logistics)
- Exploitative requests (high fees to participate, unclear consignment terms, no contract)
- Flattery that substitutes for commitment
- Patterns of disorganization or disrespect

**Gallery representation is not a lottery ticket.** Research what representation with this gallery actually means in practice: How much marketing do they do? How communicative are they during the exhibition process? Talk to other artists who've worked with them. The offer of a show is not the end of the process — it's the beginning. Make sure it's a fit you want.
