## The Three-Question Framework (Complete)

Every piece of art writing must address these questions in sequence. They form the scaffolding through which viewers access and understand your work.

### 1. What Is It? (Lesson 133)
*What does it look like? How is it made? What happened?*

This is the opening paragraph and the foundation for everything that follows. Run these checks:
- Is the first sentence the clearest, most concrete statement of what the viewer is looking at?
- Is every sentence specific to this artist and this work — non-copy-pasteable?
- Does it explain the "how" at grandmother level — accessible to an interested non-practitioner?
- Is "how" limited to roughly one-third of the paragraph? Young artists over-index on process; redirect toward what it looks like and what it does.
- Does it engage at least one non-visual sense (sound, touch, smell, time) if relevant?
- **Halfway test:** If someone reads only this paragraph and stops, do they walk away with useful clarity?

### 2. What Might It Mean? (Lesson 137)
*How does the form or event carry meaning? What is the thesis — the theory of the work?*

This is where young artists make images but mature artists make meaning. Your job is to scaffold the viewer's understanding, not to explain the work exhaustively.

**Key principle (Umberto Eco's openness spectrum):** An open work is precisely structured, not vague. Your statement provides scaffolding that lets viewers think more deeply on their own — not answers that close off interpretation.

**Guidelines:**
- Connect form, process, and concept. Show how the *what* becomes the *why*.
- Offer broad themes and ideas, grounded in concrete references (memory, place, history, personal experience).
- Don't state meaning so directly that there's nothing left for the viewer to discover. Outline contours; don't fill every detail.
- Write from wonder, not from certainty. The best art raises more questions than it answers.
- Ground ideas in the work itself. If you claim a theme, point (broadly) to evidence in the form.

**The scaffolding principle:** Your job is not to explain your work. Your job is to help a viewer go deeper with your work — often in less time than they would have on their own.

### 3. Why Does It Matter? (Lesson 137)
*So what? What does this contribute — to a conversation, a lineage, a culture, a human experience?*

- This doesn't have to be grandiose. Keep it reasonable and traceable to your answers to questions 1 and 2.
- Give readers a human reason to care, beyond technical achievement.
- Collectors invest in people and stories, not objects. This is where your personal stakes become clear.
- Connect the work to something larger: lineage, cultural moment, shared experience, the viewer's own life.
- **The essence test:** Can you name the one thing you want people to remember about the work?

## Art-World Clichés to Avoid (Lesson 137)

These phrases train readers to skip your text immediately. If you've heard it before, find a new way to say it.

**Tired verbs and structures:**
- "explores themes of"
- "investigates the relationship between X and Y"
- "interrogates notions of"
- "challenges traditional ideas of"
- "creates a dialogue between"
- "the viewer is invited to"
- "blurs the boundary between"
- "exists in a liminal space between"
- "speaks to broader questions of"

**Worn-out adjectives:**
- immersive, urgent, relational, mystical, nuanced, provocative, liminal, woke, dystopian

**Rule:** If you write a sentence and think "I've heard somebody say this before," cross it out and rewrite. Test it: could another artist use this exact sentence in their statement? If yes, make it more specific.

## How to Build or Revise a Statement

Work through these steps in sequence:

**1. Orient to the work.**
What is the work — medium, scale, materials, process? Has it been exhibited or shared? Does the artist already have a draft statement or no writing at all? Establish where we are starting from.

**2. If starting from scratch: gather material first.**
Use timed free-writing exercises (Lesson 137) to generate raw material before crafting sentences:
- **Sense description exercise:** Describe your artwork using only one sense (not the visual). This generates fresh language past clichés.
- **Eight meaning questions:** Free-write answers to: (1) What is this about to me? (2) What inspired it? (3) What are the broad themes? (4) What is it trying to express? (5) What questions is it trying to answer? (6) How does it dialogue with other works or the medium's history? (7) The one thing I want people to remember? (8) Its essence — the feeling or question that makes it special?
- **Letter to a trusted friend:** Write answering: Why is this work important to you? Is it a breakthrough? How does it push your arc forward? What was different about your approach this time? What makes it unique compared to other work in your medium?

After gathering material, **set it aside for one week.** Return, mark the strong bits, circle powerful phrases, box strong words. Copy those fragments into a new document, rearrange, write connective tissue.

**3. Audit or build the "What is it?" section first.**
This is the foundation. Run the checks listed above (concrete, specific, grandmother-level, process-one-third, etc.). A reader who understands what they're looking at can follow everything that comes next.

**4. Build or strengthen "What might it mean?"**
- Ground ideas in the work itself
- Avoid stating meaning too explicitly; leave room for viewer discovery
- Check: does a reader who didn't understand the work at first now have scaffolding to think more deeply about it?
- **Avoid:** broad claims without grounding, over-explaining every motif, assuming too much prior knowledge

**5. Develop "Why does it matter?"**
- Make it traceable to questions 1 and 2
- Include personal stakes if relevant
- Connect to something larger than the work itself
- Test: does this give readers a human reason to care?

**6. Check for failure modes.**
Flag and suggest rewrites for: art jargon, impersonal academic register, sentences that could belong to any artist, abstractions without concrete grounding, anything the artist would not actually say aloud, over-emphasis on process, grandiose language, comparison to famous artists or theorists.

**7. Identify the latch point.**
Find or create the single sentence that gives a viewer who doesn't "get" the work a foothold to enter it. This is the hospitality sentence — an open door, not a fence.

**8. Calibrate to audience.**
Who is this statement for: general public, curator, residency jury, collector, fellow practitioners, press? The register, length, and technical depth depend entirely on audience. Tune accordingly.

**9. Voice and rhythm check.**
Read the draft aloud. Do all sentences sound like something a real person would say in conversation? Does it sound like the artist wrote it, or like a press release?

## Key Principles from Lesson 137

**"An artist says a hard thing in a simple way."** (Charles Bukowski)
- Write to be understood, not to sound smart
- Depth is welcome; obscurity is not
- If you bore yourself, you bore your audience

**Scaffolding, not explanation**
- Your job is not to explain the work
- Your job is to give viewers enough structure to think deeply on their own
- Strong scaffolding increases viewing time, depth of engagement, and intellectual satisfaction

**Write from wonder, not answers**
- The best art raises more questions than it answers
- The artist statement names the questions you're exploring — it doesn't resolve them
- (Lucille Clifton: "I write out of what I wonder, not what I know")

**Broad themes, not granular motifs**
- Don't explain every symbol or justify every choice
- Give viewers a framework; they'll discover details on their own

## Self-Tests for the Final Version

1. **Read it aloud.** Do all sentences sound like something you'd say at dinner with a smart friend outside your field?
2. **Stranger test.** Could a first-time viewer picture the work after reading the opening paragraph?
3. **Copy-paste test.** Could any sentence appear unchanged in another artist's statement?
4. **Grandmother test.** Is technical explanation accessible to someone smart but outside the field?
5. **Halfway test.** If someone stops after the first paragraph, do they leave with useful clarity?
6. **Scaffolding test.** After reading the full statement, can a viewer who didn't understand the work before now think more deeply about it?
