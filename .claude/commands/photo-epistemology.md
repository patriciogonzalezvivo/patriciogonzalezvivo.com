**Step 1 — Locate the index, if there is one.** Talbot's insight, before the word "index" existed, was that a photograph is "drawn by light" — a direct causal trace of something that was there — and that it captures details the maker never intended. Ask of this work: is there a genuine causal trace back to a real thing in the world (a satellite's actual sensor reading, a real face, a real ecological event), or has that trace already been abstracted, averaged, filtered, or synthesized away before the artist ever touched it? Name exactly where, in the pipeline, the indexical link is strongest and where it breaks.

**Step 2 — Ask what perceptual threshold is being crossed.** Krasnopoler's second section shows photography extending sight into the too-small, too-far, too-fast, and invisible — and argues this doesn't just show more of the world, it changes what we imagine the world to be. If this work restores or reveals something beyond ordinary human perception (atmospheric scattering, starlight, a satellite's data, a plotter's read of a face), what new thing is it asking the viewer to imagine about the world — not just what is it showing them? Distinguish "extending vision" (Whipple, Draper, Paglen, Deschenes) from merely illustrating data.

**Step 3 — Test for the archive/grid/control mechanism (Sekula, Bertillon, Galton).** Sekula's claim is that the real artifact of a system of images is not the camera but the filing cabinet — the archive that turns individual images into comparative, judgeable knowledge. If this work involves a machine or algorithm that "sees" or sorts a face, a body, or a pattern before a human hand or eye completes it, ask: is that machine step a neutral trace (Talbot) or a sorting system already shaping what can be seen and how it will be judged (Bertillon's mugshot grid, Galton's composite average, or an ML dataset)? Consider whether the work, like Taryn Simon or Zanele Muholi, uses that same grid-aesthetic against itself to hand control back to the subject.

**Step 4 — Interrogate the documentary claim and its selection.** If the work claims to document, witness, or evidence something real (an ecological event, a lived condition, a family history), apply Rosler's critique of meliorism (does the work assume that merely showing a wrong fixes it, or does it trace the wrong to the system that produced it?) and Solomon-Godeau's "delusory presentness" (does the work's apparent transparency hide the selection, staging, or cropping that produced it, the way Lange's chosen negative or Evans's rearranged broom did?). Name the selection decisions the work is making and whether they are acknowledged or hidden.

**Step 5 — Confront the break in reference, if the image is generated or coded.** For any AI-generated, code-generated, or otherwise non-indexical image in the work (shader output, generative composition, synthetic imagery), apply Ritchen's warning that once photography's relationship to physical existence becomes tenuous, its whole vocabulary needs reconsidering. **Step 6 — Name the medium honestly.** Krasnopoler refuses to caption a generated image "photograph" because that word claims a specific, earned trust relationship to the world. Ask what word the artist is actually using for this work — photograph, drawing, generative image, instrument-image, composite — and whether that word claims more indexical trust than the piece has actually earned. If the work deliberately blurs this (machine-drawn skeleton plus hand-drawn completion; real satellite data plus invented atmospheric restoration), is that blur disclosed, or does the work let the ambiguity pass as unremarked truth-claim?

**Step 7 — State the stakes of the tension, don't resolve it.** Close by naming, in one or two sentences, where this work actually sits between index (a trustworthy trace of something real) and appearance (an image that looks convincing regardless of its origin) — and whether that tension is the work's subject (consistent with the "two incompatible systems on one surface" thread running through this practice) or an unexamined loose end that undermines the piece's claim to evidence, memory, or restoration.

## Staged Narrative Photography (Lesson 156, second session)

This lecture's throughline for this practice: Hybrids, Memories, and Santos are each a **tableau in Diderot's sense** — a machine-drawn skeleton and a hand-completed surface, both meant to relate legibly within one frame — and Weaver/Astros hold two knowledge systems in overt, undissolved tension the way Krasnopoler's staged photographers hold painting and photography, or documentary and fiction, in tension at once. Route here whenever the question is about deliberate composition or direction rather than evidentiary trust.

**Step 8 — Test for absorption vs. address.** Following Michael Fried (via Chardin): does the work behave as though it exists independent of a viewer — absorbed in its own internal logic, like a tableau that would be the same whether or not anyone were looking — or does it directly address the viewer, performing for the camera the way Alex Soth's or Deanna Lawson's confrontational portrait sitters do? Neither is better; name which the work is doing and whether that matches its intention. A machine-drawn skeleton, before the hand arrives, is often absorptive in exactly this sense — ask what changes once the hand's completion introduces (or withholds) address to the viewer.

**Step 9 — Locate the tableau's internal coherence.** Diderot's demand for tableau painting was total internal coherence — every figure, gesture, and object relating legibly to every other, readable without external explanation, the way a well-staged scene reads without a program note. Ask of the work: does every element (a machine-generated form, a hand-drawn addition, a restored atmospheric layer, a celestial coordinate) relate legibly to every other, or does the piece depend on an artist's statement to explain a relationship the image itself doesn't carry? This is a construction question, distinct from Step 1's truth-question.

**Step 10 — Name what tradition, outside the work's own medium, it reaches toward.** Krasnopoler's postmodern staged photographers deliberately reach *outside* photography's own history — into painting (Wall remaking Hokusai, Shonibare remaking Goya), altarpiece convention (Adi Nees and the *Sacra Conversazione*), or cinema (Crewdson, Prager) — rather than pursuing medium-specificity the way modernist photography (Weston, Bourke-White) did. Ask: is this work reaching toward a specific outside tradition (the history of astrophotography, portraiture, cartography, tarot, scientific illustration) the way Wall names Hokusai — and if so, is that reach named, or left implicit in a way a viewer has no way to recognize?

**Step 11 — Apply the Deanna Lawson test: let the ambiguity stand.** Krasnopoler's central caution is that "found portrait" and "fully authored tableau" are not opposites on a line but can operate in the same image *simultaneously* — Lawson's portraits are both a real subject's space and Lawson's staged intervention in it, and the tension is the content, not a flaw to resolve. Ask: does this work contain a comparable doubled claim (real satellite data *and* invented atmospheric restoration; a real face *and* a machine's prior, uncorrected read of it) — and if so, resist pushing the artist toward resolving which one is "really" true. Name the doubled claim explicitly instead, the way Lawson's work is best read by naming both chords at once.

**Step 12 — Bridge back to the index, when both tracks apply.** When a work raises both an evidentiary claim (Steps 1–7) and a staging/construction claim (Steps 8–11), close by naming how they interact: does the work's *staging* (Steps 8–11) disclose or obscure its *indexical status* (Steps 1–7)? Krasnopoler's own throughline across both lectures is that disclosure of process — whether Crewdson's blog, Jeff Wall's theoretical writing, or a work's own visible seams — is what lets a viewer experience the "vacillation of meaning" (learning a work is staged, and having that change everything) as an invitation rather than a betrayal. State plainly whether this work currently discloses its own construction, and if not, whether that's a deliberate choice serving the "two systems on one surface" thread or an unexamined gap.

Be specific throughout Steps 8–12: reference Krasnopoler's staged-narrative artists and terms by name (Fried's absorption, Diderot's tableau, Sherman, Wall, Crewdson, Lawson, Starkey, An-My Lê, Shonibare, Weems), and ground every observation in the actual work's construction — which elements are machine-generated, which are hand-completed, and which relationship between them the work is asking the viewer to read.
