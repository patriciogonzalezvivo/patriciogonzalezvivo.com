## Why artist statements matter

An artist statement opens doors. Curators, gallerists, residency juries, journalists, grant committees, and casual viewers all rely on an artist's text. Journalists working on deadline pull from your writing. Curators building exhibition pages lean on your words. Museum education departments train docents from your text. The people who will represent you and your work will use your words to do it — so getting them right matters.

A statement's purpose is not to explain everything the work already says visually. As Vicky Krohn-Ambrose wrote: *"You don't have to translate into words the entire content of what you've already expressed visually. Your task is to give your audience a compelling reason to look closer. Help them look."*

Writing an artist statement is opening a door rather than forcing viewers to climb in through the window.

---

## Know your audience before you write

Every piece of art writing is for a specific audience. Decide in advance:

- **General viewer** (gallery walk-in, website visitor, Instagram follower): write accessibly, tell a good story, avoid jargon entirely.
- **Curators / residency juries / grant committees**: slightly more context about concept and lineage is appropriate, but clarity still wins.
- **Fellow practitioners**: more technical depth is welcome, but this is rarely the right register for a public-facing statement.

A useful trick: write for one specific person in your life — someone smart but outside your medium. If you'd lose them at dinner, rewrite the sentence.

## The three core questions (Gilda Williams)

Every piece of art writing must answer three questions. Balance is important — don't let one crowd out the others.

### 1. What Is It?
*What does it look like? How is it made? What happened?*

This is the foundation. Give viewers something concrete to hold onto before anything else. If they can't picture the work, every abstraction that follows floats free.

**Guidance for this section:**
- Open with a clear, specific description. A viewer who has never seen the work should be able to picture it.
- Sequence sentences by essentialness: overall structure first, then secondary qualities, then details. Even a reader who stops halfway through the first paragraph should walk away with useful clarity.
- The *how* (process, materials) is only one-third of this question — resist the pull to let it dominate. Young artists especially over-weight process because it's what excites them most. Remember: practitioners (people who make art) are only about one-fifth of art audiences. Most viewers want to know what they're looking at, what it means, and why it matters — not the technical details.
- Always place the *why* before the *how*. Process should serve meaning, not replace it.
- Explain technical aspects as if talking to your grandmother: someone interested, smart, but without specialized knowledge. If you'd lose her, simplify it.
- Don't give away trade secrets in public-facing statements.
- The test: after reading the opening paragraph, can a first-time viewer picture the work? If no, this section needs more work.

**A strong "What Is It?" example:**
> *"These are historical found photographs from the mid-20th century collaged on repurposed scrap wood. The photographs were sourced from family estate sales on Orcas Island, off the coast of Seattle. The artist found the wooden planks and tree limbs to hold the photographs while walking the rocky beaches of the island each summer."*

That paragraph answers what it looks like, what it's made of, and how it came to be — without jargon, with a strong sense of place, and enough specificity to make the artist feel like a real person.

**A bad "What Is It?" example (what to avoid):**
> *"Always seeking to expand notions of what an image can be, [the artist] produces pictures that escape their indexical relationships by highlighting their formal properties and by opening the way for questions regarding their connection to specific cultural contexts."*

This piles abstractions on top of abstractions. After reading it, you cannot picture the work at all. It answers none of the three questions.

### 2. What Might It Mean?
*How does the form or event carry meaning? Where do the ideas live in the work itself?*

- Connect form, process, and concept. Show how the *what* becomes the *why*.
- Offer emotional and intellectual context without over-explaining.
- Ground ideas in concrete references: memory, place, history, personal experience.
- Don't state meaning so directly that there's nothing left for the viewer to discover. Outline the contours; don't fill in every detail.
- This is where a good statement becomes a dialogue with the viewer — your thoughts interacting with theirs.

As Edgar Degas said: *"Art is not what you see, but what you make others see."* The statement helps that happen.

### 3. Why Does It Matter?
*So what? What does this contribute — to a conversation, a lineage, a culture, a human experience?*

- This doesn't have to be grandiose. Keep it reasonable and traceable to your answers to the first two questions.
- Give the reader a human reason to care, beyond technical achievement.
- Collectors don't invest in art — they invest in people and stories. This section is where your story gives them something to believe in.
- Connect the work to something larger: lineage, cultural moment, shared experience, the viewer's own life.

As Pablo Neruda wrote: *"All paths lead to the same goal: to convey what we are."*

## Principles to check

- **Invite the viewer in** — does the opening welcome curiosity, or does it create distance? Saying "you tell me what it is" puts a fence around the work.
- **Write like you talk** — read it aloud. If a sentence sounds like something you'd never actually say, rewrite it. Saltz: *"Don't use art jargon, write in your own voice. Write how you talk. Don't try to write smart. Keep your statement direct, clear, to the point. If it's pretentious, don't say it."*
- **Be specific and concrete** — flag every sentence that is abstract to the point of vagueness. If you can't picture what the sentence is describing, neither can the reader.
- **Apply the copy-paste test** — if a sentence could appear unchanged in another artist's statement, it's not specific enough. Every sentence should be traceable to Patricio specifically.
- **Avoid jargon** — flag technical or academic terms a non-specialist would find opaque. "International art speak" sounds smart but pushes people away.
- **Don't over-explain process** — technical details should serve the ideas, not dominate them. The how is one-third of the first question, not the whole statement.
- **Make it personal** — include "I" where appropriate. A statement should sound like a specific person wrote it, not like a press release.
- **If you're boring yourself, you're boring your audience** — find what genuinely excites you about the work, then write from that.

## Red flags (signs of bad art writing)

Flag any of the following in the current statement:

- Abstract opening with no concrete image or description
- Sentences where no one can picture what is being described
- Academic jargon used to signal intelligence rather than communicate clearly
- Over-emphasis on technical process at the expense of meaning
- Sentences that could apply to any artist working in this medium
- Repetition that adds words without adding meaning
- An audience mismatch: written for curators/academics when it should speak to a curious general viewer
- A tone that feels distant, impersonal, or written to impress rather than to welcome

## Self-tests to apply to the revised version

1. **Read it aloud.** Do all the sentences sound like something a real person would say in conversation? Flag anything that doesn't.
2. **Stranger test.** Could a first-time viewer who has never heard of Patricio picture the work after reading the opening paragraph?
3. **Copy-paste test.** Could any sentence appear unchanged in another artist's statement? If so, make it more specific.
4. **Grandmother test.** Is the process explanation accessible to someone smart but outside the field?
5. **Halfway test.** If someone reads only the first paragraph and stops, do they walk away with useful clarity?
