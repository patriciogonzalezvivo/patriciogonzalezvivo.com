## Overview

This is the first of a two-part Business of Art lecture. Haiver opens by removing the shame around selling: selling art is what lets an artist keep making work, and a sustainable practice requires enough income to keep setting aside time. Artists should value their labor, ideas, experience, and time — not just their materials. The most easily forgotten cost is the unpaid hours of gathering ideas and building skill (the Picasso-napkin logic: "it has taken me 40 years to do that").

The lecture's central reframe: **price is not an objective measurement of artistic worth.** Failing to sell at a price doesn't mean the work lacks value; selling doesn't confirm the work is better than another. Collectors "often have bad taste" (they spend 99% of their time on some other career and art is a hobby); mediocre work can command high prices through reputation, scarcity, speculation, or a few key backers; and genuinely good work may have no established market yet. Therefore: **the goal of pricing is not to determine what the work is really worth — it is to establish a financial relationship with a collector or group of collectors that can support the practice.** Worth is decided elsewhere: by present enjoyment (the $20 street-fair print you love) and by posterity (curators centuries from now), both largely out of the artist's hands.

The practical spine of the lecture is that pricing balances **two categories** — what it costs to make the work (production costs, labor, studio overhead) and where the work sits in the market (exhibition/sales history, comparables, demand, collector purchasing capacity) — and that getting the second right requires humbling, shoe-leather market research. It then walks through the pricing calculator (and its limits), consistent price structures within a body of work and across mediums, the target-net formula once a gallery takes its cut, the evidence-based signals for raising prices, discounts and payment plans, inventory management and responsible-vs-artificial scarcity, the special danger of auctions and charity donations, and the closing reminder that a show does not have to sell out to succeed.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Pricing is a financial relationship, not a verdict on worth.** The goal is to build a supportable, ongoing exchange with collectors. Worth is set by present enjoyment and future posterity — neither of which the price captures or the artist controls.
- **Value your time, not just your materials.** Labor, ideas, experience, and the years spent building skill are real costs. The Picasso napkin ("40 years to do that in 30 seconds") and the Whistler v. Ruskin libel trial (paint "flung in the public's face"; Whistler won but received a single farthing) both dramatize that speed of making is not a measure of value — and that to someone who dislikes the work, *any* price is too high.
- **The Pixar approach.** You cannot please everyone; make what you genuinely like and assume there are others like you. Find the people who share your taste rather than pricing to convince skeptics.
- **Keep a half-time job even after success.** Structure makes the free hours more precious and more productively used; removing the pressure for *every* work to pay the bills protects the joy and experimentation that good art requires. "Remove the need for every artwork you create to support you financially."
- **The two-column balance.** Left column = what it costs to make (materials/fabrication/framing, labor, studio overhead — all trackable, often tax-deductible). Right column = where it sits in the market (exhibition/sales history, comparables at your scale and medium, current demand, collector purchasing capacity). The price is a combination of both.
- **Market research takes humility.** You must find your *true* peers — similar career stage, medium, scale/mode of production, geography, exhibition history, gallery representation, collector base. A famous artist in your medium is who you aspire to be, not a valid comparable. This is the "fundamental piece of pricing strategy."
- **Context matters; markets are regional first.** The same work commands different prices in different markets, but you price for the market you're actually selling into. Local factors: collector wealth, cost of living, gallery infrastructure, buying habits (wealth ≠ art-buying — e.g. SF tech money that never bought art), and audience reach (local/national/international).
- **One retail price everywhere.** The *same work* must carry the *same price* across studio, galleries, fairs, and online, regardless of continent. A collector must never discover it's $5,000 in one place and $3,000 in another — that feels like being ripped off. As your market grows to new locales, raise everywhere, not selectively.
- **Consistency is king.** Within a body of work, the relationship between sizes should feel coherent and predictable; two near-identical works should not carry wildly different prices just because one took longer. Confusion stops sales.
- **Responsible scarcity vs. artificial scarcity.** Deliberately pacing how much work enters the market (releasing 10–15 as a coherent body rather than dumping all 40) is responsible. Lying about near-sold-out status, faking competing demand, or changing an edition size after sales is artificial and dishonest. "The supply of your work should grow alongside the demand for it."

## The Pricing Calculator (and Why Not to Trust It)

- **The formula:** set a price per square inch, then multiply by the piece's area. Example: 24 × 36 in = 864 sq in; at $4.50/sq in ≈ $3,900.
- **What it's good for:** keeping similarly sized works priced consistently.
- **Why not to trust it:** it doesn't truly account for what the work costs to make (unless your per-square-inch rate was well calculated), and — more importantly — it says nothing about **whether the market can support the price.** Show up at a $500-and-under community fair with a $3,900 painting and you have to ask whether the collectors who'd spend $3,900 even attend that event, or whether it's people looking for a $200–300 piece "to hang above their toilet." **Context matters; don't price by cold numbers alone — do the research.**
- **Don't price bigger-is-more / smaller-is-less by dimension alone.** Doubling width and height quadruples the area but should *not* quadruple the price — there is a curve. (Example progression: 12×16 at $480, 24×36 at $1,200, 36×48 at $2,000; a more established artist in a richer market might run $1,500 / $3,500 / $5,500.)

## Building a Consistent Price Structure

- **Within a body of work:** choose a small set of sizes and keep the price relationships coherent and predictable. Vary the *quantity* by size (e.g. ten small, eight medium, three large) rather than pricing near-identical works differently. "A collector can't see" why one took longer.
- **Justified exceptions must be visible.** A sculpture may legitimately cost more (casting, fabrication, packing/shipping); a piece with gold leaf or precious materials can go up — but only if the difference is visible and there are enough comparable pieces that the logic reads as a structure, not a random one-off. A single unexplained outlier confuses collectors.
- **Calculating studio overhead (the third left-column cost):** rather than per-work guesses, take total annual overhead ÷ works produced/sold per year. Example: $12,000 ÷ 40 works = $300 overhead folded into each sale price. Overhead includes rent, utilities, insurance (space and work), equipment/software, storage (possibly climate-controlled), general supplies, and admin/website costs. Track it so you're not under-budgeting and eroding profit.
- **Don't let one medium undermine another.** In a multidisciplinary practice collectors compare prices across mediums; the differences must feel intentional and coherent as a group. Traps: a unique drawing priced *below* an edition print; a small sculpture at a third of a same-size painting; a $100 open edition that looks almost identical to a $1,000 limited edition; works on paper *always* cheaper "just because they're on paper" (which teaches collectors those works are less important). Don't build a rigid hierarchy — ask whether the relationships make sense when someone sees the whole practice together. The art world's default hierarchy (paintings > drawings > limited editions > open editions) is a tendency, not a rule: a *major* drawing can cost more than a *small* painting.

## Pricing Once You Have a Gallery — The Target-Net Formula

- **Splits:** most galleries take 50/50 (some 40/60). The commission covers their rent, staff, marketing, shipping, art-fair travel — real risk (most galleries don't survive past three years). A good gallerist is an expert salesperson watching the market daily, with connections and doors you don't have; that is what the percentage buys.
- **Work backwards from your target net** — how much *you* must retain from each sale. The gallery's cut and any collector discounts mean the list price is far above what you net.
- **The formula:** `Retail price = (desired net + production costs + allocated studio overhead) ÷ your percentage`.
  - Example at 50%: desired net $2,000 + production $400 + overhead $300 = $2,700 needed; $2,700 ÷ 0.50 = **$5,400 retail.** The gallery keeps $2,700; you keep $2,700; subtract your $700 costs → your $2,000 target. (At 60/40, divide by 0.60.)
- **When the number is too high:** the formula tells you what the work *would need* to cost, not what the market *will bear*. If it says $5,400 but comparables sell at $2,000–3,000, the formula has revealed a *structural problem* to solve — lower production costs, change scale, find other regional channels, work in higher-volume editions, or support the work by other means while you gradually build a market that can support the target.
- **Galleries don't cover your art-making materials, framing, studio overhead, or taxes** — they cover *their* space, staff, and overhead. Materials/fabrication set the *floor* of your cut, not the market price.

## When (and How) to Raise Prices — On Evidence, Not Time

Signals that justify a **gradual** increase:
1. A body of work is selling **repeatedly** (you make more, they sell again).
2. New works sell **soon after** release (fast turnaround).
3. You have **regular inquiries and a sustained waiting list.**
4. **Supply consistently fails to meet demand**, and/or a new body of work involves substantially **greater scale or production.**
5. Your **exhibition history or institutional recognition passes big milestones** (a group show at a major museum, a write-up in a major magazine).
6. You get **gallery representation** — but you should already have been raising gradually so you don't have to spike prices to offset the gallery's cut.

- **Review prices every 6–12 months** against metrics: % of available work sold, time from release to sale, which sizes/mediums move, waiting-list length, inquiries vs. purchases, repeat collectors, whether discounts are needed to close, regional demand differences, and production sustainability. Raise on evidence, not gut, and not "years put in." Growth is a marathon: gradual increases keep from pricing out existing collectors.

## Discounts, Payment Plans, Inventory, and the Secondary Market

- **Discounts.** Galleries commonly offer ~10% to favored collectors — *privately*, never crossed out on a public price list (once list prices seem always negotiable, they become meaningless; use discounts rarely). A discretionary discount's cost is usually split evenly between artist and gallery, but this is *not* universal — negotiate and get it in writing. Categories: private courtesy discount (loyal collector); museum/institutional discount (worth doing to close a placement that raises your profile — but still get paid; institutions have acquisition budgets, so don't just donate); volume discount (buy three, get a break — good salesmanship).
- **Payment plans and credit risk.** A payment plan is not a discount. Decide and document: does the collector receive the work before full payment; does the gallery pay the artist before collecting the full balance; are deposits refundable (and how much); who absorbs credit-card fees; what happens after a missed payment and who follows up; who bears the risk if a collector defaults. No standard exists — get it in writing.
- **Inventory management.** Reserve strong works for upcoming exhibitions (so critics/curators see your best, not private buyers); combine pieces into coherent bodies of work with a coherent pricing structure; hold some pieces back for museum acquisition; maintain an artist's archive of reference examples. Don't release everything at once — pace it out.
- **Auctions / secondary market — be especially careful.** Auction results are *public comparison points* with lasting effects. A low result sets a precedent that undercuts your primary-market prices and arms wary collectors ("it sold for that, why charge me more?"). An unexpectedly high result can prematurely pressure you to raise prices your handful of collectors can't sustain. Unsold/bought-in lots signal weak demand. Keep an open conversation with your gallery about when/where work has appeared at auction, and maintain a consistent, credible structure before consigning new work.
- **Charity auctions and gifts.** A heavily discounted charity sale can become a public "market value." Always set a sensible reserve; understand results may be public; be strategic about what you donate (avoid a standard edition of your primary work that could devalue the rest); offer alternatives instead — a special proof, a studio visit, an experience, or a custom piece made for the fundraiser.

## The Double-Edged Sword: Over- and Under-pricing

- **Overpricing** puts you in competition with far more established artists, keeps early collectors out, can fake demand, leaves work unsold for years, and makes later reductions awkward or reputationally damaging (if you drop a price, give earlier buyers a rebate or a discount on the next piece — "no one likes finding out they could have gotten it cheaper").
- **Underpricing** signals immaturity and low confidence, can make the practice financially unsustainable, obstructs future gallery representation (a large cheap-volume collector base is hard for a gallery to double), and breeds resentment and burnout when a sale barely covers production.

## Artists, Writers & Works Referenced

- **Pablo Picasso** — the apocryphal napkin story (from Mark McCormack's 1984 *What They Don't Teach You at Harvard Business School*): "$10,000 … it has taken me 40 years to do that in 30 seconds." Speed of making ≠ value.
- **James McNeill Whistler** — *Nocturne in Black and Gold* (shown 1877, Grosvenor Gallery, priced 200 guineas). Sued critic **John Ruskin** for libel over "flinging a pot of paint in the public's face"; won but received a single farthing and no costs. Recounted his side in *The Gentle Art of Making Enemies* (1890).
- **John Ruskin** — the critic; his own notes on **J.M.W. Turner** argued a 20-minute Turner beats a 10-year effort by an inexperienced artist — so his attack on Whistler was about *style and price*, not time.
- **Pixar / Toy Story** — the "make what you like and assume others like it" approach.
- **Avant Arte (London)** — cited for well-run time-limited editions with major artists (Ai Weiwei, the Mondrian estate, a Cindy Sherman color-film "outtake" from the *Untitled Film Stills* era); its big-name prices sit well above the lecture's hypothetical ranges.
- **Van Brunt Stillhouse (NYC whiskey), Twinkies, Coca-Cola** — supply-and-demand analogies: scarcity after closure raises price; abundance keeps price low.

## Core Takeaways

- **Price to build a supportable financial relationship, not to certify worth.** Selling failures aren't verdicts on the work; selling successes aren't proof of quality. Develop thick skin and separate your worth from the market's response.
- **Value your whole labor — then research your true market with humility.** Balance the two columns (costs vs. market position), find real peers (not aspirations), and price for the region you actually sell into. Do the shoe-leather research: read price lists, talk to gallery staff, artists, and collectors about what *actually* sold.
- **Consistency is king.** Coherent, predictable price relationships within a body of work and across mediums; one retail price everywhere for the same work; justified exceptions must be visible. Confusion kills sales.
- **Once a gallery takes its cut, work backwards from your target net** — `(net + production + overhead) ÷ your %`. If the number exceeds the market, that's a structural problem to solve, not a price to list.
- **Raise prices on evidence and gradually** — repeat sales, fast turnaround, waiting lists, scale increases, institutional milestones, representation — reviewed every 6–12 months against real metrics.
- **Manage availability honestly, and beware public comparison points.** Pace releases (responsible scarcity, never artificial), protect your best work for exhibitions and institutions, and handle auctions and charity donations carefully — their public results follow you. A show does not have to sell out to succeed: reviews, curatorial relationships, institutional interest, and future commissions are all real returns.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Across your mediums — oil paintings, plotter/drawing-machine works, shader captures, astronomical-instrument pieces, and on-chain works — do your prices *cohere* when a collector sees them side by side, or would a unique drawing accidentally undercut an edition print, or a work "on code/paper" read as less important than a canvas?
2. Who are your *true* peers right now — same career stage, medium, scale, geography, and collector base — and when did you last read an actual gallery price list or ask an artist what really sold, rather than benchmarking against the famous names you aspire to?
3. What is your genuine annual studio overhead (rent, utilities, insurance, storage, equipment/software, website/admin), and how much of it are you currently folding into each sale — or are you guessing and under-budgeting?
4. For your strongest current body of work, run the target-net formula at a 50% split: `(desired net + production + overhead) ÷ 0.50`. Does the resulting retail price match what comparables in your market actually sell for — and if not, is the fix scale, materials, channel, or editions?
5. Which pieces are you tempted to release all at once, and what would a *responsible* pacing look like — a coherent body of 10–15, some works held back for exhibitions and museum acquisition — without tipping into the artificial scarcity of implying more demand or less supply than truly exists?
