## Overview

This lecture introduces the Feldman Method of art criticism as the foundational framework for looking at and talking about art. Instructor Cheyenne Rudolph frames the session around reducing the anxiety of critique by replacing gut reactions and snap judgments with a disciplined, sequential process: describe, analyze, interpret, and evaluate. The core argument is that strong interpretation and evaluation can only stand on solid observational ground — you earn the right to meaning by first accounting for the facts.

The lecture unfolds through two live group exercises. The first uses Faith Ringgold's *American People Series No. 20: Die* (1967) as a demonstration of thorough description before interpretation. The second applies all four steps to Jack Whitten's *9-11-01* (2006), a large-scale mosaic incorporating ash and blood. Both works are chosen deliberately for their social and emotional weight, reinforcing the idea that formal rigor is not cold or detached but actually deepens empathic engagement. A short video clip of critic Jerry Saltz introduces the session with a democratic, anti-academic invitation to look and talk honestly.

The final section introduces four evaluative theories — formalism, imitationalism, expressivism/emotionalism, and instrumentalism — as lenses that reveal a viewer's own value system. Rudolph argues that knowing which theory you privilege is essential self-knowledge for any artist: when you evaluate a work, you are always speaking from a position, and identifying that position is the first step to honest criticism and self-awareness in your own practice.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **The Feldman Method** — A four-step process for art criticism developed by Edmund Feldman:
  1. **Describe** — State observable facts without judgment; what is literally present (materials, figures, colors, scale, location of elements)
  2. **Analyze** — Examine how the described elements are organized; relationships between formal elements (composition, contrast, emphasis, rhythm, balance)
  3. **Interpret** — Derive meaning from the relationships identified in description and analysis; supported by evidence; brings in cultural/historical context
  4. **Evaluate (Judge)** — Assess whether the work achieves its intention; best approached after the first three steps are complete

- **Elements of Art** — The basic ingredients: color, line, shape, texture, value, space, form, time

- **Principles of Design** — Arrangements of elements: contrast, emphasis, pattern/repetition, variety, proportion, rhythm, movement, balance, harmony, unity

- **Formalism** — Art is valued for mastery of formal elements and principles; content is secondary or irrelevant

- **Imitationalism (Realism)** — Art is valued when it accurately represents or mirrors reality; proportion and perspective matter

- **Expressivism / Emotionalism** — Art is valued when it evokes an emotional response in the viewer

- **Instrumentalism** — Art is valued when it communicates a social, political, or moral message

- **Jerry Saltz's heuristic** — "Ask yourself: what would I like about this if I were the kind of person who liked it?" — a tool for suspending defensiveness and approaching work with provisional openness

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Faith Ringgold** — *American People Series No. 20: Die* (1967) — Primary demonstration of thorough description before interpretation; a protest painting against racial violence modeled compositionally on Picasso's *Guernica*
- **Henry Ossawa Tanner** — *The Banjo Lesson* (1893) — Live group whiteboard exercise; discussed in the context of post-Civil War America and counter-narratives to minstrel stereotypes
- **Jack Whitten** — *9-11-01* (2006) — Second whiteboard exercise; large-scale mosaic made of acrylic, ash, blood, hair, and mixed media; used to practice analysis and interpretation with layered material and symbolic content
- **Pablo Picasso** — *Guernica* — Cited as the compositional and conceptual model for Ringgold's *Die*
- **Joseph Albers** — *Homage to the Square: Apparition* — Cited as an example of formalism
- **Jacob Lawrence** — *Confrontation at the Bridge* — Cited as an example of expressivism/emotionalism
- **Käthe Kollwitz** — *Woman with Dead Child* — Cited as an example of raw emotional expressivism
- **Nick Cave** — Sound Suits — Cited as instrumentalism; the suits conceal race, class, and gender to force a different kind of looking
- **Yoko Ono** — *Cut Piece* — Cited as instrumentalism; challenges gender stereotypes and violence against women

## Core Takeaways

- Start with description — it is the evidence phase. Every subsequent interpretive claim must be traceable back to something you observed and stated as fact.
- The difference between describe and analyze: describe names what is there; analyze examines how those things are arranged and related.
- Interpretation is where conceptual and historical context enters legitimately — but only after the formal foundation is laid.
- Evaluation is often already happening in us automatically; the Feldman process delays that instinct long enough to ground it in evidence.
- Knowing which evaluative theory you most identify with is crucial self-knowledge — it tells you where your judgments come from.
- What you notice first when looking at a work is information about yourself, not a flaw in your looking.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. When you present or talk about your own work, which step do you tend to skip? Do you jump to interpretation or evaluation before fully describing what is physically present?
2. Which evaluative theory — formalism, imitationalism, expressivism, instrumentalism — most reflects how you judge whether your own work is successful?
3. What do you tend to notice first when looking at work (your own or others')? What might that tell you about where your attention naturally lives?
4. If you applied Feldman's four steps to a piece of your own work as if you were a stranger to it, what would the description phase reveal that you have been taking for granted?
5. Are there works you love or hate that you have never fully described? What happens when you do?
