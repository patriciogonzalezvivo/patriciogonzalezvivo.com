**Instructor/Guest:** Dr. Polina Rud (PhD art history; MA international relations; curator, Moscow Museum of Modern Art & Polytechnic Museum; author, *Cosmic Game of Snakes and Arrows*; co-founder, The World Hurts NGO)
**Topic:** An art-history lecture on how artists have represented society across time — three recurring strategies (**flattery**, **diagnosis**, **eliciting/staging**) traced through portraiture, propaganda, satire, and participatory art from ancient Egypt to AI image classification

---

## Overview

Rud opens not with chronology but with a question every artist eventually faces: *what is my position in all of this, and should it appear in my work?* She frames these as questions about people rather than politics — how we see another human being, how we relate to society, what kind of world and what kind of person we are becoming. From that starting point she lays out three artistic strategies for responding to society, insisting these are **recurring** (not progressive) approaches that resurface across eras. Rather than a timeline, she builds a deliberate "time-lapse" that lets each strategy reappear in wildly different contexts to expose its underlying logic.

The three strategies structure the lecture. **Flattery** creates the image a society wants to believe about itself — improving reality on the surface of an idea (power, virtue, belonging), which hardens over generations into cultural code and norm. **Diagnosis** reveals what society prefers not to see — its symptoms, then its hidden structures, then how those structures become internalized inside the individual. **Eliciting** (or staging) stops representing society altogether and instead builds situations in which society begins to reveal itself through participants' own actions. Each block closes with the same move: the diagnosis turns inward, becoming a form of self-recognition — *what kind of person has this world made me?*

Throughout, Rud pairs artworks with social theory (Cassirer, Kantorowicz, Bourdieu, Anderson, Cooley, Foucault, Althusser, Dewey, Bourriaud, Byung-Chul Han), showing that portraiture and social imagery are never neutral records but instruments that produce belief, legitimacy, and identity. Her recurring warning is that all three strategies persist today in more hidden form: flattery is now personalized by algorithms, diagnosis is harder because power hides in code and data, and genuine eliciting — real people meeting, disagreeing, acting together — has become one of the rarest things culture can offer.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Three recurring strategies (not a chronology)** — *Flattery* (create the image society wants to believe), *Diagnosis* (expose symptoms, structures, and internalized systems), *Eliciting/Staging* (create situations in which society reveals itself). They recur across all eras; the lecture is a time-lapse, not a timeline.
- **Flattery as cultural code** — Not "making beautiful" but improving reality on the surface of an idea (power, virtue, belonging, the ideal body, the ideal society). Repeated across generations, these images stop feeling like choices and become the way a society understands itself — a norm.
- **We live by symbols (Cassirer)** — We don't live by facts; at moments of crisis we reach for something larger — a story, a symbol, a leader — that makes the world feel stable. This is why flattery works: it gives us the reality we hope to find.
- **The king's two bodies (Kantorowicz)** — The ruler has a mortal body and a political body representing the continuity of the state. Portraiture gives visible form to the second: the aging ruler stays young, the uncertain ruler becomes decisive, fragile order appears stable.
- **The ideal is what a society values, not what is beautiful** — Republican Rome portrayed wrinkles and sagging skin as wisdom and moral authority (verism); Augustus reversed this with an eternally youthful, calm image to sell the never-before-seen idea of empire. The ideal changes; the mechanism does not.
- **Flattering the whole order, not one person** — Casta paintings and Socialist Realism flatter an entire system: colonial hierarchy shown as harmonious and scientifically organized; Soviet life shown "in its revolutionary development" (as it was *supposed to become*), erasing purged officials from paintings.
- **Constructed hierarchy becomes common sense (Bourdieu)** — The made-up order gradually reads as natural; taste is not innocent but a mechanism that produces and displays social identity. *Habitus*: dispositions so embodied they feel natural rather than historically produced.
- **Nations held together by shared images (Anderson)** — Jan Matejko gave Poland a visual memory when it had no state; Mexican muralism (via Vasconcelos's *Cosmic Race*) reinvented national identity around workers, peasants, and indigenous communities.
- **The looking-glass self (Cooley)** — We understand ourselves through how we imagine others see us. Morimura, Martin Parr, and Amalia Ulman extend this into personal and class flattery, and into social media's self-curation.
- **Byung-Chul Han's "you can" → "you must"** — Power no longer commands from outside; it is internalized as self-regulation. Success, productivity, attractiveness feel like personal choices but are absorbed social expectations. AI now flatters our *abilities*, not just our image.
- **Parrhesia** — Ancient Greek "speaking everything": the courage to tell uncomfortable truths to power, even at personal cost. The root gesture of the diagnostic strategy.
- **Images as symptoms (Warburg)** — Art preserves emotional tensions across generations (*Pathosformel* — pathos formulas), carrying fears, desires, and conflicts a society never resolved.
- **From symptom → structure → self** — Artists diagnose visible symptoms (habits, follies, masks), then uncover hidden structures (Durkheim: personal crises have social origins; Weber: institutions shape behavior; Foucault: power operates through ordinary rules), then show how those structures become part of who we are (Althusser: ideology interpellates us as subjects).
- **Relational aesthetics / social laboratory (Dewey, Bourriaud)** — Meaning emerges through interaction between people and environment. Contemporary artists produce situations, not self-contained objects; conversations, decisions, and conflicts *are* the artwork.
- **The presence of absence** — In *El Siluetazo*, traced body outlines make the disappeared visible without recreating them; absence itself becomes the subject, and private grief becomes collective action.

## The Three Strategies — Chronological Arc of Works

**Flattery (creating the image society believes):**
- Ancient Egypt — pharaohs shown timeless, youthful, divine, and *male*; **Queen Hatshepsut** gradually acquires broad shoulders, muscular torso, and a ceremonial beard — political image over biological body.
- Rome — Republican verism (age as authority) → **Augustus**, thousands of identical calm, strong statues promising the *Pax Romana*.
- Court portraiture — **Holbein**'s Henry VIII (aging, unhealthy king made monumental); **Elizabeth I**'s "Mask of Youth" (state-controlled image of the unmarried, forceful queen).
- **Jacques-Louis David** — *Napoleon Crossing the Alps* (rearing horse, flying cloak — he actually rode a mule; contrast **Paul Delaroche**'s harsh version); *The Coronation of Napoleon* (paints the political reality Napoleon needed — hostile mother and sisters made devoted attendants).
- **Casta paintings** (18th-c. colonial Mexico/Peru) — classify racial combinations as elegant, prosperous harmony, hiding forced labor and violence.
- **Socialist Realism** (1932, Stalin) — optimism as mandate; purged figures repainted into trees and empty spaces (see David King, *The Commissar Vanishes*). **Norman Rockwell**'s *Four Freedoms* — the flattering image of what America wanted to believe about itself.
- Nation-building — **Jan Matejko** (Polish history); **Diego Rivera**'s Emiliano Zapata (a new model of Mexican identity, dignity once reserved for kings).
- Self-flattery — **Yasumasa Morimura** (inserting himself into Velázquez, Rembrandt, Frida Kahlo); **Martin Parr** (consumer choices as symbolic self-portraits); **Amalia Ulman**'s *Excellences and Perfections* (a four-month fabricated Instagram influencer life that nearly everyone believed).

**Diagnosis (exposing what society avoids):**
- **Pieter Bruegel** — *Netherlandish Proverbs*, *The Fight Between Carnival and Lent*: society as protagonist, proverbs as the "memes" of their time; communities imprisoned by their own habits.
- **William Hogarth** — series as visual novels: *Gin Lane / Beer Street* (vice as social symptom, not personal failure); *Marriage à la Mode* (people revealed unconsciously through objects, spaces, the dog that finds the infidelity first).
- **James Ensor** — *Christ's Entry into Brussels*: masks let people become more honest about cruelty and vanity; laughter as social weapon; Christ lost in the spectacle.
- **José Guadalupe Posada** — *La Calavera Catrina* (1910): the elegant skeleton in a European hat mocking indigenous elites who rejected their own culture; death as the great equalizer.
- **Cildo Meireles** — *Zero Cruzeiro* (banknotes valued at zero, printed with an indigenous man and a psychiatric patient — those the nation preferred not to see); *Insertions into Ideological Circuits* (Coca-Cola bottles carrying critical messages back into circulation, versus Warhol's bottle as consumer symbol).
- **Mierle Laderman Ukeles** — *Manifesto for Maintenance Art*, *Touch Sanitation* (shaking hands with 8,000+ NYC sanitation workers): why does society hide the labor it depends on?
- **Kader Attia** — *The Repair from Occident to Extra-Occidental Cultures*: WWI "broken faces" beside visibly repaired African objects — Western repair hides the wound; other traditions keep the scar as history.
- **Jo Spence** — breast cancer (1982) work: entering the medical system, she ceases to be a person and becomes "the patient"; where does knowledge end and authority begin?
- **Tania Bruguera** — *Tatlin's Whisper #6*: a free-speech podium in Cuba where learned self-censorship keeps people silent; dictatorship maintained through internalized behavior.
- **Trevor Paglen & Kate Crawford** — *ImageNet Roulette*: machine vision reduces individuals to racist, classist stereotype labels; AI as another apparatus of power deciding who we are allowed to be.

**Eliciting / Staging (situations where society reveals itself):**
- **Rirkrit Tiravanija** — sharing meals as social technology; during 2010 Thai unrest, red/yellow/green curries let political difference be *lived with* rather than explained.
- **Thomas Hirschhorn** — the *Bataille Monument* (Documenta 11) in a Turkish immigrant housing estate, forcing art-world and marginalized publics to collide (Georges Bataille's non-productive "expenditure").
- **Rimini Protokoll** — *100% City*: 100 residents matching city statistics, each representing 1%, moving across a stage by agreement/disagreement — statistics reborn as living bodies.
- **Santiago Sierra** — pays people to perform degrading tasks (sitting in cardboard boxes); payment (not volunteering) exposes the exploitation already invisible all around us.
- **El Siluetazo** (Argentina, 1983) — Aguerreberry, Flores & Kexel with the Mothers of Plaza de Mayo: traced silhouettes of the *desaparecidos* pasted across Buenos Aires; the presence of absence; private grief becomes collective action.
- **Jeremy Deller** — *The Battle of Orgreave*: 800+ participants (former miners, police veterans, reenactors) restage the 1984 miners'-strike clash — "digging up a corpse and giving it a proper post-mortem."

## Core Takeaways

- **Portraiture and social imagery are never neutral records.** They are instruments that produce belief, legitimacy, and identity — deciding what deserves admiration, what stays invisible, and what becomes "normal."
- **The three strategies are tools, not moral positions.** Flattery can legitimize oppression *and* build dignity, hope, and national identity; the same mechanism serves either end. What matters is knowing which one you are using and why.
- **The ideal image encodes what a society values, not what is beautiful** — wrinkles as wisdom in Rome, eternal youth for Augustus, indigenous dignity for Rivera. Read any portrait by asking what value it is asking you to admire.
- **Diagnosis moves from symptom to structure to self.** The strongest critical works do not stop at exposing a fault; they trace it to a hidden system and then to how that system lives inside the individual — ending in self-recognition, not accusation.
- **Eliciting makes society the medium.** When the artwork becomes a situation, designing that situation — who meets whom, under what friction — becomes the core artistic act.
- **All three strategies have gone hidden today.** Flattery is personalized by algorithms; power hides in code and data rather than visible institutions; and genuine encounter between disagreeing people has become rare and valuable. The updated question: *what does art make possible in this context?*

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. When your work portrays a person, a group, or a "we," which strategy are you using — flattery (giving them the image they want to believe), diagnosis (exposing what they avoid), or eliciting (staging a situation where they reveal themselves)? Is that choice deliberate?
2. What value is your portrait asking the viewer to admire, and whose ideal is it? Could the same face be read as dignity by one community and as erasure by another?
3. Does your work expose a system of power, or does it unintentionally reproduce one? What invisible structure — economic, technological, institutional — conditions how and whom you depict?
4. When you restore or "repair" a face, an ancestor, or a memory, are you hiding the wound (the Western repair) or keeping the scar visible as part of its history (Attia's model)? Which does your subject deserve?
5. If your work were a *situation* rather than an object, what kind of encounter would it make possible — and who, in your community or family history, would be brought into the same room?
