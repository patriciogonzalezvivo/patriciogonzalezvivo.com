# 113 · How to Build a Portfolio

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Haiver (program mentor/instructor)
**Topic:** Portfolio construction, curation, sequencing, and format for artists

---

## Overview

This lecture reframes the portfolio not as an archive or greatest-hits list but as a designed viewing experience — an edited selection of best work built for a specific viewer and a specific purpose. The core argument is that a portfolio is a tool of persuasion, not preservation: its job is to help a particular audience (an admissions committee, residency jurors, gallery directors, collectors, grant reviewers) make a decision in the artist's favor within five minutes of looking. This requires understanding the audience's needs and tailoring selection and sequencing accordingly — the underlying works may remain stable, but the arrangement shifts with each application context.

The lecture covers the full process: gathering more work than needed, editing by cutting the weakest 25% and removing anything that no longer represents the artist's current thinking, and sequencing like a curator organizing an exhibition with a beginning, middle, and end. Haiver draws on a personal story from the 2022 Sundance Film Festival — watching every film and disagreeing with all the jury awards — to argue that rejection is often about jury taste and context, not work quality, and that artists should feel free to send work out without treating rejection as a verdict on their practice.

The lecture closes with practical guidance on PDF and website formats, file naming conventions, captioning standards, documentation photography, and portfolio software tools.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Portfolio as Persuasion** — Distinguishes portfolio (persuade) from archive (preserve); the portfolio answers "Who is this artist today?" for a specific audience.
- **Five-Minute Decision Rule** — Key question: "What decision do I want them to make after five minutes with my work?" All curation decisions flow from this.
- **Gathering, Editing, Sequencing** — Three-phase process: gather double what you need, cut the weakest 25%, then sequence for maximum clarity and emotional rhythm.
- **Grouping vs. Weaving** — Two structural approaches: group by project/year (clarity, recommended for early-career artists) or weave between images and time periods (depth and integration, requires a large mature body of work).
- **Six Sequencing Methods** — Chronological; Inquiry and Expansion; Thematic/Conceptual; Material Connections; Narrative Frame (four acts); Anchors and Satellites.
- **Replace, Don't Add** — When updating the portfolio, replace images with stronger ones rather than expanding the total count beyond 10–20.
- **Exhibition Pacing** — Start strong, vary rhythm (dense/simple, dark/light, large/small), avoid redundancy, end with a significant closing work.
- **PDF + Website as Complementary Tools** — PDFs control sequence for applications; websites build credibility and allow discovery.
- **Documentation Standards** — Sharp, evenly lit, color-accurate, undistorted images; parallel camera for 2D work.
- **Caption Format** — Title, year, medium (specific, not "mixed media"), dimensions (height × width × depth).

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Robert Capa** — Famous blurry black-and-white D-Day Normandy landing photographs — cited as historical context for ICP (International Center of Photography), where the instructor submitted his first portfolio.
- **Kevin McCoy** — Visiting artist in the program — cited for noting artists typically have only one or two core ideas across a 30-year career, reinforcing the importance of identifying core practice principles.

## Core Takeaways

- A portfolio communicates direction and capability, not everything the artist has ever done; recency signals active engagement with current practice.
- The most common portfolio failure is not weak work — it is incoherence: a reviewer cannot make sense of who you are as an artist.
- Early-career artists should favor grouping by project; weaving between time periods requires a mature, cohesive body of work.
- Editing is a curatorial act: removing a weaker piece strengthens the remaining work's overall reading.
- Two mediums belong in the same portfolio only when they feel like different manifestations of the same inquiry.
- Strong documentation is non-negotiable: poor photography can sabotage otherwise excellent work.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. What decision do you want a viewer to make after five minutes with your current portfolio?
2. What are the two or three recurring questions, materials, or emotional tones that thread through your bodies of work?
3. If you grouped your recent work into two to three bodies, what would each grouping be called, and what is the connection between them?
4. Which pieces in your current selection are you hesitant to show? What does that hesitation tell you?
5. Does your strongest work open your portfolio, or are you warming up slowly? What would happen if you led with it?
