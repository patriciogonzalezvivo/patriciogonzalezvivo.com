# 116 · Art History Lecture — Architectures of Interactivity: Movement, Systems, and the Black Box

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Dr. Polina Rud (art historian and lecturer)
**Topic:** History of interactive and participatory art from kinetic sculpture through cybernetics; the philosophical and political stakes of viewer activation

---

## Overview

This art history lecture traces the shift in Western art from static object to dynamic field — from art that is looked at to art that is activated through the body, movement, and eventually feedback systems. Dr. Rud organizes the lecture into two major conceptual arcs: first, the emergence of viewer activation from the late 19th century through kinetic and Op Art, and second, the transformation of participation through cybernetics, feedback loops, and what she calls the "black box" — the opacity of increasingly complex interactive systems.

The first arc begins with the philosophical premise that by the end of the 19th century, matter itself was understood as process rather than mass. This sets up a trajectory through Duchamp's viewer-triggered readymades, Leon Theremin's body-as-instrument interface, Constructivist claims about art as a field of forces, Kinetic art's equation of sculpture with movement, Op Art's engagement of the retina and brain as active completers of the work, and the participatory politics of Lygia Clark and Hélio Oiticica in Brazil.

The second arc examines cybernetics as a transformation of interactivity from visible movement into invisible processing. The artwork no longer simply reacts — it adapts. The lecture identifies three core conflicts within cybernetic interactivity: (1) Communication vs. Control — who governs the feedback loop?; (2) Participation vs. Constraints — what range of action is actually available within the system?; (3) Transparency vs. the Black Box — does the participant understand what is happening? The lecture closes with the argument that cybernetic artworks appear to grant freedom while actually designing behavior within pre-coded limits.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **From Object to Field** — Art's historical shift from a static thing to be viewed at a distance toward a dynamic environment that unfolds through presence, movement, and time.
- **Activation** — The replacement of passive spectatorship with embodied participation; interactivity as a democratic gesture.
- **Kinetic Art** — Art in which movement is structural rather than representational; sculpture that *is* movement.
- **Op Art / Perceptual Activation** — The retina and brain complete the work; interactivity occurs at the level of perception.
- **Relational Aesthetics** — Nicolas Bourriaud's framework: art as a field of encounter, a space produced together by artist and viewer.
- **Phenomenology of Perception** — Merleau-Ponty's claim that "I am not in front of my body, I am in it or rather I am it"; foundational to participatory art's turn toward lived experience.
- **Cybernetics** — The science of feedback, control, and communication in systems; transforms interactivity from visible trigger into invisible loop.
- **Feedback Loop** — The system's continuous self-correction in response to changing conditions; the viewer's actions become data that the system recalibrates against.
- **The Black Box** — A system in which only inputs and outputs are visible; internal processing is opaque.
- **Three Conflicts of Cybernetic Interactivity:**
  - Conflict 1: Communication vs. Control — Who controls the feedback loop?
  - Conflict 2: Participation vs. Constraints — What range of action is actually available?
  - Conflict 3: Transparency vs. the Black Box — Does the participant understand what is happening?
- **Pre-coded Freedom** — Cybernetic artworks grant activity while defining its limits; freedom exists within rules; interactivity becomes the design of behavior.

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Marcel Duchamp** — *Bicycle Wheel* (1913) and *Roto-Reliefs* (1935) — the viewer triggers the object
- **Leon Theremin** — Theremin instrument (1920) — the body enters an invisible field; instrument played without contact
- **László Moholy-Nagy** — *Light-Space Modulator* — artwork as field of forces; spectator as active factor
- **Jean Tinguely** — *Homage to New York* (1960) — the viewer must adapt to the machine's behavior
- **Lygia Clark** — *Bichos* and *The House Is the Body* — embodied participation; phenomenological intensity
- **Hélio Oiticica** — *Parangolés* — the viewer becomes implicated in the culture of the favela
- **Gordon Pask** — *Music Colour* and *Colloquy of Mobiles* — cybernetic communication and feedback systems
- **Robert Rauschenberg** — *Open Score* (E.A.T., 9 Evenings: Theatre and Engineering)
- **Edward Ihnatowicz** — *SAM (Sound Activated Mobile)* and *Senster* — responsive cybernetic sculpture
- **Myron Krueger** — *Psychic Space* (1971) and *Video Place* (1975) — programmed agency confused with freedom
- **David Rokeby** — *Very Nervous System* (1986–90) — interactive sound system; body movement as input
- **Maurice Merleau-Ponty** — *Phenomenology of Perception* — foundational to embodied participation
- **Nicolas Bourriaud** — *Relational Aesthetics* — art as field of encounter produced together

## Core Takeaways

- Art's 20th-century history is a history of progressively dissolving the boundary between artwork and viewer — from object to field, from spectator to participant, from participant to component within a feedback loop.
- Interactivity began as a democratic gesture but cybernetics complicated that claim: participation can be designed, and designed behavior is a form of control.
- The three conflicts — Communication vs. Control, Participation vs. Constraints, Transparency vs. the Black Box — are live design questions for any interactive or systems-based artwork made today.
- As systems grow more complex, the artist's ethical responsibility toward the viewer intensifies: invisible processing is still processing, and its effects on behavior are real.
- Form communicates before concept does — asking what a work would say if stripped of all explanatory text is a fundamental diagnostic for any practice.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. When your artwork involves viewer interaction, who actually controls what happens? Is that visible to the viewer or hidden inside a black box?
2. What range of responses does your interactive work actually permit? Are those constraints intentional?
3. If your work were stripped of all explanatory text — title, statement, caption — what would the form alone communicate? Can you name it in one word?
4. Does your work ask something of the viewer? What would a questionnaire embedded in your practice look like?
5. Where in your practice does participation risk becoming compliance — the viewer adapting to the work's behavior rather than genuinely acting within it?
