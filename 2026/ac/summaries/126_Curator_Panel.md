# 126 · Panel with Curators Adam Heft Berninger and Brian Beccafico

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Adam Heft Berninger (Founder, Heft Gallery) and Brian Beccafico (Art Advisor, Independent Curator; formerly Sotheby's Paris)
**Topic:** The gallery and curator relationship — how curators work, find artists, evaluate portfolios, structure exhibitions, and sustain long-term partnerships

---

## Overview

This panel pulls back the curtain on what curators and gallerists actually do — a day-to-day reality far removed from hanging art on walls. Adam Berninger, who came to gallery ownership through a 12-year career running a digital creative agency, describes his role as relational: developing artists' work, managing collectors, logistics, fabrication, a print shop, and constant communication. Brian Beccafico, who organized hundreds of open calls at Art Crush and served as the youngest director in Sotheby's history, frames his work around three pivots: date, location, and artist — any one of which can be the starting point for an exhibition, as long as all three eventually converge.

Both curators emphasize that artist discovery rarely comes through cold submissions. The most reliable route is proximity and recommendation — being visible at openings, earning trust so that the artists a gallery already represents are motivated to introduce you. Brian notes that galleries have shrunk their rosters from roughly 25 to about 12 artists over the past five years, making every submission slot more competitive. The warmest path in is a personal introduction from an artist the gallery already respects.

The conversation turns to the "post-show slump" — the quiet period after a solo exhibition when an artist feels the gallery's attention has moved on. Both panelists acknowledge that curators go through their own version of grief when a show closes. Their advice: don't wait for the slump — dive into the next body of work before the show ends, actively propose specific outreach strategies to your gallery partner, and channel energy into applications for residencies and grants.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Three pillars of exhibition planning (Beccafico):** Date (cultural event or calendar anchor), Location (gallery's geographic and thematic identity), and Artist (who fits both). Every show starts from one of these and must eventually satisfy all three.
- **Proximity and visibility:** Showing up at gallery openings and being seen in the spaces you want to enter is often more effective than cold submissions.
- **Formalizing your practice:** A portfolio is not a website. A PDF is a standardized document that signals you can present your practice with structure and discipline.
- **Articulation of work:** Artists need to be able to communicate their work — through writing, conversation, or with a partner's help. Galleries depend on language to place artists in press, grant applications, and collector conversations.
- **First right of refusal:** A practical middle-ground for gallery partnerships — the gallery gets first access to new work, especially work that resembles what they helped develop.
- **The Pareto dynamic:** In most galleries, 2–3 artists generate 80% of revenue. This structural reality shapes how galleries invest attention and resources.
- **Soccer club model (Beccafico):** A proposed reform where galleries that helped build an artist's career receive compensation when the artist moves to a larger gallery.

## Core Takeaways

- Cold portfolio submissions are rarely effective; warm introductions from trusted artists in a gallery's circle have close to a 100% open rate.
- Recommending other artists to a gallery reflects on your own judgment and taste — do it carefully and it signals your merit.
- A portfolio (PDF) is still the industry standard; a website is an archive, not a portfolio.
- Gallery partnerships are relationships, not contracts — they require consistent communication, trust, and shared investment.
- After a solo show closes, the most productive response is to immediately start the next body of work and bring specific, actionable outreach proposals to your gallery partner.
- The gallery split (50–60%) reflects the full scope of labor, risk, and infrastructure the gallery provides — including marketing, fabrication, collector relationships, and logistics.
- Geography matters: working with galleries in non-overlapping regions is generally acceptable; working with competing galleries in the same city almost always damages the primary relationship.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Who in your existing network — artists, advisors, collectors — could make a warm introduction to a gallery or curator you want to reach? How are you nurturing those relationships?
2. Do you have a portfolio (not just a website) that can be read clearly in under five minutes?
3. Can you articulate your work — in writing, verbally, or with a partner's help? What is the clearest one- or two-sentence description of what you make and why?
4. After your last show or project closed, what did you do with the quiet that followed?
5. What galleries or curators operate at the level you are aiming toward? What work are they showing, and how does your practice measure up against that frame of reference?
