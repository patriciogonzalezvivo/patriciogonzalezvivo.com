# 108 · Art History — Systems Learn Uncertainty

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Dr. Paulina Rood (art historian)
**Topic:** The history of humanity's relationship with uncertainty and chance — from pre-religious cultures through quantum physics, Dada, Fluxus, and generative art — as the context for understanding algorithmic and generative art practice

---

## Overview

This lecture traces how human consciousness has negotiated with uncertainty across history, arguing that this oscillation between control and unpredictability — between structure and chance — is exactly what generative art is about. Dr. Rood moves not chronologically but conceptually, showing how each era's relationship with chance reflects its broader epistemological and spiritual conditions: in Paleolithic cultures, chance was a co-creative spiritual force to be listened to; in Byzantine iconography and Islamic art, it was suppressed by divine order; in the Enlightenment, it was rationalized into probability theory; in the 20th century, it was embraced as a liberatory gesture (Dada) or a method for removing authorial ego (Cage, Fluxus); and in generative and on-chain art, it has become a structural condition of how works exist.

The middle section of the lecture focuses on the crucial mid-20th century shift: cybernetics, information theory, and systems thinking reframed noise not as an enemy of order but as the condition under which a system can be known. Error becomes information. Deviation allows feedback. This reframing — from chance as threat to chance as knowledge — is what enables Vera Molnar's "imaginary machine," Sol LeWitt's instruction-as-algorithm, and eventually the full apparatus of digital generative art. The lecture carefully distinguishes different types of generative systems: procedural, algorithmic, cellular automata, data-driven, and on-chain.

The lecture closes by arguing that contemporary generative practices do not represent a radically new condition but a return to a very old one — co-creation with forces beyond the isolated author, whether those forces are named God, nature, noise, algorithm, or blockchain.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Tiche vs. Techne (Aristotle)** — *Tiche*: accidental cause, chance. *Techne*: a form of knowing, the capacity to produce artifacts through conscious application of causes. Technique does not eliminate chance — it intervenes with it.
- **Aleatoric randomness** — Pure chance; a single random act directly determines the outcome
- **Stochastic processes** — Probability distributions; randomness is shaped by likelihood parameters
- **Pseudorandomness** — Randomness generated by an algorithm; given the same initial seed, the result is identical every time
- **Emergence** — No randomness is introduced, yet results appear unpredictable because the interactions within the system become opaque; global behavior arises from local rules
- **Cybernetics** — Mid-20th century science of control and self-regulation in complex systems; core insight: no system can be fully controlled; control is only possible through feedback; error is not failure but information
- **Signal vs. noise (information theory)** — Noise can never be fully removed and without it the signal cannot be measured; noise becomes something the system must work with
- **Vera Molnar's "imaginary machine"** — A set of rules, repetitions, and transformations that can be executed without a computer; her "controlled chance" introduces minimal errors that make the system visible without breaking it
- **Sol LeWitt's instruction-as-algorithm** — "The Idea Becomes a Machine That Makes Art"; instructions designed for precise transmission; randomness delegated and bounded by the system
- **Fluxus event scores as triggers** — Instructions that initiate an event; no correct outcome exists; authorship dissolves
- **Oulipo constraint** — The stricter the rule, the more freedom it generates
- **Cellular automata** — Grids of cells following local rules; no global plan; macroscopic structure emerges from microscopic rules
- **On-chain generative art** — The algorithm is written directly onto the blockchain; at the moment of minting, the algorithm receives a random seed and produces a unique realization that can never be repeated

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Lascaux and Altamira cave painters** — Used cracks and bulges in cave walls to shape animal figures; the environment itself as co-creative force
- **Hans Arp** — Dada collages made by tearing paper and dropping pieces — Chance as a liberatory gesture against imposed meaning
- **Tristan Tzara** — Cut-up poetry — Formal instruction replaces inspiration; artwork emerges as result of procedure
- **Marcel Duchamp** — *Trois Stoppages Étalon* (1913–14) — Dropped threads onto canvas; fixed accidental configurations as new units of measurement
- **John Cage** — *4'33"* (1952) — Performer plays no notes; the hall fills with ambient sound; chance via I Ching coin tosses
- **Yoko Ono / George Brecht** — Fluxus event scores — Short instructions that initiate events rather than realize ideas
- **Raymond Queneau** — *100,000 Billion Poems* — The author writes the structure, the reader generates the text
- **Georges Perec** — *A Void* (1969) — Novel written without the letter "e"
- **Vera Molnar** — Early computer drawings and manual algorithm works (1960s–) — Pioneer of algorithmic art; controlled chance as cybernetic feedback
- **Sol LeWitt** — *Wall Drawings* (from 1967) — Instructions as algorithms for precise transmission
- **Georg Nees** — *Schotter* (1968) — Grid structure with algorithm introducing increasing random deviations; one of the earliest algorithmic artworks
- **John Conway** — *Game of Life* (1970) — Cellular automaton; produces stable structures, oscillators, and self-replicating patterns
- **Tyler Hobbs** — On-chain algorithmic painting; dances between control and uncertainty
- **Dmitri Cherniak** — *Ringers* series — Extreme formal restrictions producing complex architectural compositions
- **Emily Xie** — Generative systems evoking handwriting and calligraphic movements

## Core Takeaways

- Generative art is almost never about chaos — it is about the boundary between control and unpredictability. The artist does not design a form but a process.
- Error is not failure; it is information. Cybernetics reframed deviation as the mechanism through which a system becomes knowable.
- The four types of randomness (aleatoric, stochastic, pseudorandom, emergent) are genuinely different tools with different creative and philosophical implications.
- The stricter the rule, the more freedom it generates (Oulipo principle).
- LeWitt writes algorithms (designed for correct realization; randomness delegated and bounded). Fluxus writes triggers (no correct outcome; authorship dissolves). These are fundamentally different stances.
- Contemporary generative art is a return to an ancient condition: co-creation with forces beyond the isolated author.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Where does chance enter your process, and is it invited, managed, or suppressed? Which type of randomness is operating — aleatoric, stochastic, pseudorandom, or emergent?
2. Are the deviations in your process informing you about the system? Are you learning from the noise?
3. When you give instructions (to yourself or others, or to a system), are you writing an algorithm (correct realization intended) or a trigger (any outcome acceptable)?
4. What happens when you make the rules tighter rather than looser? Have you tested the Oulipo principle in your own work?
5. Who or what is your co-author — the force beyond you that you are co-creating with? Have you named it?
