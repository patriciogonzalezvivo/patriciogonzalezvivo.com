# 120 · Philosophy of Art: What Am I Making? (Part 2)

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Dr. Rebecca Millsop (philosopher, painter)
**Topic:** Continental philosophy of art — four frameworks for understanding what art does and why it matters

---

## Overview

Dr. Millsop's second lecture shifts from analytic philosophy's definitional question ("Is this art?") to the continental tradition's functional question ("What does art do?"). She presents four major frameworks: Benjamin's account of how mechanical reproduction transforms art's nature; Heidegger's claim that art is where truth happens (aletheia as unconcealment); Adorno's argument that art serves society best by refusing to serve it; and Rancière's theory that art is whatever redistributes the sensible — reorganizing what can be seen, heard, said, and thought.

Each framework is traced through its core claim, illustrated with specific artists, and pushed to its limits through critical examples that show where it breaks down. Benjamin's aura/reproduction binary is complicated by works like the Rothko Chapel and Beeple's NFT. Heidegger's disclosure framework struggles with flat, anti-experiential conceptual art. Adorno's autonomy-through-refusal leads to a practical dead end for artists who need to survive economically. Rancière's redistribution framework risks being so expansive that anything qualifies.

Dr. Millsop consistently encourages artists to use these frameworks as tools in a toolkit rather than competing dogmas to be resolved. The Q&A discussion extends the frameworks into live tensions: the persistence of aura in an age of digital saturation, the problem of AI and authorless images, the privilege embedded in Adorno's "starving artist" ideal, and how artists like Sonia Almeida use painting's resistance as a strategic tool.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Aura** (Benjamin) — the unique presence of an artwork in a specific time and place; the "here and now" of an original that weakens as copies multiply through mechanical reproduction
- **Aura → political art** — for Benjamin, two paths after reproduction: art for art's sake (reactionary recreation of aura) vs. politicized art that embraces reproduction as democratic force
- **Aletheia / unconcealment** (Heidegger) — truth as "un-hiddenness"; art doesn't represent truth but is where truth happens; the Greek temple as paradigm case
- **Strange tools** (Alva Noë) — art takes organized activities (seeing, moving, listening) and puts them on display; it disrupts habitual perception and unconceals the structure of experience
- **Culture industry** (Adorno/Frankfurt School) — capitalism produces predictable, consumable cultural products that reinforce the status quo; art's job is to refuse absorption
- **Autonomy through refusal** (Adorno) — real art resists commodification by being difficult; its political power comes from not being useful, not giving easy pleasure
- **Dissensus vs. consensus** (Bishop, extending Adorno) — participatory art that creates comfortable, feel-good community moments may be absorbed by the logic of consensus; politically effective art creates antagonism and genuine friction
- **Distribution of the sensible** (Rancière) — at any moment, a configuration determines what is visible/invisible, audible/inaudible, sayable/unsayable, and who counts as a legitimate participant
- **Redistribution of the sensible** — art's political function: making visible what was invisible, audible what was inaudible; the politics is in formal operations, not in explicit content
- **Aesthetic regime** (Rancière) — the current historical regime of art, characterized by breaking down hierarchies of subject matter and suspending normal purposes

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Mark Rothko** — *Rothko Chapel*, Houston — cited to complicate Benjamin: site-specific, aura-bearing, yet politically active
- **Sherrie Levine** — *After Evans* — re-photographed Walker Evans' Depression-era photos; makes Benjamin's aura/reproduction tension visible as art itself
- **Trevor Paglen** — facial recognition training data sets displayed as art — pushes Benjamin past his assumptions; asks what aura and reproduction mean when images are made by machines for machines
- **Agnes Martin** — grid paintings — pale, quiet, refusing spectacle; for Adorno, more politically powerful than the Hope poster precisely because it cannot be consumed quickly
- **Carrie Mae Weems** — *Kitchen Table Series* — widely reproduced, emotionally accessible; challenges Adorno's equation of popularity with compromise
- **Yinka Shonibare** — headless Victorian mannequins in African Dutch wax fabric — accessible, popular, sharply post-colonial; shows accessibility and critique can coexist
- **Santiago Sierra** — paid unemployed men to have a line tattooed across their backs — makes exploitation visible without redemption narrative
- **Theaster Gates** — transforms abandoned Chicago buildings into cultural centers — challenges Adorno: political power through engagement rather than refusal
- **James Turrell** — *Sky Spaces* — Heideggerian unconcealment of sky as act of perception
- **Kerry James Marshall** — large-scale paintings of Black life using intensely Black paint — redistributes the sensible by making Black bodies monumentally visible within art-historical space
- **Forensic Architecture** — 3D model of Syria's Saydnaya prison from survivor sound memory — Rancière in purest form: making state violence perceptible and navigable

## Core Takeaways

- Continental philosophy asks not "Is this art?" but "What does art do?" — four frameworks provide different answers, each useful as a lens rather than a final truth.
- Benjamin: technology changes what art is; the tension between aura and circulation remains real for practicing artists.
- Heidegger (via Noë): art unconceals organized activities we take for granted; it makes the structure of perception itself visible; art is a strange tool that interrupts automation.
- Adorno: art's resistance to easy consumption is its political power — but taken to its extreme this framework leads to martyrdom and ignores the economic reality of making a living.
- Rancière: art is political through formal operations, not through message; it redistributes the sensible by reconfiguring who gets to be seen, heard, and counted.
- These four frameworks are not mutually exclusive; a single work can navigate post-aura conditions (Benjamin), disclose truth (Heidegger), maintain some autonomy (Adorno), and redistribute the sensible (Rancière) simultaneously.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Does your work have aura? If yes, where does it live — in the object, the space, the viewer, somewhere else?
2. What organized activity does your work put on display? What does your audience normally do on autopilot that your work might interrupt?
3. Where does your work fall on the spectrum between autonomous (difficult, refusing easy pleasure) and instrumental (useful, accessible, communicative)? Is that where you want it to be?
4. What does your work make visible, audible, or sayable that wasn't before? What distribution of the sensible is it challenging?
5. When you make choices about accessibility, edition size, platform, or pricing, are you taking a philosophical position about what art is and what it's for?
