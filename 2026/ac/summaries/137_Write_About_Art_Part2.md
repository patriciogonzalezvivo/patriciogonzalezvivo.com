# 137 · How to Write About Your Art — Part 2

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Haiver (program instructor)
**Topic:** Writing the artist statement — answering "What might this mean?" and "Why does this matter?" through scaffolding, cliché avoidance, and free-writing exercises

---

## Overview

This lecture-workshop continues from Part 1 by addressing the second and third core questions of art writing: *How does the form or event carry meaning?* and *Why does this matter to the world at large?* The instructor builds an argument around the concept of **scaffolding** — the idea that a mature artist's role is not to explain the work exhaustively but to give viewers enough structure to think more deeply about it themselves. 

The session opens with a distinction between intellectual and artistic communication: Charles Bukowski's frame ("An intellectual says a simple thing in a hard way. An artist says a hard thing in a simple way") becomes the governing principle. The instructor dismantles common myths: that revealing meaning diminishes mystery, that avoiding jargon means dumbing down, and that a work's significance must be stated grandiosely.

A major focus is the systematic cataloging and avoidance of **art-world clichés** — the tired verbs, abstract nouns, and pseudo-intellectual phrases that signal lazy thinking and train readers to skip artist statements entirely. The session also models how to write in service of wonder and questions rather than answers, citing Lucille Clifton: "I don't write out of what I know. I write out of what I wonder."

The workshop portions use timed free-writing exercises (sense descriptions, eight guided questions, a letter to a trusted friend) to move past self-censorship and into discovery. The session closes with seven things to avoid and a homework protocol: write badly first, set the work aside, mark the strong bits a week later, then assemble and edit.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Scaffolding (Bloom's taxonomy / pedagogy)** — The artist's job is not to explain the work in full but to set up a framework that lets viewers discover deeper meanings on their own. Strong scaffolding increases viewing time, depth of engagement, and intellectual satisfaction.
- **The communication principle** — "Tell the truth and not the facts" (Maya Angelou). Facts are boring and often push readers away. Dig for the essence — the human truth beneath technical details.
- **The P.T. Anderson / Magnolia principle** — The timing of obscurity matters. In a film, a director can throw a wrench into the viewer's understanding at the climax *after* investment is built. In an artist statement, you're writing at the *beginning*, before viewers care. Therefore: clarity and invitation come first; mystery follows naturally.
- **Openness spectrum (Umberto Eco)** — Closed work (predetermined meaning) → Guided (scaffolded meaning) → Open (viewer completes meaning) → Underbuilt (work collapses into vagueness). The open work is precisely structured, not vague.
- **Free-writing as material gathering** — The goal of rough drafts is volume and discovery, not perfection. Write badly, write clichés, write anything to fill a barrel with words. Editing is a separate activity. Giving yourself permission to write badly unlocks the voice.
- **Sense description as exercise** — Constraining yourself to describe one work using only a single sense (taste, touch, smell, hearing, or sight) pushes you past obvious first details and into texture. Applied to your own work, it generates fresh language.
- **Eight questions on meaning** — (1) What is this piece about to me? (2) What inspired this work / set its creation in motion? (3) What are the broad themes? (4) What is the work trying to express or communicate? (5) What questions is this piece trying to answer? (6) How does it dialogue with other artworks or the history of the medium? (7) What is the one thing you want people to walk away with or remember? (8) What might be the essence — the feeling or question that makes it special?
- **Mature artists make meaning, not images** — Young artists make art they love and can't entirely explain why. Mature artists develop a consistent set of themes and theories about how their work speaks. This is the thesis statement.
- **Borrowed theory is allowed** — Nothing is original. Look at artists who inspire you, note what theories they're working with, borrow and adapt those ideas to your own practice at 10% transformation. Don't have to invent everything from scratch.
- **The absence of theory is a theory** — Artists who say "I have no theory, I just made it" are claiming a theory: that art should resist articulation. Recognize this choice as such and own it.

## Art-World Clichés to Avoid (Comprehensive List)

**Tired verbs and phrases to strip from drafts:**
- "explores themes of"
- "investigates the relationship between X and Y"
- "interrogates notions of"
- "challenges traditional ideas of"
- "creates a dialogue between"
- "the viewer is invited to"
- "blurs the boundary between"
- "exists in a liminal space between"
- "speaks to broader questions of"

**Worn-out adjectives:**
- Immersive, urgent, relational, mystical, nuanced, provocative, liminal (especially liminal)

**Rule of thumb:** If you've heard someone else say it before, cross it out and rewrite to find what you haven't heard before. Readers perform triage and categorize too quickly when they recognize a cliché — they will skip ahead or stop reading.

## Core Takeaways

- The three-question framework (Part 1 + Part 2 complete): What is it? What might it mean? Why does it matter?
- **Scaffolding is your primary job as a writer.** You are not explaining the work; you are setting up the conditions for viewers to think more deeply about it than they would on their own.
- **Avoid art-world clichés at all costs.** They train readers to skip your text and categorize you too quickly. If you've heard it before, find a new way to say it.
- **Write from wonder, not from answers.** The best art raises more questions than it answers. The artist statement names the questions you are exploring — it doesn't resolve them.
- **Broad themes, not granular motifs.** Don't explain every symbol or justify every choice. Give viewers a framework; let them discover.
- **The open work is precisely structured.** Vagueness is a failure mode. Openness to interpretation is built through clear scaffolding, not through unclear writing.
- **Free-writing permission is essential.** You will write badly, write clichés, write sentences you'll discard. That's the point. Quantity of material is more important than quality in the gathering phase.
- **Editing happens later.** Write first draft without censoring. Set it aside for a week. Return, mark the strong bits, circle phrases, box words. Copy those into a new document, rearrange, write connective tissue.

## Core Exercise Structure (Homework Protocol)

**Session 1:** Gather material through three timed free-writing exercises
1. Five-minute sense description of the room you're in (using only one sense)
2. Two-minute sense description of your artwork (using the same sense)
3. 15-minute free-write on first four meaning questions
4. 10-minute free-write on second four meaning questions
5. 10-minute letter to a trusted friend answering why this work is important, how it's a breakthrough, how it pushes your arc forward, what's different this time, and what makes it unique

**Session 2 (one week later):** Edit and assemble
1. Pull out the writing, mark the strong bits
2. Underline strong sentences, circle strong phrases, box strong words
3. Copy those fragments into a Word document
4. Rearrange and play with order
5. Write connective tissue (the sentences that stitch fragments into paragraphs)

**The assembled version** becomes your first complete draft, answering all three questions with the structure: What is it? What might it mean? Why does it matter?

## Seven Things to Avoid (Red Flags in Finished Statements)

1. **Not having a thesis.** Collectors need to believe the artist thinks deeply about their work. Without a thesis, you say nothing. A thesis doesn't need to be grandiose — it just needs to be present.
2. **Telling the viewer what they will think or feel.** This is easily refuted: "You might notice X" (but what if I don't?). Let viewers complete their own experience.
3. **Over-using "My work explores X and Y."** Juxtaposing big concepts (nature/culture, digital/analog) without specificity reeks of international art English.
4. **Writing only for insiders.** Fifty-cent words are fine; explain them or make them clear through context. Don't wall off your work behind jargon.
5. **Being overly technical about process.** Process is one-third of the "What is it?" question, not the whole statement. Over-indexing on technical details pushes viewers away.
6. **Grandiose or pretentious declarations.** Not every work is monumental. Readers see through overblown language. Stay reasonable and traceable to your answers to questions 1 and 2.
7. **Comparing yourself to or quoting the greats.** Don't say "Like Picasso..." or quote Foucault/Deleuze/Derrida. Come up with your own theory. You are not them; what people want is to know you.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. What are the eight meanings or questions your current work is exploring? Free-write answers to the eight-question framework without self-censorship.
2. Pick one work and describe it using only a single sense (not the visual). What new language emerges?
3. Who is the "trusted friend" in your mind — the person you'd write a letter to about this work? Write that letter.
4. Go through your current artist statements and flag every tired verb (explores, investigates, interrogates, etc.). For each one, rewrite that sentence in the language you'd actually use at dinner.
5. After writing, set your draft aside for one week. When you return, mark the strong bits. What did you write that surprised you or sounded like your actual voice?
6. Apply the scaffolding test: After reading your statement, can a viewer who didn't understand the work at first look now think more deeply about it? Or does the statement assume too much prior knowledge?
