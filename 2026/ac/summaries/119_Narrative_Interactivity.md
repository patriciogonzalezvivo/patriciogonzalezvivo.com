# 119 · Art History Lecture: Narrative & Interactivity (Part 2)

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Dr. Polina Rud
**Topic:** Interactive and non-linear narrative in art history — from experimental music through Net Art to game art

---

## Overview

Dr. Rud traces the long arc from fixed, linear storytelling to open, participatory, and ergodic narrative structures. Beginning with Walter Benjamin's critique of modernity's destruction of communal storytelling, she moves through experimental music (Stockhausen), Fluxus performance (Dick Higgins), and video/film installations (Nam June Paik, Lynn Hirschman-Leeson, Graham Weinbrun) to show how each medium incrementally transferred agency from author to audience.

The second major block covers Net Art, examining how artists embedded in state-controlled or corporate digital infrastructures — from Brazilian video texto to the early World Wide Web — used hypertext, databases, and networked data as both formal experiments and political critiques. Works by Eduardo Kac, Olia Lialina, and Susan Birkenhager demonstrate that interactivity can be liberating, poetic, or deliberately obstructive, depending on the ideological context in which it operates.

The final block turns to game art, tracing interactive systems back to military radar technology. Artists including Harun Farocki, Molleindustria, Angela Washko, Theo Triantafyllidis, Mary Flanagan, and Pippin Barr use game structures to stage ethical dilemmas, critique capitalism, rehearse difficult conversations, or simply refuse the logic of productivity — revealing that games are laboratories of decision-making and, potentially, spaces of rebellion.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Linear vs. non-linear narrative** — the historical shift from fixed, hero-centered storytelling to open, fragmented, audience-navigable structures
- **Ergodic structures** — narratives that require effort and participation to unfold (Espen Aarseth via Borges); no predetermined path
- **Rhizome / rhizomatic logic** (Deleuze & Guattari) — a network without hierarchy where any point connects to any other
- **Database logic** (Lev Manovich) — digital systems store elements as collections accessible in multiple orders; meaning emerges from paths and choices
- **Active vs. subjunctive states** (Weinbrun) — the active state (making a selection) vs. the subjunctive (awareness that another choice could have led differently), which destabilizes narrative inevitability
- **Interactivity as political act** — embedding non-linear participatory work within systems built for control is a form of critique
- **Game vs. play** — games are defined by rules, goals, and measurable outcomes; play is open-ended and exists for the experience itself
- **Feedback loop** (radar origin) — seeing → reacting → adjusting; the foundational triadic relationship underlying interactive media

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Karlheinz Stockhausen** — *Klavierstück XI* — score of 19 fragments, performer chooses sequence; earliest example of performer agency in composition
- **Dick Higgins / Fluxus** — *Danger Music No. 17* — anti-theatre with minimal instructions; collapses boundary between art and everyday life
- **Nam June Paik** — *Random Access* — magnetic tape fragments attached to wall; visitor uses playback head to create non-linear sound
- **Lynn Hirschman-Leeson** — *Lorna* — interactive video installation using TV remote control to navigate a fictional agoraphobic woman's life
- **Graham Weinbrun** — *The Earl King* — interactive cinema on laser disc combining Goethe and Freud; viewer edits film in real time
- **Eduardo Kac** — *Reabracadabra* — animated visual poem on Brazil's state-controlled video-texto network; political disruption via participatory poetry
- **Olia Lialina** — *My Boyfriend Came Back from the War* — browser-based hypertext; slow-loading GIFs as metaphors for post-war silence and communicative failure
- **Harun Farocki** — *Serious Games* — video work examining military use of game simulation for training and therapy
- **Molleindustria** — *McDonald's Video Game* / *Every Day the Same Dream* — systemic critique; players operate within capitalist structures to expose their logic
- **Angela Washko** — *World of Warcraft* interventions / *The Game* — uses game space to rehearse difficult conversations about sexism and manipulation
- **Theo Triantafyllidis** — *Pastoral* — game without objectives; foregrounds presence and play over achievement
- **Mary Flanagan** — Giant Joystick — monumental controller requiring collective bodily collaboration
- **Pippin Barr** — *The Artist Is Present* / *Let's Play Ancient Greek Punishment* — games that stage waiting and the limits of the hero-player fantasy
- **LaTurbo Avedon** — avatar existing only through interaction; narrative emerges through co-creation

## Core Takeaways

- Storytelling was originally a communal, collective act; modernity isolated us and stripped our power to exchange sincere, lived narratives.
- The 20th-century avant-garde progressively transferred agency from author/composer to performer/viewer/participant.
- Non-linear and ergodic structures reveal narrative as a temporary configuration shaped by choices and context, not a closed, inevitable sequence.
- Game art inherits its interactive logic from military radar technology; games are laboratories of decision-making, consequence, and behavioral conditioning.
- The distinction between "game" (rules, goals, win/loss conditions) and "play" (open-ended, present-focused) is a generative tension for artists working with interactivity.
- Every interactive artwork raises the questions: who decides? within which system? at what cost? how are we engaging our viewers?

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. How could you incorporate choice — real, illusory, or deliberately frustrated — into your artistic practice?
2. Do you leverage any technical or material aspect of your medium as a metaphor, the way Lialina used slow internet load times as a stand-in for post-war silence?
3. Is your work closer to "game" (structured, goal-oriented, measurable) or "play" (open-ended, experiential, resistant to productivity logic)?
4. What system are participants (viewers, collectors, institutions) participating in when they engage with your work? What does that participation support or reinforce?
5. Could you embed a political or ideological critique inside a familiar interactive format, the way Molleindustria uses game mechanics to expose capitalist logic?
