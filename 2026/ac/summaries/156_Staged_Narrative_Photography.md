# 156 · Art History: Staged Narrative Photography

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Dr. Elliot Krasnopoler (PhD, Bryn Mawr College; BFA, Rochester Institute of Technology, Photography; MA, History of Art, Williams College; editor, *Breaking the Silence: Methods of Writing Art History*; 2021–2022 George Gurney Postdoctoral Fellow, Smithsonian American Art Museum; staff at Smarthistory.org) — see also [[156_Epistemology_of_Photography|the companion Lesson 156 session]] on photography's evidentiary claims, delivered by the same guest.
**Topic:** A chronological art-history lecture tracing "staged narrative photography" (also called tableau photography, or Coleman's "the directorial mode") from 1700s French absorptive painting and history painting, through the medium's earliest tableaux vivants, into its full flowering from the 1970s onward (Cindy Sherman, Jeff Wall, Gregory Crewdson, and many others) — asking what it means to compose a photograph the way a painter composes a canvas, and why artists turned to that mode specifically as photography entered postmodernism.

---

## Overview

Krasnopoler opens with Jeff Wall's *A Sudden Gust of Wind* (1993) — a 7–8-foot-wide photograph built from multiple exposures, actors, location scouts, and a full production crew over several days — to establish immediately that "staged narrative photography" describes something categorically different from what most people mean by "taking a photograph." He is explicit that this is not a clean art-historical "-ism" with a single lineage — Coleman called it "the directorial mode," others call it tableau photography — but a loose amalgam of artists working in very different registers toward a shared concern.

The lecture has three movements: (1) the pictorial and narrative logic staged photography inherits from 1700s French painting — absorption, tableau, history painting; (2) why artists turned to this mode specifically, framed through modernism vs. postmodernism, questions of truth and fiction, and mass-media saturation; (3) a wide survey of staged-narrative work organized by theme (art history, documentary, family, cinema). A constant refrain throughout is that **the category resists clean rules** — for nearly every distinction Krasnopoler draws, he shows an artist (Deanna Lawson above all) who breaks it, and treats that messiness as the actual, interesting territory rather than a flaw in the lecture's framing.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

### Painting's inheritance: absorption, tableau, history painting

- **Absorption (via Michael Fried).** Chardin's *A Man Reading* (1734) shows a figure completely absorbed in his own task, performing nothing for the viewer — "as if the painting denies the beholder." This shift — a pictorial space that exists independently of whether anyone is looking at it — is, per Fried, foundational to how modern painting understands pictorial address, eventually running all the way to Abstract Expressionism. Contemporary absorptive photography: Carrie Schneider's *Women Reading Women* series; Jeff Wall's *Adrian Walker, Artist, Drawing from a Specimen* (a near-direct remake of Chardin's draftsman).
- **Tableau (Denis Diderot).** A term borrowed from theater: the painting should have total internal coherence — every figure, gesture, and object relates legibly to every other, entirely within the frame, without needing external explanation, the way a well-staged theatrical scene reads without a program note.
- **History painting.** Extends absorption and tableau logic to *shared, known* narratives (myth, history) rather than invented ones — Jacques-Louis David's *Oath of the Horatii* (1784, painted just before the French Revolution, widely read as carrying a political meta-narrative about its own moment) is the model example: does the viewer recognize the story, and does the arrangement of figures successfully carry it, the way a well-composed sentence carries meaning?
- **Key point:** this absorption/tableau/history-painting lineage is exactly what staged narrative photography imports into a medium that, at its invention, was assumed to only *record* rather than *compose*.

### The medium's own history of staging

- **Tableau vivant.** French for "live painting" — the pre-photographic practice (post-1700s) of costumed people posing stationary to recreate a scene from an important narrative. Julia Margaret Cameron's *King Lear* and *Merlin and Vivien* photographs (staged in her own home, with an actual oak tree brought indoors for the latter) are staged tableaux vivants captured on film — asking, at the very origin of the medium, what photography specifically could do with narrative that theater and painting could not.
- **Early composite images.** Henry Peach Robinson's *Fading Away* (a composite of five negatives, depicting a taboo subject — someone dying) establishes multi-exposure, multi-negative image construction as a technique with 1850s roots, directly continuous with Jeff Wall's own multi-exposure compositing over a century later.
- **A note on "staging" as a much broader category.** Still-life, advertising, and even canonical "documentary" photography (Walker Evans, staging a farmer's kitchen interior for compositional effect) all involve staging — the useful distinction is **intention**: still life stages to elevate the photographer's eye; advertising stages to sell; documentary claims to stage in service of truth/veracity. Staged narrative photography is a distinct category not because it's the only staged photography, but because of what it does with staging.
- **Cindy Sherman's *Untitled Film Stills*** (1970s) is named as arguably the *first* staged narrative photograph proper: referencing 1940s–60s advertising film stills (almost always a woman, often in distress — femme fatale imagery), each image alludes to a larger narrative it never reveals. Crucially, it gives almost no real information about Sherman herself — the staging becomes a critique of a history of images and constructed femininity rather than documentation of anything real, which Krasnopoler identifies as the medium's first real postmodern move (compare its authorship dimension in [[158_Philosophy_What_Makes_It_Mine_Part2|Lesson 158]] and its editioning/legacy dimension in [[160_Pricing_And_Editioning_Part2|Lesson 160's Sherman Legacy Project case study]]).

### Why artists turned to it: modernism vs. postmodernism

- **Modernism, defined simply:** each medium should be as much like itself as possible — painting should be painterly (Pollock), sculpture should be exactly its material (David Smith's unpainted steel), photography should be only like photography. Modernist photography examples: Edward Weston's *Dunes* and Margaret Bourke-White's George Washington Bridge — formal, high-contrast, found (not staged) compositions that "could only be made with photography," capturing something the photographer discovered rather than designed.
- **Staged narrative photography as postmodern.** Rather than being as much like photography as possible, it deliberately layers in painting, cinema, and visual-culture history, reaching *outside* photography's own medium-specificity. Adi Nees's *Job and Friends* (2007) — four men on a sidewalk, oddly lit, non-addressing the camera — is read against Bellini's *Sacra Conversazione* altarpiece tradition (a "sacred conversation" among saints who never lived at the same time), a connection Krasnopoler flags as possibly coincidental but genuinely illustrative: the image is reaching toward painting and Christian iconography, not toward photography's own history.
- **Jeff Wall's *A Sudden Gust of Wind*** (1993) as the clearest single case: an explicit remaking of Hokusai's woodcut *A Sudden Gust of Wind*, printed 7–8 feet wide and museum-mounted — cross-cultural (Japan/Canada), cross-medium (print/photograph), and operating at the physical scale and institutional cost of painting even while remaining "completely photographic."
- **Truth and fiction.** Staged narrative photography subverts photography's foundational promise (what you see existed in front of the camera), emerging alongside poststructuralism (Derrida questioning whether language means what we think it means). Examples: Kelly Connell's *Double Life* series — the same hired actress digitally composited into both roles of a couple, so convincingly that the fabrication isn't visually obvious even up close; Philip-Lorca diCorcia's *Street Scenes* — fully staged, lit, and posed New York and Tokyo street photographs that read, at a glance, as unplanned documentary; Jeff Wall's *Men Waiting* — Wall hired real day laborers he'd observed waiting in Vancouver and had them re-pose the exact waiting he'd witnessed, producing an image that is simultaneously entirely staged and entirely true to what was actually happening.
- **Mass media saturation.** Later Cindy Sherman (*Socialites* series) and Alex Prager's *La Grande Sortie* (Prager's own title referenced as "California" in the transcript) engage constructed identity, race, class, and influencer/social-media visual culture directly and overtly. Andreas Gursky's *99 Cent* is offered as a related-but-distinct case: not staged narrative, but emerging from the same era and engaging the same questions (mass media, scale, whether photography can occupy a wall "like a painting") through a different (non-narrative, landscape/typological) photographic tradition.

### Survey by theme

- **Art history remakes.** Jeff Wall's Adrian Walker (after Chardin) and *A Sudden Gust of Wind* (after Hokusai); Yinka Shonibare's near-exact remakes of Goya's *The Sleep of Reason Produces Monsters* and Hogarth's *A Rake's Progress*, restaged using brightly patterned "Dutch wax" textile associated with West African dress — Shonibare (of mixed British/Nigerian heritage) uses the remake to think through colonization, race, and the modern era via the history of painting and printmaking itself; Stan Douglas's *Abbott & Cordova, 7 August 1971* — a meticulously researched (archives, news accounts) recreation of an actual Vancouver protest/arrest moment from the 1970s, made in the 2000s, described as "anachronic and heterochronic" — made in modernity but built to look older, about a real historical moment its maker did not live through as a working photographer.
- **Documentary-adjacent.** Hannah Starkey's images (tilted framing, off-kilter mirrors, a moth pinned to a hand, matched color palettes like pink umbrella/pink hair or a scattering of yellow objects) look "shot from the hip" but are meticulously art-directed with hired actors and location scouting — using documentary's own visual grammar against itself. Philip-Lorca diCorcia's *Street Scenes* (above). An-My Lê's reenactment photographs: embedding with a Vietnam War reenactment group in North Carolina (1999) with a 4×5 black-and-white camera — genuinely staged (it's a reenactment) but not designed by the artist, who discovers rather than directs the scene; and her 29 Palms series, photographing real U.S. military training exercises before deployment — not a reenactment at all, but ambiguous enough in appearance to raise the same "what am I actually looking at" question. Deanna Lawson's portraiture (discussed at length below) also belongs here.
- **Family.** Sally Mann — large-format (8×10) photographs of her own children that are simultaneously found and directed ("let's do that again, when the light is good"); real events, her own children (not hired actors), yet carefully composed. Larry Sultan's photographs of his own aging parents, made from an explicit pre-written shot list, engaging directly with what's public vs. private and whether the project's implied "dashed American dream" allegory belongs to his parents' actual lives or to Larry's authorial framing of them. Tina Barney — kept a 4×5 camera permanently set up in her family's house until they stopped reacting to it, then photographed apparently unstaged, ordinary domestic moments; the ambiguity of "is this staged or not" becomes productive rather than resolved.
- **Cinema.** Sherman's *Untitled Film Stills* (again, as the cinema-relationship's founding case). Gregory Crewdson's elaborate soundstage productions (built at MASS MoCA, with furniture built at two-thirds scale in one image to make a submerged actress read as larger than life; another remaking Millais's *Ophelia*) — full film-crew-scale productions (30-person crews, half-page shot scripts, extensive location scouting) that treat the photographer as a creative director in the fullest cinematic sense, giving rise to the term "the directorial mode." Alex Prager's audience/theater-watching images and movie-scene-compression stills, explicitly described by Prager as "pushing photography towards cinema through freeze frames." Carrie Mae Weems's *Kitchen Table Series* (1990s) — a fictional, autobiographical-feeling drama staged around one recurring set (a kitchen table) across many images that function individually but accumulate cumulatively, like film vignettes, to address femininity, domestic space, and Black life; Weems is also a theorist/writer/conceptual artist, not solely a photographer.

## The Deanna Lawson Problem (a recurring case against clean categories)

Lawson's portraits (e.g., *Ring Bearer* and works featuring "Mama Goma," 2014) are singled out repeatedly as the lecture's best argument against tidy rules. A single centered sitter confronts the camera directly (non-absorptive, like Alex Soth's *Charles*) — but unlike Soth (who found his subject and setting largely as-is on a road trip), Lawson embeds slowly with a community, builds real trust over time, and *then* asks to rearrange furniture, props, and clothing before making the image. The resulting picture is genuinely ambiguous: is this the sitter's own space and belongings, or Lawson's staged vision imposed on it? Is it a portrait of a real person's life in the Democratic Republic of Congo, or a work of Lawson's own imagination? Krasnopoler explicitly uses Lawson to argue that **the two poles (found portrait vs. fully authored tableau) are not opposites on the same line but can operate simultaneously in the same image** — "it sort of ends up playing both chords at the same time."

## Truth, Betrayal, and the Present Moment (Q&A themes)

- **The "vacillation of meaning."** Knowing a photograph is staged (An-My Lê's reenactment; Deanna Lawson's arranged interior) can retroactively and completely change how an image reads — a vacillation Krasnopoler argues is specific to photography's history of claimed truth-value in a way it isn't for painting (always understood as constructed) or cinema (always understood as fiction). The open question, raised by the host and left unresolved: how does an artist manage that flip without it registering as *betrayal* to the viewer — inviting the viewer further in, rather than pushing them away?
- **Effort as value, and its erosion.** Staged narrative photography's market rise (peaking, per Krasnopoler's own research impression, around 2012) tracks a period when this kind of image required enormous, visible effort (large-format film, weeks of scouting, professional crews, expensive scanning and printing) — effort that conferred value and justified institutional embrace (Gagosian, Hauser & Wirth representation; MoMA moving photographers out of the photography department and into contemporary-art exhibitions). Generative AI and smartphone-era image proliferation threaten exactly that effort-based value: a task that took Crewdson weeks (stitching multiple exposures into one coherent scene) can now be approximated by a text prompt. Krasnopoler frames this, cautiously, as a real "tragedy" for the economics of the mode, not just a technical shift.
- **Text and disclosure.** How much a photographer *shows their work* (Crewdson's blog with sketches and outtakes; Jeff Wall's own extensive theoretical writing about how he wants his photographs read; Carrie Mae Weems's nearly invisible white-on-white punched text in the *Kitchen Table Series*, legible only from extremely close and at the right gallery lighting angle) shapes how much interpretive control the artist retains versus hands to critics and historians — echoed directly by the host's real anecdote about hired actors used in a Super Bowl "He Gets Us" tableau ad (Julia Fullerton-Batten) being accused of being AI-generated, precisely because effortful, carefully lit staged photography and AI-generated imagery can now look alike to a skeptical public.

## Core Takeaways

- **Photographs have been staged since the medium's 1839 invention — the 1970s–2000s "staged narrative" mode is a specific, historically bounded intensification, not the origin of staging itself.** Krasnopoler is explicit about this to avoid a false-novelty narrative.
- **Staged narrative photography imports painting's own pictorial logic (absorption, tableau, history painting) into a medium originally assumed to only record.** The through-line runs directly from Chardin and David to Wall and Crewdson.
- **The mode is fundamentally postmodern: it deliberately subverts photographic medium-specificity rather than pursuing it, reaching outward into painting, cinema, and mass visual culture, and interrogating how images make meaning (post-structuralism) rather than simply what they show.**
- **The category resists clean rules on purpose — Deanna Lawson above all shows that "found portrait" and "fully authored tableau" can operate in the same image simultaneously, not as a contradiction to resolve but as the actual interesting territory.**
- **The mode's economic and institutional rise (large-format cameras, expensive scanning and printing, museum-scale prints, major gallery representation) was built on *visible effort* as a component of value — a component now under real pressure from generative-AI image-making, which can approximate the same "impossible" composited scenes without the labor that once certified them as serious art.**
- **Disclosure of process (blogs, artist writing, visible or hidden text) is itself an authorial choice that shapes how much interpretive control an artist keeps versus hands over to critics, historians, and viewers — the "vacillation of meaning" a viewer experiences on learning a work is staged is specific to photography's particular history of claimed truth.**

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Where does your machine-assisted portrait work (Hybrids, Memories, Santos) sit on the absorption/theatricality axis Krasnopoler opens with — is the machine-drawn skeleton before the hand arrives an absorptive image (existing independent of a viewer) or one already staged toward an address to the viewer, and does the hand-completed final image change that answer?
2. Krasnopoler's postmodern staged photographers deliberately reach *outside* their own medium (into painting, cinema, altarpiece tradition) rather than pursuing medium-specificity. Where in your own practice — plotter work reaching toward the history of painting, shader work reaching toward astronomical/scientific imaging conventions — are you doing something structurally similar, and have you named the specific outside tradition you're reaching into the way Wall names Hokusai or Shonibare names Goya?
3. Deanna Lawson's work operates as both a found portrait and a fully authored tableau at once, refusing to resolve which. Is there a work in your practice (an astronomical instrument image, a satellite-restoration piece) where a similar "plays both chords" ambiguity — real data and fully authored intervention operating simultaneously — is present but unnamed, and would naming it (rather than resolving it) strengthen the piece?
4. Given the lecture's closing argument that visible effort long certified staged photography's seriousness and market value, and that generative AI now threatens that certification — how does your own practice's already-considerable visible technical effort (plotter mechanics, satellite data processing, custom shader/generative code) function as a comparable certification, and is that effort currently visible to a viewer, or does it disappear behind a finished image the way Crewdson's or Wall's production process would without their blogs and books?
5. For any work that risks the "vacillation of meaning" Krasnopoler describes — a viewer's understanding flipping entirely once they learn a machine, an algorithm, or a data-restoration process was involved — have you decided, deliberately, how and when to disclose that (in the work itself, in wall text, in an artist statement), or is the disclosure currently accidental or absent?
