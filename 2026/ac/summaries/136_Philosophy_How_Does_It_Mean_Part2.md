## Overview

Where Part 1 (Lesson 127) examined the "meaning levers" available to artists through analytic frameworks — Peirce's sign types, Goodman's symbol systems, Carroll's intentionalism, Walton's categories — Part 2 shifts to the larger systems and worlds that artworks enter. These are "meaning conditions": the codes, myths, audience positions, and structures of openness that determine how a work will be read before the artist even begins.

The lecture covers four continental frameworks in sequence. Ferdinand de Saussure's structuralism establishes that signs mean relationally — through their position within a system of differences — not by carrying intrinsic essence. Roland Barthes extends structuralism into cultural myth theory, showing how images and objects move from denotation through connotation to myth: a natural-seeming story that conceals its historical construction. Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding model foregrounds the audience as an active, socially positioned decoder who may accept, negotiate, or reject the meaning an artist built in. Umberto Eco's open work theory frames the artist's task as building structured conditions for meaning rather than controlling a single correct reading.

Together the four frameworks shift the studio question from "what does this mean?" to "what system makes this readable, what myth is already present for my audience, who is decoding and from what position, and what kind of openness does this work build?"

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Structuralism (Saussure):** Signs are composed of signifier (sound-image or mark) and signified (concept); meaning is relational — a sign means because of its difference from other signs in the same system, not because it carries essence. The white cube gallery means "authenticity" not naturally but because the system says so.
- **Code:** The system of differences that makes a sign readable. Artists work with, bend, break, or expose codes. If your audience does not share the code, the sign does not communicate.
- **Myth (Barthes):** Three-layer model — denotation (what is shown) → connotation (what it suggests) → myth (the larger cultural story made to feel natural and inevitable). Myth turns history into nature; ideology hides inside apparent common sense. The artist's job is to decide whether to use, expose, interrupt, or inadvertently repeat a myth.
- **Encoding/Decoding (Stuart Hall):** Artists encode meaning through material, formal, and contextual choices (using the dominant code). Audiences decode from their own social position (class, race, gender, education). Three decoding positions: **dominant** (accepts the preferred meaning), **negotiated** (accepts in general but adjusts for local context), **oppositional** (fully reads the encoding and systematically rejects it). An oppositional viewer is not confused — they understand the work and resist.
- **Reception as the site of the work:** Some practices deliberately stage the decoding as the meaning event — the gap between what is encoded and what the audience can or will decode *is* the work.
- **Open Work (Eco):** Artworks require completion by an audience; openness is not vagueness but structured possibility. Four degrees: (a) **closed** — tightly steered toward one reading (protest poster); (b) **guided** — strong structure that creates conditions without prescribing emotion (Maya Lin, Vietnam Veterans Memorial); (c) **open** — field of movement with limits (Lygia Clark's *Bichos*); (d) **underbuilt** — artist has not left enough for the viewer to follow (a failure, not a virtue).
- **Score vs. performance:** Sol LeWitt's wall drawing instructions are scores; each installation is a performance. The artist authors the conditions; the world authors the instance.
- **Material codes:** Materials arrive pre-coded — they carry existing myths and cultural histories. Using a material without auditing its codes means inheriting them silently.

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Xu Bing** — *Book from the Sky* (1987–91) — invented Chinese characters that look fully authentic but cannot be read; makes the code fail, exposing the system by refusing it
- **Cindy Sherman** — *Untitled Film Stills* (1977–80) — photographs that feel like movie stills; exposes the image scripts through which femininity is made culturally readable; myth made visible by repetition with slight displacement
- **Adrian Piper** — *My Calling Card No. 1* (1986–90) — printed cards handed to individuals who made racist remarks in Piper's presence; encoding is fixed; decoding exposes the receiver's social position; reception staged as the site of meaning
- **Maya Lin** — Vietnam Veterans Memorial (1982) — guided openness: strong formal structure (black granite, chronological names, reflective surface) creates conditions for grief without prescribing its form
- **Lygia Clark** — *Bichos* (1960–63) — hinged aluminum sculptures designed to be handled and reconfigured; open structure with defined limits; meaning completed through the viewer's body
- **Sol LeWitt** — Wall drawing instructions — score-based practice; artist writes conditions, others execute; the instruction is the work, each installation a performance
- **Maurizio Cattelan** — *All* (Guggenheim, 2011) — entire career's sculpture suspended in atrium; meaning assembled as viewer moves through the space; no single vantage point delivers the full work
- **Dora Salcedo** — shoe niches (1992–96) — shoes inserted into wall niches; viewer completes through memory and knowledge of disappeared women in Colombia; underspecified surface, overspecified resonance
- **Hélio Oiticica** — *Parangolés* (1964–79) — wearable capes completed only through dance; the work exists in activation, not display
- **Nick Cave** — Sound Suits — wearable sculptures that mean through performance and movement; connect to Parangolé logic of activation

## Core Takeaways

- Meaning is not transmitted from artist to viewer like a signal; it is produced at the intersection of encoded work and socially positioned decoder. The audience is not a passive receiver.
- Every material, form, and context arrives pre-coded. Auditing your materials' codes, myths, and histories is part of the conceptual work of making.
- Myth turns history into nature. The artist's relationship to myth — using it, exposing it, interrupting it, or repeating it unconsciously — is a meaningful choice.
- Openness is a formal property, not an absence of intention. The best open works are the most carefully structured: they build conditions for meaning rather than prescribing it.
- "Underbuilt" is a failure mode as real as "over-controlled." The viewer needs enough to follow.
- The studio questions shift: not "what does this mean?" but "what code am I working in, who has access to it, what myth is already present, who is my decoder and from what position, and how open is this structure?"

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. What code does your current work operate in? Who shares that code, and who is excluded? Is that exclusion intentional?
2. What myth is already present in your materials, format, or subject before you begin? Are you using it, exposing it, interrupting it, or inheriting it by default?
3. Who is your likely decoder, and from what social position will they encounter the work? Is there a dominant reading you are encoding and a population for whom that reading will be negotiated or oppositional?
4. On Eco's spectrum from closed to open, where does your current work sit? Is that placement intentional — and if the work is open, is it structured enough for the viewer to follow?
5. Is there a version of your practice in which the gap between your encoding and the audience's decoding is not a failure but the site of the work?
