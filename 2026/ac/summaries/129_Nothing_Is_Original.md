# 129 · Nothing Is Original: Copying and Appropriation in Art

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Professor Cheyenne Rudolph
**Topic:** Originality as myth; copying, appropriation, and remix as central creative practices throughout art history and contemporary practice

---

## Overview

Cheyenne Rudolph opens with a provocation — "nothing is original" — and then proceeds to show why this is liberating rather than deflating. The lecture traces a long lineage of artists learning through copying (Michelangelo drawing from Masaccio, Cézanne drawing from Michelangelo, Rubens building a personal library of thousands of copies during eight years in Italy) to contemporary artists who make appropriation itself the subject and substance of their work. The key distinction is not between copying and not copying — all art copies — but between copying as passive imitation and copying as active transformation with intentional meaning.

The lecture surveys artists across a wide range, from Kehinde Wiley's New Master Portraits (substituting Black men for European rulers in exact compositional formats) to Yasumasa Morimura's photographic self-insertions into Western canonical works, to Elaine Sturtevant's practice of making near-identical copies of other artists' works as her medium, to the Guerrilla Girls' data-driven appropriation of Ingres's *Le Grand Odalisque*, to the Carters' video *Apeshit* as an embodied intervention into the Louvre — itself a direct heir to Faith Ringgold's 1991 story quilt imagining Black bodies dancing in that same space. Throughout, Rudolph frames these not as derivative acts but as acts of insertion, critique, and reclamation: who belongs in art history, who gets represented, who tells the story.

The legal dimension emerges through the Richard Prince / Patrick Cariou *Canal Zone* case and the Shepard Fairey / Associated Press *Hope poster* case. Rudolph introduces the fair use doctrine's four factors and notes that the U.S. legal system has been consistently unable to definitively answer when a work is transformative enough to be considered new. The lecture ends with Kirby Ferguson's "Everything Is a Remix" framework — copy, transform, combine — as both a description of all creativity and a permission structure for artists paralyzed by the myth of originality.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Originality as myth (Rudolph's thesis):** Originality is a construct built around individualism and Western notions of the solitary genius. All art comes from something. The goal is not originality but authenticity and intentionality.
- **Guild and apprenticeship model:** Pre-modern artists learned by copying masters directly. This embodied learning — inhabiting technique in the body — is different from visual research alone.
- **Appropriation art (MoMA definition):** The intentional borrowing, copying, and alteration of pre-existing images and objects. Transformation and context-shift are what distinguish appropriation from mere copying.
- **Fair use doctrine (four factors):** (1) Who is using it — nonprofit/educational vs. commercial; (2) What is being used — creative work is more protected; (3) How much is used — use only what is necessary; (4) How the use affects the original — does it substitute for or diminish the market of the original?
- **Transformativeness:** The central unanswered legal question: how different does a work need to be from its source to constitute a new creative work?
- **Copy, transform, combine (Kirby Ferguson):** The three operations of all creativity and remix culture. Applicable from TikTok duets to canonical art history.

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Beyoncé and Jay-Z (The Carters)** — *Apeshit* (2018, Louvre) — embodied intervention in Western art space; cited as heir to Faith Ringgold's 1991 quilt
- **Faith Ringgold** — *Dancing at the Louvre* (1991, story quilt series) — imagined Black bodies in the Louvre; precursor to Apeshit; also *American People Series: Die* — composition based on Picasso's *Guernica*
- **Michelangelo** — *Dying Slave* (1513) — copied by Cézanne; also copied Masaccio
- **Paul Cézanne** — drawing from Michelangelo's *Dying Slave* — demonstrates lineage of learning through copying
- **Peter Paul Rubens** — built a library of thousands of copies during 8 years in Italy; used these throughout his career
- **Kehinde Wiley** — *Ice-T* (2005) — after Ingres's *Napoleon on his Imperial Throne* (1806); same composition, Black subject in place of Napoleon
- **Yasumasa Morimura** — self-portrait series copying Van Gogh, Dürer, Manet's *Olympia*, and others — inserts a Japanese male body into Western canonical works
- **Édouard Manet** — *Olympia* (1863) — cited as a copy/transformation of Titian's *Venus of Urbino* (1538)
- **Elaine Sturtevant** — near-exact copies of Warhol silkscreened flowers, Duchamp's *Fountain*, Jasper Johns's Flag — copying as the medium itself; questions authorship and technique
- **Judy Chicago** — *The Dinner Party* (1974–79) — uses Georgia O'Keeffe's flower paintings as source for place settings; critiques erasure of women from art history
- **Guerrilla Girls** — *Do Women Have to Be Naked to Get into the Met Museum?* (1989, 2005, 2012) — appropriates Ingres's *Le Grand Odalisque* with gorilla mask
- **Richard Prince** — *Untitled Cowboy* series; *Canal Zone* — collaged Cariou's *Yes Rasta* photos without permission; settled out of court
- **Shepard Fairey** — *Hope* poster (2008) — appropriated AP photographer's photo of Obama; settled out of court

## Core Takeaways

- Originality is a construct. All art comes from prior art. The question is not whether you are copying but how you are transforming and what meaning the transformation produces.
- Copying with your hands — not just looking — builds embodied knowledge of technique. This is what the guild and apprenticeship model understood.
- Appropriation is most powerful when it inserts absent perspectives into spaces from which they have been excluded, or when it uses a dominant form to say something the form was never designed to say.
- Fair use is legally unsettled in the U.S. The safest approach: make it highly transformative, use only what is necessary, and ensure your use does not substitute for or economically damage the original.
- Permission to not be original is permission to work. "Copy, transform, combine" is a complete creative method.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. What are the hidden or unacknowledged influences in your current work? Where did your subject matter, aesthetic choices, or working methods actually come from?
2. Is there a format, composition, or historical genre you could appropriate to say something it was never designed to say?
3. Have you tried copying a work you admire — not as plagiarism but as embodied learning? What did you discover in the body that you could not see with your eyes?
4. How are you transforming your sources? Is the transformation strong enough that the work stands on its own as a new object with its own meaning?
5. Who is absent from the art historical lineages you work within? What would it mean to insert that absence into the center of the frame?
