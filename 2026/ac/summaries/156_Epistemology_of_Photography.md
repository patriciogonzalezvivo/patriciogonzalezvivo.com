# 156 · Epistemology of Photography

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Dr. Elliot Krasnopoler (PhD, Bryn Mawr College — dissertation *What the Earth Remembers: Landscape and Materiality in Contemporary Art*; BFA, Rochester Institute of Technology, Photography; MA, History of Art, Williams College; editor, *Breaking the Silence: Methods of Writing Art History*; 2021–2022 George Gurney Postdoctoral Fellow, Smithsonian American Art Museum; staff at Smarthistory.org)
**Topic:** A chronological art-history lecture on how photographs have worked as evidence and generated knowledge, from Talbot's 1839 invention through scientific, state, documentary, and now AI-generated images — asking, at each stage, *how do photographs give us information, and how has that changed?*

---

## Overview

Krasnopoler opens by defining epistemology — the branch of philosophy examining the nature, origin, and limits of knowledge — and reframes the lecture's question as: *how do photographs work to give us information, work as evidence, and how has that changed over photography's history?* The talk is not a comprehensive history but a chronological through-line across five sections: (1) photography's earliest promises (Talbot), (2) photography beyond human sight (science), (3) images as instruments of the state (criminality and control), (4) documentary and manipulation, and (5) the present rupture of generative AI.

The throughline across all five sections is the concept of the **index** — the semiotic idea (from Peirce, via [[127_Semiotics|semiotics]]) that a sign relates causally and directly to what it depicts, like a footprint in sand. Talbot understood, at photography's very invention, that a photograph is "drawn by light" rather than by hand, that it captures details the maker never intended, and that this makes it "evidence of a novel kind." Every subsequent section shows a different institution — science, the state, documentary rhetoric, and now the machine-learning dataset — building on and exploiting that indexical trust. Krasnopoler's closing argument, delivered partly through Fred Ritchen's 1990 quote, is that generative AI is the first real threat to make that trust "tenuous," forcing a full reconsideration of photography's vocabulary — and that we are living through that reconsideration now, without yet having the language for it.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Epistemology of photography** — not "what is a photograph" but "how does a photograph produce knowledge, and how has the trust we place in it as evidence shifted across 185 years?"
- **The index (Peirce, via semiotics)** — a sign that relates directly and causally to the world (footprint, shadow, photograph). Talbot's own language — "drawn its own picture," "drawn by light" — is an intuitive grasp of indexicality before the term existed. Photographs are indexical (they relate to the world) *and* iconic (they resemble the world) at once.
- **Capturing beyond intention** — from photography's first plates, the camera recorded details (a clock's time, a placard) the photographer never noticed or intended. This decouples the image from a single controlling author in a way painting and drawing never were, and reframes agency and authorship.
- **Photography beyond human sight** — the camera doesn't just record what the eye sees; it extends vision into the too-small (photomicrographs), the too-far (astrophotography), the too-fast (chronophotography, stop-motion), and the invisible (X-rays). Each extension "changes how we see and imagine the world," not just what images can show.
- **Rodin's objection** — "It is the artist who is truthful, and it is the photograph... that lies. For in reality, time does not stop." A live 19th-century argument about which medium held the more real truth: the eye's synthesis (painting) or the camera's frozen instant (photography) — a debate that recurs, restructured, in every later section.
- **Baudelaire's exclusion of photography from art** — "Let photography enrich the traveler's album... let it be the secretary and record-keeper of whomsoever needs absolute material accuracy for professional reasons" — i.e., let it remain a scientific instrument, not art. An argument that was live and unsettled in the 1880s, later lost.
- **The archive/filing cabinet as the real apparatus of state photography (Allan Sekula)** — "The central artifact of this system is not the camera, but the filing cabinet." Bertillon's and Galton's innovation wasn't a new *kind* of photograph but a new *system*: text, measurement, and archive layered onto the image, turning individual photos into collective, comparative knowledge used for control, not just information.
- **Composite/averaged portraiture as false authority (Francis Galton)** — overlaying many faces to "reveal" shared traits (criminality, illness) lent photography's aura of objectivity to eugenicist pseudoscience; even Galton's own source-selection was biased. A caution about how indexical trust can be laundered into authority for a predetermined conclusion — directly relevant to how AI training-data bias gets naturalized as "what the data shows."
- **Documentary as historical, not ontological, term** — "documentary photography" was coined and championed (Riis, Hine) in service of social reform; it is not inherent to what photography *is*. It always already involved selection, editing, and manipulation while purporting not to.
- **Meliorism and its fallacy (Martha Rosler)** — the early documentary hope that simply *showing* a wrong would fix it "did not perceive those wrongs as fundamental to the social system that tolerated them... but bred them" — a basic fallacy of social-work photography that assumed visibility alone produces justice.
- **Delusory presentness (Abigail Solomon-Godeau, via Victor Burgin)** — a photograph effaces its own making; the viewer's eye, the camera's eye, and the photographer's eye collapse into one flattened point of view, conferring "pure but delusory presentness." A photograph, per Burgin, is "an offer that cannot be refused" — we trust what we see even when the scene was staged, cropped, or selected.
- **Selection as manipulation** — Dorothea Lange chose one negative out of many for *Migrant Mother* and recorded a detail (sold tires) later found untrue; Walker Evans rearranged the Burroughs' broom and dishware for compositional beauty. Documentary "truth" was never free of the photographer's aesthetic and institutional aims (the FSA employed Lange to build support for FDR's programs).
- **Kodak's chemistry was trained on whiteness** — analog film emulsions were historically calibrated to render light skin accurately and dark skin poorly, prefiguring the same bias problem now visible in generative-AI datasets (Minne Atairu's *Cornrow Studies*).
- **Fred Ritchen's 1990 warning** — "Should photography's relationship to physical existence become suddenly tenuous, its vocabulary would be transformed and its system of representation would have to be reconsidered." Written before digital/generative imaging existed at scale; treated by Krasnopoler as the essay's real thesis statement.
- **The index under generative AI** — indexicality used to be simply *true*: an indexical-looking image was an indexical image. Now the index itself has to be argued for, verified, or debunked case by case (a crowd photo at a rally that turns out to be AI-generated). Krasnopoler: exciting for a photo historian, "not ideal" for society.
- **Medium as a promise, not a neutral label** — Krasnopoler refuses to call generative images "photographs": as an art historian, the wall label's medium line is the first thing he reads, and "photograph" signals a specific, trusted relationship to the world that a generated image hasn't earned. He currently defaults to "generative image" / "AI image."
- **Camera authentication / provenance standards** — industry efforts (mentioned in Q&A) to embed verifiable metadata — camera, date, GPS — at the point of capture, so a "chain of custody" for an image can be established against generative fakes.
- **"Post-photography"** — a term raised in Q&A that neither speaker commits to, marking how unsettled the vocabulary for this moment still is.

## Chronological Arc

**1. The earliest promises of photography (1839–)**
William Henry Fox Talbot, *The Pencil of Nature* (plates of a china cabinet, Queen's College Oxford with an accidental clock reading the time). Photography is "drawn by light," faster than a written inventory, capable of being "evidence of a novel kind" in a courtroom, and able to record what the photographer never noticed. Contemporary counterpoint: Victor Burgin's *Photopath* (1967) — photographs of a gallery floor printed at actual size and affixed to that same floor, literalizing and gently mocking photography's indexical promise.

**2. Photography beyond human sight**
Photomicrographs (Talbot's plant stem; Arthur Durham's flea; Wilson Bentley's snowflakes — which established that no two are alike). Astrophotography (John Adams Whipple's clock-driven telescope daguerreotype of the moon; Henry Draper's sharper 10-second exposure). Contemporary artists: Trevor Paglen (hidden satellites caught at the edge of beautiful sky photographs, surfacing military-industrial systems); Liz Deschenes (lensless tintypes exposed only to ambient moon and starlight overnight — an anti-sharpness counterpoint). Chronophotography and motion (Étienne-Jules Marey's soldier-motion studies for military efficiency, echoed in Duchamp's *Nude Descending a Staircase*; Eadweard Muybridge's fast-shutter horse-gallop studies for Leland Stanford, which settled a real scientific debate and provoked Rodin's objection). X-rays (Wilhelm Röntgen's 1886 image of his wife's hand — "I have seen my death" — and Rauschenberg's *Booster* lithograph). Baudelaire's argument that photography should stay a scientific "secretary," not become art.

**3. Images as instruments of the state**
Alphonse Bertillon's anthropometric mugshot system (Paris police, 1880s–90s) — the origin of the modern ID photo and the grid-of-faces as bureaucratic control (Sekula: "the central artifact... is not the camera, but the filing cabinet"). Francis Galton's composite/averaged portraiture in service of eugenics — photography lending false authority to pseudoscientific typing. Contemporary artists: Taryn Simon, *A Living Man Declared Dead and Other Chapters* — Bertillon-style clean grids and dry captions used to expose a real land-bribery injustice in India; Zanele Muholi, *Faces and Phases* — 300+ uniform portraits of queer South African women, using the mugshot's own grid aesthetic to hand identity and control back to the sitters rather than the state.

**4. Documentary and manipulation**
Jacob Riis, *How the Other Half Lives* (1890, invented flash photography to shoot Bowery slums, catalyzed housing-code reform). Lewis Hine's child-labor photographs (successfully changed labor law). Martha Rosler's critique of documentary's "meliorist" assumption. Dorothea Lange's *Migrant Mother* (FSA-commissioned; one selected negative out of many; a detail in her account later proven false; the subject received no aid). Walker Evans's Allie Mae Burroughs portraits and staged interior still lifes for *Let Us Now Praise Famous Men* (rearranged broom and dishware for compositional beauty, confirmed by Burroughs family testimony). Abigail Solomon-Godeau on documentary's "delusory presentness." Contemporary artists: Martha Rosler, *The Bowery in Two Inadequate Descriptive Systems* (drunkenness shown only through discarded bottles and text, refusing the documentary snapshot); Philip-Lorca diCorcia, *Hustler* series (male sex workers paid their going rate to pose, fully lit and staged, in a deliberate inversion of the documentary "caught" moment).

**5. The present rupture: generative images and AI**
Joan Fontcuberta's invented botanical/scientific specimens (*Herbarium*) as a decades-old precedent for subverting photography's promise of reference, pre-dating AI. *Aperture* magazine's issue on photography and AI (cover image, ironically, a real photograph by Trevor Paglen about facial recognition). Boris Eldagsen's Dall-E-generated image, which won and then was withdrawn from a 2022 Sony World Photography Award category, forcing the question of what counts as a photograph. Minne Atairu's *Cornrow Studies* — generative images that reveal dataset bias in rendering Black hair and skin, explicitly linked to the history of Kodak film's own racial bias. Fred Ritchen's 1990 quote on photography's vocabulary needing reconsideration once its relationship to physical existence becomes tenuous.

## Core Takeaways

- **Photography's trust as evidence was never neutral — it was built, section by section, by science, the state, and documentary rhetoric, each layering new authority onto the same indexical promise Talbot named in 1839.** Every institution that has used photography as knowledge has also used it, deliberately or not, as a tool of persuasion or control.
- **"Documentary" is a historical invention, not a natural category of photography.** It has always involved selection, staging, and editing while claiming transparency — the manipulation is not a modern corruption of an once-pure form.
- **Photography's promise to extend sight (micro, macro, fast, invisible) doesn't just show us more of the world — it changes what we imagine the world to be.** The extension of perception is itself the content, not just the vehicle.
- **Systems of images (archives, grids, datasets) generate a different, more powerful kind of knowledge than single images do** — and that power has historically served control (Bertillon, Galton) as often as insight, a pattern Sekula's "filing cabinet" names directly and that recurs unchanged in today's AI training datasets.
- **Generative AI is not the first challenge to photographic truth, but it is the first to threaten the index itself** — whether an image relates causally to the world at all is now a live, case-by-case question rather than a given, and the field has not yet built the vocabulary to describe what has replaced it.
- **Naming the medium is not neutral.** Calling a generated image a "photograph" claims a trust relationship it hasn't earned; the label itself carries an epistemological argument.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Where in your own instrument-based work (satellite feeds, astronomical data, plotter drawings, generative shaders) does the *index* live — is there a genuine causal trace back to something in the world, or has that trace already been abstracted, averaged, or synthesized away before you touch it?
2. When a machine first "sees" or renders a face or a landscape before your hand completes it (Hybrids, Memories, Santos), is that machine step closer to Talbot's "drawn by light" — a neutral trace — or closer to Bertillon's grid and Galton's composite — a system already shaping what can be seen and how it will be judged?
3. In work that restores what a sensor or instrument stripped away (HOGAR's atmospheric scattering, ESTRELLAS's stars, Weaver's/Astros's celestial maps), are you extending human sight in Krasnopoler's sense — and if so, what new thing does that restored image ask viewers to imagine about the world, beyond simply showing them more of it?
4. Where does your work make a documentary-style claim to evidence or truth (Efecto Mariposa's ecological consequence, HOGAR's home/planet framing) — and have you named, the way Rosler and Solomon-Godeau insist on naming, the selection and staging built into that claim, or does the work let the image's "delusory presentness" go unexamined?
5. For any AI-generated or code-generated image in your practice (BLINK, PixelSpirit, shader work), what word are you actually using for it, and what trust does that word claim? Following Krasnopoler's own discipline — refusing to call a generated image a "photograph" — is there a term you use, deliberately or by default, that claims more indexical trust than the image has earned?
6. Fontcuberta's *Herbarium* and Eldagsen's contest image both stage a confrontation between an image's polish and its actual relationship (or non-relationship) to the world. Does your work ever let a viewer believe an image is more indexical than it is — and is that ambiguity productive (a "two systems on one surface" tension, per your practice's throughline) or does it need to be disclosed?
