# 160 · Business of Art: Pricing and Editioning (Part 2 — Editioning)

**Program:** Artist Commons

**Topic:** The second half of the pricing-and-editioning lecture. Part 1 ([[153_Pricing_And_Editioning_Part1|Lesson 153]]) covered pricing mechanics; Part 2 covers **editioning** — the vocabulary of unique works, limited editions, and open editions; reproductions and posters; artist proofs and other proof types; the legal weight of an edition promise; documentation and certificates of authenticity; production strategy (all-at-once vs. batched); damage and replacement policy; and a closing case study on Cindy Sherman's Legacy Project.

## Overview

Haiver opens by tying the two lectures together with a supply-and-demand analogy: a can of Coke is cheap because billions exist; a Twinkie became valuable the moment production was rumored to stop. **Price is tied to supply, and for art, supply is set by how you edition your work.** Some mediums only ever produce unique objects (painting, drawing, collage, most ceramics, many forms of sculpture); others make it possible for the same image or object to exist as multiple authorized examples (photography, printmaking, cast sculpture, artist books, audio, video, digital work). The moment an artist decides more than one authorized copy can exist, they have entered editioning — and taken on a new responsibility.

The lecture's central definition: **an edition is a predetermined number of authorized copies, and "authorized" is doing the real work.** A collector buying edition 3 of 10 is buying two things — the object, and the artist's *promise* that the edition stays capped at the structure set in advance. That promise cannot be revised upward after the fact ("these sold really well, I'll just make 20 more") without breaking the collector's trust and, in some jurisdictions (Haiver cites New York's Arts and Cultural Affairs Law), the artist's legal warranty. Editioning is, at bottom, **supply management with a legal and ethical dimension** — and it has to be decided *before* the first copy is marketed, not discovered afterward.

The back half of the lecture moves through the full practical vocabulary a serious edition-based practice needs (proof types, the bon à tirer, certificates of authenticity, replacement policy) and closes on Cindy Sherman's Legacy Project as a case study in what happens when a decades-old edition's physical materials begin to age — and who gets to decide what "the artwork" actually is once the artist is no longer the one making that call.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Price is tied to supply, and supply is set by editioning.** The Coke-can / Twinkie-shortage analogy: scarcity (real or engineered) raises value; abundance keeps it low. For art, the artist controls that supply lever directly through edition structure.
- **An edition is a promise, not just an object.** "Authorized" means the collector is buying both the physical work and the artist's commitment that the total population will never exceed the stated structure. This is the ethical (and in places legal) core of the whole lecture.
- **You must set the edition structure before the first sale — and never revise it upward.** Changing the cap after collectors have already bought in retroactively devalues what they purchased and breaks the transaction's foundation.
- **Unique work / limited edition / open edition — the three base categories.** *Unique work*: one authorized object (the Mona Lisa). *Limited edition*: a predetermined maximum, capped in advance. *Open edition*: no predetermined maximum; can continue as long as there's demand. None is inherently better — the question is whether the artist is *choosing* the category deliberately, and whether collectors are told the truth about it.
- **Medium-specific vocabulary artists should know even outside their own medium:** *monotype* (one unique impression from a printmaking process), *monoprint* (a repeatable process where each impression varies), *photographic edition* (often with additional editions at different sizes), *edition sculpture* (multiple authorized casts/fabrications), *reproduction* (a printed copy of an artwork originally made in another medium).
- **Reproduction is not a dirty word.** A reproduction's defining trait is its *relationship to a source image* (it documents a unique work), not its quality — a reproduction can be museum-grade archival pigment print, artist-supervised, color-matched, signed, numbered, and accompanied by a certificate of authenticity. Reproduction describes a relationship, not a tier of seriousness. (Counter-case: Picasso's silly Antibes lithographs — "nonsense works," small edition, but authentic, beautiful, and valuable *as lithographs*, separate from the originals gifted to the city.)
- **Posters are not a dirty word either — but they must be visually distinct from the limited edition they're derived from.** The actual problem is a $100 poster that looks identical to a $1,000 limited edition, collapsing the reason to pay the difference. Fix through paper choice, dimensions, printing process, white space, and text (title/venue/date printed in the margin) — decisions that make a poster still feel like a poster even if someone tried to crop and frame it as "the original."
- **Signing and numbering document the object; they do not create value.** A signed, numbered $20 poster is still a $20 poster. Signature = the artist's authorization/participation. Number = the promised edition size. Neither, alone or together, establishes artistic or financial value — that comes from everything else (materials, process, market position, scarcity that's actually real).
- **Total edition population, not just the number on the print.** "3/10" tells you about *this size/format* only — it says nothing about whether other sizes or formats of the same image exist. Editioning in multiple sizes (e.g., 3 large + 12 medium + 25 small) means the *true* population is the sum across all sizes — 40, in that example — and that full population, not any single size's count, is what a collector is actually buying scarcity against. **Once the total population is set across all formats, it cannot be expanded later** (adding a "new" size years later to double-dip on higher prices is explicitly called out as breaking the ethical contract with earlier collectors).
- **Edition-size decision factors** (there is no universally correct size): realistic sales likelihood; size of your actual collector base; whether scarcity matters in your intended market; production time and cost per piece (20 days/piece × 100 editions = ~5.5 years of only that one edition); consistency of the reproduction process over a long print run; storage/inventory burden; whether the piece is an accessible entry point or a major work; anticipated future demand. **A large inventory does not create a large market** — an edition of 300 that's only sold 20 after two years signals *weak* demand to a savvy collector, which can be worse than a smaller edition that's harder to get.
- **The trade-off between small and large editions.** Smaller editions: stronger scarcity, support higher pricing, sell out faster, less future inventory burden — but cap upside if demand later grows. Larger editions: more sales opportunities, usually require lower pricing, serve more collectors, but require more fulfillment time and create longer inventory obligations. You cannot retroactively decide "that edition of 3 should have been 20" once demand grows — the promise was already made.
- **Numbering conventions:** "3/10" = edition number 3 of a numbered run of 10 (says nothing about print order — with contemporary printing, the whole edition may be produced together and numbered after; low numbers are not inherently "earlier" or "better," a persistent but mistaken collector belief). "7+2 AP" = 7 numbered editions plus 2 artist proofs = 9 total authorized examples in that format/size.
- **The full proof vocabulary:**
  - **Artist Proof (AP)** — historically a test/approval proof from printmaking with slight variation from the final run; today, with inkjet/digital processes, often physically identical to the numbered edition, but the designation is retained by convention. Uses: keep for yourself, exhibit (especially once the numbered edition has all sold and left your hands), lend to museums/curators/researchers, donate, gift, reserve for your estate, or eventually sell (often decades later, at appreciated value, as revenue for you or your heirs).
  - **Printer's Proof (PP)** — a complementary proof given to the printer/collaborator.
  - **Bon à tirer (BAT)** — French, "good to pull": the artist-approved master reference impression that every subsequent print in the edition is checked against, critical when a run spans years, uses multiple inks/plates, or moves through different hands or printers.
  - **Hors commerce (HC)** — "outside commerce": never for sale, used for exhibition/archival purposes only (e.g., an oversized exhibition-only print made from a small original when a curator needs a different scale for a space).
  - **Trial proof** — an experimental impression made while still working out color, paper, ink, or pressure on the way to the bon à tirer; usually stays archival, rarely enters the market until late in an artist's life or estate.
  - **State proof** — documents a specific stage in a multi-plate printing process (which plates/layers have been laid down so far); more relevant to traditional plate-based printmaking.
  - **Cancellation proof** — a print pulled *after* the plates/matrices have been deliberately marred/altered/destroyed, proving the edition is truly closed and can't be secretly extended.
- **Proof count discipline.** APs must be set at the same time as the total edition size — never added after the numbered edition has begun selling or sold out (that would be an unethical, undisclosed expansion of supply). Rule of thumb: roughly ≤10% of the numbered edition size for editions of 10+ (though single-digit editions commonly run proportionally higher, e.g. 1 AP on an edition of 3, 2 APs on an edition of 7). **Do not sell APs concurrently with the numbered edition** — it functions as a hidden expansion of supply the collector didn't agree to.
- **Certificates of Authenticity (COA) must be information-rich to be useful.** A COA that just says "this is authentic, signed by me" proves nothing. A useful COA documents: artist's name, title, date, medium/materials/process, dimensions, edition number and total edition size, proof type and count, and signature — cross-referenceable against the artist's own private records so discrepancies can be caught later (by the artist, an estate, or a museum).
- **Provenance adds meaning but not automatic value — and only works when documented.** A work owned for decades by its own sitter, or an artist's proof that hung in the studio, can carry real meaning for the right collector (Haiver's own story: buying art from the Joan Didion estate sale specifically *because* it had belonged to her) — but provenance claims require actual records: correspondence, transfer documents/bill of sale (even at $0), dates, exhibition/loan history, and any resale restrictions. **Memory is not provenance.** Haiver cites his own research into 1930s–40s African American exhibitions in DC where critically important works are now simply lost because no one tracked where they went — a cautionary tale for keeping records now, for both the artist's career and art history's sake.
- **All-at-once vs. batched production — the central operational choice.** Producing an entire edition at once guarantees consistent calibration, paper, ink, and hand/machine marks; lets everything be signed and numbered together; and protects against materials, printers, or shops disappearing or changing formulation over the years (Haiver's examples: paint formulations changing, printer/ink lines discontinuing, a favorite print shop closing). It also creates immediate inventory for studio visits. Costs: large upfront production spend, storage/insurance/inventory-management burden, and cash tied up in unsold stock. **Batched/hybrid production** requires disciplined documentation (recording the exact printer model and color profile, purchasing and setting aside dedicated extra paper/ink stock, maintaining a bon à tirer reference, and keeping steady access to the same equipment/shop) to guarantee later batches still match the first.
- **Destroying the plates/matrix is now considered a historical practice, not a requirement.** Historically, printmakers physically defaced plates/stones/blocks/screens to prove an edition was closed (followed by a cancellation proof). Today, Haiver argues for **preserving production files/plates under tightly controlled-use policy** instead of destroying them — for legitimate replacement of damaged work, future conservation research, and archival/estate purposes — with the safeguard being professional record-keeping and clear usage policy, not physical destruction.
- **Editioning carries real legal weight.** Marketing a limited edition makes representations — about size, proof count, authorization, process, format, and prior editions of the same image — that a collector's purchase decision relies on. In jurisdictions like New York (Arts and Cultural Affairs Law, cited specifically), a stated edition size functions as an actual **warranty**; breaking it can mean legal liability for misrepresentation, not just reputational damage.
- **Collector care policy.** Beyond the COA, give collectors written handling, display (light exposure), climate/humidity, framing, installation, and cleaning guidance, plus an explicit, upfront statement of your replacement policy (not a guarantee) — this reduces damage risk and gives you a documented process to point to when an issue does arise.
- **Damage and replacement policy must be decided in advance, not improvised.** Options when a piece is damaged: conservation (stabilize without substantially altering), restoration (return closer to original appearance), replacement (produce a new authorized impression to stand in), or destruction (surrender/destroy the damaged piece). A sound replacement policy prevents two objects sharing the same edition number from existing simultaneously: inspect the damaged work, decide on conservation first, document/destroy the original, produce the replacement (ideally under the artist's supervision, or a defined process for when they can't be), reissue the *same* edition number with a new COA that explicitly names it a replacement, and settle who covers production/shipping/admin costs. **A replacement should never increase the edition size** — it stands in for, and requires surrendering, the original.

## Case Study: The Cindy Sherman Legacy Project

Sherman's *Untitled Film Stills* prints (1970s gelatin silver, later chromogenic color) are now 50+ years old; black-and-white prints yellow and lose highlight brightness, and chromogenic color prints age unevenly because different color dyes degrade at different rates. Rather than leave this to chance or a future curator, Sherman's estate/studio built a formal **Legacy Project**: a collector submits an application with provenance and condition documentation and a non-refundable $1,000 fee; if accepted, pays an additional $9,000 in administrative fees plus production/shipping, surrenders the original for in-person condition assessment, and receives a modern archival-inkjet replacement (rated for hundreds of years vs. the original process's ~50) — after which the **original is destroyed**, preventing two versions of the same edition number from circulating.

Haiver frames this as treating replacement as a **professional service with real labor and administration behind it**, not a favor owed to a disappointed collector — a model other artists with long careers and aging archival material could learn from. But he also surfaces the real ethical tension it raises and leaves open rather than resolving:
- What is lost when the original material object — with its own history — is destroyed, versus preserved as a historical artifact in its own right (even faded)?
- Is a replacement made 50 years later, under an estate's authority rather than the living artist's, "fundamentally the same artwork," and should it be labeled as a replacement if so?
- Who has the authority to authorize replacements after the artist's death, and should an estate hold the same authority the living artist did?
- Does the steep cost ($10,000 all-in) effectively restrict this kind of legacy preservation to wealthy collectors and institutions?

- **Greg Miller** (visiting AC artist, referenced from an earlier session) — Haiver's own open-edition print purchase, bought directly from the artist's website at a lower, non-scarcity-driven price, used to illustrate that not every collector is chasing scarcity.
- **Dorothea Lange** — *Migrant Mother* negative-roll archive; Haiver's anecdote about a 20th-century photo editor hole-punching directly through negative rolls, used as a cautionary image about physically marring source material.
- **Joan Didion (estate)** — Haiver's own purchase from her estate sale, used to illustrate how documented provenance (owned by a specific, meaningful person) adds value beyond the object itself.
- **Cindy Sherman** — *Untitled Film Stills* (1970s–80s, gelatin silver and chromogenic color); the Legacy Project as the lecture's closing case study (also connects to [[156_Staged_Narrative_Photography|Lesson 156's]] treatment of Sherman's *Untitled Film Stills* as an early staged-narrative work).

## Core Takeaways

- **An edition is a promise about the future, not just a physical object.** The artist is responsible for maintaining the integrity of the *entire* edition connected to any one collector's piece, for as long as that edition (and the artist's reputation) exists.
- **Set the total population — across every size and format — before the first sale, and never expand it.** This is the single non-negotiable rule threading through pricing, proofs, reproductions, and legal exposure alike.
- **Signing and numbering document; they do not create value.** Value comes from real scarcity, real materials, real market position — never from the ritual of a signature and a fraction.
- **Reproductions and posters are legitimate art objects, not admissions of lesser seriousness — but only if they are visibly, deliberately distinct from the limited edition or unique work they relate to.** Confusable pricing tiers, not the existence of a reproduction, is the actual failure mode.
- **Documentation is the entire game.** COAs, edition records, provenance files, and a bon à tirer reference are what let a promise made today still be verifiable, defensible, and honorable decades from now — including after the artist is no longer the one answering questions about it.
- **Decide your replacement and estate policy before you need it.** Who can authorize a replacement, under what process, using what source materials, and at what cost — these are studio-practice decisions now, not just estate-planning decisions for later.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Across your instrument- and code-based work (plotter drawings, satellite/astronomical prints, generative or shader-driven images, on-chain pieces), which are genuinely unique objects, which are reproducible, and for the reproducible ones — have you actually set and disclosed a total population size across every format and scale, or is that decision still implicit?
2. If a work exists at multiple sizes (a large plotter drawing and smaller study prints of the same image, for instance), what is the *true* combined population across all of them — and does your current pricing and marketing let a collector see that full number, or only the count for the size in front of them?
3. Do you currently hold back artist proofs from any body of work? If not, what would a deliberate AP policy (roughly ≤10%, set at the same time as the edition, reserved for exhibition loans, gifts to collaborators, or your own estate) look like for your strongest series?
4. For any work involving a printer, fabricator, or collaborator (plotter output, cast elements, printed photographic or satellite-derived pieces), do you have a bon à tirer-equivalent reference and documented process — model of printer/plotter, ink or pen, paper stock — so a later batch could actually be verified to match the first?
5. If a piece from an edition were damaged today, do you have an actual decided policy (conservation vs. replacement vs. destruction, who bears the cost, how the replacement is documented and linked back to the original), or would you be improvising a precedent in the moment — one that would then bind every future case?
