# 109 · How to Crit Better (Cohort Feedback Sessions)

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Haiver (program mentor/instructor)
**Topic:** Structured peer feedback methodology for artists

---

## Overview

This lecture introduces the Artist Commons program's eight-step cohort feedback framework — a structured alternative to the traditional "crit" model. The instructor opens by acknowledging that bad critiques are common: they leave artists confused, stung by careless language, and without actionable next steps. To counter this, the program replaced the word "crit" entirely with "cohort feedback sessions," foregrounding trust, multiplicity of perspective, and the artist's ownership of the process.

The framework is organized around three broad phases: a **looking phase** (neutral observation before interpretation), a **meaning phase** (hearing what the work communicates before the artist explains intent), and a **strength phase** (constructive suggestions and commitments). Throughout, the lecture stresses specific, evidence-backed language framed in "I" statements, and the deliberate avoidance of certainty language ("obviously," "everyone will," "you should").

A central argument of the session is that artists are legitimate critical thinkers — criticism, Haiver argues, is simply a form of thinking about a work, something every human does when they walk into a gallery. The session closes with a reminder that the goal of feedback is not perfect consensus or a defended position, but two concrete explorations the artist commits to trying before the next session.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Four Core Agreements** — Assume goodwill; multiple readings coexist; be specific; maintain confidentiality.
- **Eight-Step Feedback Framework** — (1) Set up the artwork, (2) Silent look, (3) Clarifying factual questions, (4) What's working and why, (5) What is this about?, (6) Artist-led: effect sentence + context + key questions, (7) What could be stronger, (8) Artist reflection and commitments.
- **I-Statement Language** — "I notice," "I'm curious about," "I'm confused by" — frames feedback as personal perspective, not objective truth.
- **Evidence-Backed Feedback** — Every observation is anchored to something visible or audible in the work.
- **Effect Sentence** — A single sentence naming what the artist wants the viewer to experience, understand, or feel.
- **Key Questions** — Two to three artist-generated questions, each singular, specific, viewer-answerable, and decision-linked.
- **Explorations/Commitments** — Two concrete, measurable experiments the artist commits to before the next feedback session.
- **Certainty Language** — Words to avoid: "obviously," "always," "never," "everyone will," "you should."
- **Artist Rights** — The presenting artist can ask for what they need, pivot, ignore feedback without debate, and pause the session at any time.
- **Observation / Effect / Evidence Formula** — "I notice X — it made me Y — because Z."

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Agnes Martin** — abstract paintings with closely related tonal values — cited as an example when discussing a "quiet and restrained" palette, used to illustrate a "what could be stronger" feedback scenario.
- **Ben Davis** (art critic, Artnet) — cited as an example of a critic (philosophy background) who practices the same kind of close looking/thinking that artists do.

## Core Takeaways

- A portfolio is completed by the viewer: the effect on the viewer, not the artist's experience of making, is what feedback surfaces.
- Specificity always beats vagueness — anchor every comment in observable evidence.
- The first two-thirds of a feedback session should be purely observational and interpretive; constructive advice only arrives after deep looking.
- Self-deprecating framing ("this isn't ready," "I kind of hate this piece") sabotages neutral feedback by redirecting the group toward reassurance.
- The artist never needs to defend the work or respond to every comment; "noted" is a complete answer.
- The session ends in action, not consensus — two concrete explorations forward the practice.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. When you share work for feedback, do you front-load explanation or apology? What would happen if you let the work speak first?
2. What is your effect sentence for your current project — the one-sentence description of what you want a viewer to experience?
3. Which two to three pivotal decisions in your current work do you most need outside eyes on?
4. After your last feedback session, what did you actually do with the notes? Were your commitments specific and measurable?
5. Do you tend to over-explain when your work feels misunderstood, or can you sit with the gap between intent and reception?
