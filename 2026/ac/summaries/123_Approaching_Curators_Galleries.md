**Program:** Artist Commons

## Overview

This lecture addresses the question artists most frequently raise in mentorship meetings: how do I get a solo show or gallery representation? The core argument is that professional success in the art world is built through community and genuine relationship, not networking transactions. Warm connections — people who know your work and your context — consistently outperform cold outreach, and the highest-success form of outreach is a warm introduction written by a mutual connection. Every strategy in the lecture flows from this premise.

The middle section moves through the mechanics of intentional outreach: researching the right targets (the "dream 15–20" list, inspired by Chet Holmes' dream 100 concept), understanding the functional difference between curators (who organize exhibitions around ideas and write art history) and gallerists/dealers (who build markets and manage inventory), and preparing a coherent packet of materials — website, bio, statement, 8–15 strong images, image list, CV, and a well-crafted introductory email. The anatomy of a strong email is broken down in detail, with emphasis on specificity, appropriate ask size, and sounding human rather than generic.

The final section covers studio visits — what different visitors are actually looking for, how to prepare and sequence the experience, physical logistics, what not to do, and how to conduct a post-visit reflection for ongoing improvement. The lecture closes with a realistic framework for assessing readiness for outreach, identifying red flags in galleries, and understanding that professional development in art is a slow, non-linear, human process of becoming known and seen.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Warm vs. cold outreach** — warm connections dramatically outperform cold contact; trust is the underlying currency
- **Warm introduction template** — point of reference + artist's name (repeated) + specificity to the recipient's interests + concrete description of the work + multiple off-ramps for the recipient
- **Dream 15–20 list** (from Chet Holmes' "dream 100") — identify the most aligned galleries/curators through deep research; specificity dramatically raises success rate
- **Curator vs. gallerist/dealer distinction** — curators organize shows around themes and research; gallerists build markets, manage rosters, attend fairs, and focus on sales; they ask different questions in a studio visit
- **The goal is the next conversation** (Silicon Valley pitch logic) — don't try to close the deal in a single email; pique curiosity enough that they want to continue
- **Stand-up comedian model** — workshop your introductory sentences the way a comedian workshops material: test them on real people, iterate
- **Outreach tracker** — maintain a systematic record of contacts, dates, context, responses, and follow-up points
- **Studio visit sequencing** — know your audience (curator vs. dealer); plan a beginning, middle, and end; leave something out for post-visit follow-up
- **Post-visit reflection** — systematically review: what questions kept arising? what resonated? what felt muddy? every studio visit is a learning opportunity

## Core Takeaways

- It's not networking, it's community: the most durable professional relationships in the art world are built on genuine curiosity and repeated presence.
- Warm is always better than cold; a mutual introduction written on your behalf is the highest-yield form of outreach.
- Research deeply before outreach — understand the specific person's curatorial interests, recent shows, and institutional context; generic praise is an easy delete.
- Curators and gallerists/dealers are looking for fundamentally different things; tailor your approach accordingly.
- The goal of any outreach email or initial meeting is not to get the show — it is to earn the next conversation; keep asks small and appropriate.
- Professional maturity includes discernment: not every gallery or opportunity is worth pursuing; evaluate the other side the way they are evaluating you.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Who in your current network could write a warm introduction on your behalf to a curator or gallery you'd like to connect with?
2. Have you identified your "dream 15–20" aligned galleries or curators? Do you know enough about each one's specific interests to write a non-generic email?
3. What are the one to three sentences that most reliably generate "tell me more" when you introduce your work to a stranger? Have you tested them out loud?
4. If a curator came to your studio today, do you know what you would show first, second, and last — and why that order?
5. Where are you in your readiness for outreach? Is your work forming a coherent enough body that someone could imagine programming it?
