**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Alissa Wilkinson, film critic, New York Times; interviewed by Haiver (program mentor)
**Topic:** Film criticism, the film festival ecosystem, the business of cinema, and what criticism can teach artists about looking

---

## Overview

This interview brings NYT film critic Alissa Wilkinson into conversation with the program's cohort to explore the practice of criticism — what it is, what it is not, and why it matters for visual artists regardless of medium. Wilkinson begins by reframing criticism as an act of love and expansion rather than judgment: "Our job is to expand movies, give context to movies, and explore movies... We wouldn't do this job if we didn't love the art form." She argues that a good review's goal is not for the reader to know what the critic thinks — it is for the reader to have a better sense of what *they* think. This reframing makes criticism structurally parallel to the program's cohort feedback methodology.

The interview covers the full ecosystem of film festivals — Cannes, Venice, Toronto, Sundance, and mid-tier festivals — as both professional gatekeeping structures and genuine community spaces for artists seeking exposure, funding, and collaboration. Wilkinson unpacks how the film industry's business conditions (streaming platform contraction, studio consolidation, AI anxiety, union negotiations) are not external to the art form but actually shape it at the level of form — down to the three-minute trailer-for-itself that now opens every Netflix documentary.

The conversation closes with reflections on experimental and video art's unique capacity to defamiliarize screen experience for audiences so desensitized by passive consumption that only something radically spatial or durational can "rearrange the internal furniture." Wilkinson introduces the concept of "pseudonarrative" to describe time-based video art that carries narrative structure (characters, events, time) without carrying a plot — and argues this is actually closer to life than conventional cinema.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Criticism as Expansion** — Criticism is not fault-finding; it is opening a work up for the reader to walk around inside it. Wilkinson's stated goal: the reader finishes the review with a better sense of their own thoughts.
- **Attending Closely** — The shared root of "critic" and "crit/critique": both require paying close attention and taking a work seriously on its own terms, asking "What is this trying to be?" before evaluating whether it succeeds.
- **Taking the Work on Its Own Terms** — The critic (and peer reviewer) must first ask "What are you?" before assessing success or failure.
- **Double Attention** — Attending to the work and attending to oneself simultaneously: discerning your own reaction and diagnosing whether it comes from the work itself or from personal associations.
- **Jerry Saltz Heuristic** — "What would I like about this if I were the kind of person who liked it?" — a technique for bracketing taste and approaching a work generously.
- **The Business Shapes the Form** — Commerce is not separate from the art form; it works back on the artwork itself (e.g., Netflix's mandatory self-trailer format at the start of all documentaries).
- **Film Festivals as Artist Infrastructure** — Mid-tier and local festivals offer exposure, critical coverage, community, potential collaborators, and market connections.
- **Immersive Sidebars at Festivals** — Cannes, Venice, and Toronto now have dedicated sections for VR, AI, and immersive projects; these are serious competitive venues with juries and awards.
- **Pseudonarrative** — Video art or experimental film that carries narrative structure (characters, time, events) without carrying a plot; allows the viewer to "sink into it as art, almost as if watching a painting."
- **Place-Based Viewing** — Art film and video art often demand presence in a specific physical space; this is a radical political act in a world of platform-agnostic streaming consumption.
- **Rearranging Internal Furniture** — The artist's capacity to shock, move, and reorder the viewer's relationship to screens and moving images.

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Laura Poitras** — *Citizenfour* and *All the Beauty and the Bloodshed* — works simultaneously in art film and political documentary; had a show at the Whitney
- **Christian Marclay** — *The Clock* — cited as a "blockbuster exhibition" that demands place-based viewing at MoMA; cannot be experienced at home
- **A. Young Kim** — *Delivery Dancer* — triptych of films at MoMA PS1; presented as pseudonarrative: characters, movement, commerce, identity — but no plot
- **Bong Joon-ho** — *Parasite* — screened at New York Film Festival before its Oscar win; example of festival discovery
- **Andrei Tarkovsky** — *Stalker* — cited as a model of film that resists conventional narrative and rewards patience
- **Jeff Koons** — Hulk sculptures at Frieze — cited as an example of work that is simultaneously fun and cynical; "both can be true at the same time"
- **Jen and Kevin McCoy** — VR and NFT work at Sundance; visiting artists in the CA program; work appears in major festival immersive sections
- **A.O. Scott** — former NYT film critic; coined "giant ATMs in the sky" for purely commercial blockbuster films

## Core Takeaways

- The critic's primary job is generosity: assume the maker was trying to make something good, take it on its own terms, and open it up for the reader rather than closing it down with a verdict.
- Criticism and peer feedback share a common root: both require close attention, I-statement perspectives, and the discipline to diagnose your own reactions before attributing them to the work.
- Commerce is not separate from form — it is constitutive of it. Understanding the material conditions of production (funding, platform, distribution) makes you a more sophisticated reader and maker.
- Film festivals — including mid-tier and local ones — are legitimate infrastructure for visual artists working in moving images, VR, and immersive formats.
- Video art and experimental film have a specific power that conventional cinema has largely lost: the capacity to defamiliarize screen experience and create a physical, spatial, durational encounter.
- Rejection from festivals and competitions is most often about jury taste and context, not the quality of the work. Being turned down is not a verdict.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. What would a generous critic — someone who assumes you meant to do everything in the work — say your piece is trying to be? Does your work succeed on those terms?
2. Is there a festival, screening series, or immersive sidebar where your work belongs? Have you researched who has shown there before?
3. How does the business or platform context in which your work will be encountered shape the form itself?
4. If your work involves moving images, how does it treat the relationship between the viewer's body and the screen? Is that relationship intentional?
5. What is the non-art background, discipline, or life experience you bring to your practice that you might be hiding or underplaying?
