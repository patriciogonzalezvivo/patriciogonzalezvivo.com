# 146 · Philosophy of Art — What Makes It Mine: Originality and Lineage

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Dr. Rebecca Millsop (philosophy of art lecturer; holds both a studio practice and a doctorate in philosophy)
**Topic:** The first of two Q3 lectures on **originality and authorship**. This session asks: *if every work comes from somewhere, what makes it original?* Four frameworks — Kant on genius, Eliot on tradition, Bloom on the anxiety of influence, Krauss on originality and repetition — reframe influence and inheritance as material to work *with* rather than an anxiety to escape.

---

## Overview

Millsop opens, as she always does, from a "studio anxiety": the fear that *everything has already been done.* Someone has already painted this way, used this material, made the thing you thought was yours. Rather than try to prove that a work "came from nowhere" — which she calls impossible — she flips the question. If every work does come from somewhere, what makes it original? The through-line for the session is **inheritance**: "I'm interested in how we as artists handle what we inherit." The goal is to move from anxiety toward a stable position — *"I can name what I inherit and what I do with it."*

This is a deliberate structural shift from her earlier quarters. Q1 (*what am I making?*) and Q2 (*how does it mean?*) split cleanly into analytic and continental halves; this quarter she abandons that split and organizes around a single **problem with two approaches**. Today's problem is originality and lineage; the companion lecture takes up authorship and circulation ("if meaning and materials move beyond me, what makes me the author?"). She frames all four thinkers as *tools*, not doctrines to defend — the format runs framework, art example, then a three-minute silent reflection question, with a companion PDF toolkit of exercises and reading guides.

The four frameworks answer four different questions you can ask of a single work: **what does it open** (Kant), **where does it sit historically** (Eliot), **what does it turn away from** (Bloom), and **what does it repeat** (Krauss). Crucially, Millsop pairs each canonical (mostly white, male, European) thinker with a corrective — Nochlin, Muñoz, and the artists themselves — to keep what is useful (aliveness, reciprocity, the swerve, repetition-as-original) while discarding what is not (isolated genius, the "mind of Europe," rivalry-as-violence). As the host reframes it in the closing Q&A, all four are ultimately **relational** questions: originality is not what separates you from others but what connects you to them.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Genius gives the rule to art (Kant, *Critique of the Power of Judgment* / Third Critique, 1790)** — Original art is *rule-generating*, not *rule-following*. Two parts: **originality** (no existing rule, recipe, or convention could have prescribed the work in advance) and **exemplarity** (the work becomes a *model* other artists learn from — they respond, resist, or build from it — without giving them a recipe to copy). Being *new* is not enough; a work can be unprecedented without becoming important to anyone else.
- **The reversal of sequence (Kant)** — In the ordinary model, the *rule comes first* (perspective drawing, a glaze recipe, a photographic process) and the work follows. In the genius model the *work comes first*, and only afterward can others understand the possibility it opened. "A recipe gives you steps for reproducing a result. An exemplary work does something else."
- **Exemplarity over self-judgment (Kant, applied)** — The genius question curdles into a verdict on the self ("Am I original enough? Am I talented enough?"). Exemplarity relocates attention from the artist's identity to the work: *what possibility is this making available that wasn't there before?* "This does not have to be grandiose" — it can be a modest possibility inside your own practice or community.
- **Tradition as an active, living order (Eliot, "Tradition and the Individual Talent," 1919)** — Tradition is not a fixed list of admired predecessors but an order that is *alive*. A new work enters it **and changes how the whole order is read** — influence runs in both directions. The **historical sense** is the artist's awareness that the past is past yet still present in the work being made now; it must be *studied and acquired*, not merely possessed by instinct.
- **From genius to individual talent (Eliot)** — Where Kant begins with the artist's special *capacity*, Eliot begins with what the artist *inherits* (language, form, experience, tradition). "Individual talent" is not the display of a unique personality or biography — it is the **transformation** visible in how the work selects, combines, and reworks its inheritance. Hence: honest criticism is directed "not upon the poet, but upon the poetry." *You do not have to be "you" in some wildly original way; you just do what you do to what you have.*
- **Creative misreading / the swerve (Bloom, *The Anxiety of Influence*, 1973)** — A **precursor** is a powerful earlier artist whose achievement feels impossible to ignore and begins to *occupy the imaginative space*. Under that **pressure**, the later work keeps falling back into the precursor's terms. The response is **misprision** (creative misreading). Bloom names six forms; Millsop isolates the **swerve** (*clinamen*): the later artist reads the precursor as having gone the right way and then taken a wrong turn, and makes the turn the precursor "should" have made. The swerve becomes visible in a concrete **formal decision** — scale, material, composition, procedure. The reading need not be historically fair; it needs to be *generative*.
- **Originality and repetition are not opposites (Krauss, "The Originality of the Avant-Garde: A Postmodern Repetition," 1981)** — The avant-garde's strongest myth is *self-origination*: the artist as their own origin, beginning from nothing. Krauss shows the supposedly original work always depends on something **repeatable** — the grid, the cast, a series, a reproductive process. Repetition doesn't spoil originality; **it is often what makes the experience of originality possible.** "Original" therefore cannot mean "made without repetition." Institutions, legal agreements, critical language, and exhibition history are what let a *repetition* count as an *original*.
- **Repetition alone doesn't explain what's yours (Krauss, the residue)** — If every work repeats, then saying "this repeats" doesn't tell us what makes it *this artist's* work. That is answered by **what you did with it** — cropped it, changed the scale, painted over it, placed it beside something else. This is where the other three frameworks re-enter.

## The Four Complications (canon paired with corrective)

Millsop never leaves a canonical thinker unqualified; each is paired with a figure who keeps the useful mechanism and discards the ideology.

- **Kant → Linda Nochlin.** Picasso's *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* is not a clean beginning: Cézanne, Iberian sculpture, African objects (inside colonial systems of collecting), and Braque are all present; it is even disputed as "the first Cubist painting" (sometimes "proto-Cubist"). Nochlin's **"Why Have There Been No Great Women Artists?"** (1971) shifts the question from innate genius to **institutional conditions of access** — the academy, the nude, apprenticeship, citizenship — that make a *sustained, recognized* practice possible. Keep Kant's "opening a possibility"; drop the isolated genius.
- **Eliot → the archive question.** Eliot's tradition is "the mind of Europe," running back to Homer — a *selected and arranged* canon. Keep the aliveness and reciprocity; leave that single European order behind. For many artists the relevant past survives in fragments, or in objects miscategorized by institutions that "did not understand what they were preserving." Such an artist is not entering a tradition but **recovering or constructing one**. Always ask: *who arranged the archive, and what does that let people recognize as art?*
- **Bloom → José Esteban Muñoz.** Bloom's influence is a masculine, father-son *rivalry* resolved by symbolic conquest — leaving no room for lineage through community, genre, or collaboration. Muñoz's **disidentification** (*Disidentifications*, 1999) describes minoritarian subjects working *on and against* an inherited form at once — neither full identification nor total refusal. You can stay inside a form for pleasure or survival while changing whom it serves. Keep Bloom's formal question ("where does the inherited form change direction?"); drop the story that every change is a defeat.
- **Krauss → the artists' choices.** Repetition is not copying: the grid and the cast repeat in wholly different ways, and both remain originals. The point for the studio is permission — you can use a form with a history *without pretending you invented it*, and then attend to what you actually did with it.

## Artists, Writers & Works Referenced

- **Immanuel Kant** — *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (1790); genius as "the talent that gives the rule to art."
- **Marcel Duchamp** — *Fountain* (1917); the lesson is not "a urinal can be sculpture" but "**selection** can be a technique."
- **John Cage** — *4'33"*; made *listening itself* available as compositional material.
- **Pablo Picasso** — *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon*; fractured the relationship between body, viewpoint, and pictorial space.
- **Linda Nochlin** — "Why Have There Been No Great Women Artists?" (1971); institutional conditions of artistic recognition.
- **Senga Nengudi** — *R.S.V.P.* works (1970s); nylon pantyhose filled with sand, stretched and knotted, activated by the body. "I look at art as a big ship. There's enough room for everybody" — originality does not depend on scarcity.
- **T.S. Eliot** — "Tradition and the Individual Talent" (1919); tradition as living order; criticism "not upon the poet, but upon the poetry."
- **Faith Ringgold** — *Tar Beach* (painted story quilt); once it enters the conversation, painting, quilting, autobiography, and Black feminist art can no longer be kept in separate histories.
- **Firelei Báez** — *Man Without a Country* (2014–15); ~9 × 21 ft, 225 individual deaccessioned book pages from 19th-c. texts on Hispaniola, painted over with gouache and ink — breaking open an inherited historical account rather than replacing it with a corrected one.
- **Harold Bloom** — *The Anxiety of Influence* (1973); precursor, misprision, the swerve (*clinamen*).
- **Diego Velázquez → Francis Bacon** — *Portrait of Pope Innocent X* swerved into the screaming, trapped body; keeps the chair, scale, and authority but reveals "the violence that comes with power."
- **José Esteban Muñoz** — *Disidentifications* (1999); working on and against a dominant form simultaneously.
- **Amy Sillman** — *Ghost* (2023–24, acrylic on linen) and the essay "AbEx and Disco Balls"; rereads abstract expressionism against its myth of masculine certainty, keeping gesture but rerouting it toward awkwardness and instability. *After Metamorphoses* (2015–16), a **procedural** swerve from Ovid.
- **Rosalind Krauss** — "The Originality of the Avant-Garde: A Postmodern Repetition" (1981); co-founder of *October* (1976).
- **Piet Mondrian / the modernist grid** — looks like ground zero, but the grid is inherently repeatable and precedes the painting.
- **Auguste Rodin** — *The Gates of Hell*; a posthumous bronze cast Rodin never handled, authenticated as original through an authorized relationship to his model and the conventions of casting.
- **Elaine Sturtevant** — *Warhol Flowers* (1969–70), made with Warhol's own silkscreen; deliberate near-exact repetition where the *choice to repeat closely* becomes the artist's imprint, and the artist's name becomes the operative difference.

## Core Takeaways

- **Move from "has this been done?" to "what does this open?"** The originality question is not about proving you came from nowhere; it is about naming what you inherit and what you do with it.
- **Original means rule-generating, not rule-following.** An exemplary work hands other artists a *possibility*, not a recipe — and the possibility can be modest, even one that only opens inside your own practice.
- **A new work changes its own past.** Tradition is alive and reciprocal: the lineage explains the work, but the work also revises what the lineage can be seen to contain.
- **Stay with the influence instead of fleeing it.** The swerve is where the influence *becomes yours* — look at what changed (scale, material, procedure, whom the form serves), because that change is a decision you made.
- **Repetition can be the origin of originality.** Using a grid, a cast, an existing image, or a process does not disqualify a work; what makes it yours is the specific thing you did with what you repeated.
- **Decision is the site of the artist's hand.** Across all four frameworks the locus of originality is the *choice* — which possibility you pluck, which turn you take, how closely you repeat. "Why did you make that decision?" is the originality question.
- **These are relational, not self-focused, questions.** Inheritance and exemplarity both point outward: you receive from a community and history, and you make something meant to live in the world and open possibilities for others.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. **(Kant)** Choose a recent work. What does it make *possible* that was not possible without it — for you, for your community, or for whoever encounters it? State it modestly and concretely.
2. **(Eliot)** Name one specific thing your work inherits — a medium, an image, a process, a category from an archive. What changes when that inheritance enters *your* work? What does your work do to it — combine, refuse, transform?
3. **(Bloom)** Identify a precursor whose influence you can see all over a current piece. Where exactly does your work *swerve* — and in which formal decision (scale, material, composition, procedure) does that turn become visible?
4. **(Krauss)** What form, image, or process are you repeating that you did not invent? Can you own the repetition openly — and then name precisely what repeating it *lets your work do*?
5. Ask the archive question of your own lineage: *who arranged the tradition you are drawing from,* and are you entering it, recovering it, or constructing one that was never recognized?
