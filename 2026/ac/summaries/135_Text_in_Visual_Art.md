# 135 · Art History Lecture: Text in Visual Art — Part 1

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Dr. Amy Raffel, independent scholar (PhD, CUNY Graduate Center; MA, NYU); specialist in Keith Haring and pop shop; public programs, New York Transit Museum
**Topic:** The history of text and language in visual art, from ancient writing systems through early 20th-century modernism (Dada, Cubism, Russian Constructivism, Italian Futurism, Surrealism)

---

## Overview

Dr. Raffel opens with a condensed history of writing itself — from Sumerian proto-cuneiform (c. 4000 BCE) through the alphabet's abstraction of sound and meaning (~1500 BCE) to Gutenberg's press (1450 CE) — to establish that letters, words, and writing systems are themselves highly developed visual symbols. This historical grounding argues that the integration of text into visual art in the 20th century is not an anomaly but a return to an older, deeper entanglement between the written and the seen.

The lecture then traces how early 20th-century artists, radicalized by World War I and its aftermath, used language as both subject and material. The Dadaists (Hugo Ball, Tristan Tzara, Suzanne Duchamp, Hannah Höch) treated text as something to be destroyed and reassembled — a way to critique the language of journalism, nationalism, and authority that had enabled the war. The Cubists (Braque, Picasso) approached text through the lens of semiotics, investigating how visual signs construct meaning and how far a sign can be abstracted before it stops signifying anything. Russian Constructivism (Rodchenko) treated typography as integrated image and urgent public communication. Italian Futurism (Marinetti) liberated text from grammar to make it kinetic and celebratory. Surrealism (Magritte) used text to destabilize the assumed relationship between word, image, and reality.

Throughout, Dr. Raffel connects these historical movements to more recent artists — Keith Haring's neo-Dada cut-up performances, Martha Rosler's feminist photomontage, and Erica Baum's photographic fragmentation of printed text — demonstrating that the questions these early modernists asked about language are still structuring contemporary practice.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Writing as visual symbol system** — Writing systems are sets of arbitrary symbols representing spoken language; the shift from pictographic to abstract writing (c. 3000 BCE) mirrors the modernist move from representation to abstraction.
- **The arbitrary sign** (after Saussure and Peirce) — Signs are divided into three types: *indexical* (signifies by direct effect, e.g., a thumbprint), *iconic* (resembles what it represents, e.g., a photograph), *symbolic* (represents by convention without resemblance, e.g., the word "violin"). Cubism and Surrealism both interrogate the symbolic sign.
- **Collage as philosophical act** — For Dadaists and Cubists, collage is not just a technique but an ideological gesture: cutting up mass-media materials rejects their authority, and recombining them makes new meaning. It also blurs the high/low cultural boundary.
- **The cut-up technique** (Tristan Tzara, extended by William Burroughs) — Taking newspaper text, cutting it into pieces, and reassembling it by chance to create poetry; a method of destroying the language of dominant ideology and making it unpredictable and democratic.
- **Passage** (Cézanne/Braque) — The technique of breaking contours between objects so that shapes merge with adjacent shapes — a way of reflecting how we actually perceive objects in shifting light.
- **Words and freedom** (Marinetti/Futurism) — Text liberated from grammar, punctuation, and linear order; words as kinetic, visual experience.
- **Automatism** (Surrealism) — Creating art or writing without conscious control to access the unconscious and circumvent rational censorship.
- **Photomontage as counter-reading** — Hannah Höch, Martha Rosler: cutting and reassembling official media images to destabilize their original meaning and recontextualize authoritative voices as fragments that can be questioned.
- **Typography as image** (Rodchenko/Constructivism) — Text is not just something you read but something you see; the visual form of type can embody the emotional content of its message.

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Hugo Ball** — *Karawane* (1916, Cabaret Voltaire, Zurich) — sound poem in costume; founding performance of Dada; demonstrates rejection of journalistic language through nonsense syllables
- **Tristan Tzara** — *Dada Soulève Tout* (c. 1921) — Dada cut-up poem/manifesto; inventor of the cut-up technique
- **Suzanne Duchamp** — Untitled painting combining the Eiffel Tower with scattered, fragmentary text — underrecognized woman Dadaist who combined machine imagery with broken language
- **Hannah Höch** — *Cut with the Kitchen Knife Dada* (c. 1919–20) — pioneer of photomontage; direct political critique of the Weimar Republic
- **George Braque** — *Violin and Palette* (c. 1909) and collages — developed *passage*; included an illusionistic nail as meta-comment on representation; the pun on "JOU" (Le Journal / jouer)
- **Pablo Picasso** — *Portrait of Daniel-Henry Kahnweiler* (1910) and *Still Life with Chair Caning* (1912, first Cubist collage) — integrated mass-produced materials into fine art; treated semiotics as subject
- **Alexander Rodchenko** — *Books! Please, and All Branches of Knowledge* poster (c. 1924) — Constructivist typography as integrated image; text as urgency and social action
- **Filippo Tommaso Marinetti** — *After the Marne, Joffre Visited the Front in an Automobile* (c. 1915) — Futurist text as kinetic visual experience
- **René Magritte** — *The Treachery of Images* (*Ceci n'est pas une pipe*, 1929) — Surrealist text destabilizing viewer assumptions about representation
- **Keith Haring** — Early cut-up collages and performances at Club 57 (late 1970s–early 1980s) — neo-Dada inheritor of Tzara and Burroughs's cut-up tradition
- **Martha Rosler** — *Bringing the War Home* photomontage series (1970s, revived 2008) and *Semiotics of the Kitchen* (video, 1975) — photomontage to critique gender norms and war; the alphabet as framework for semiotic critique
- **Erica Baum** — *Jaws* series (1990s–) — close-up photography of folded and cropped paperback text that abstracts language, analogous to Cubist deconstruction

## Core Takeaways

- Letters and words are visual objects before they are semantic units. Every writing system is an image system. Artists who use text are working in a tradition as old as culture itself.
- The first half of the 20th century was a concentrated period of artists investigating what language does, who controls it, and what happens when it is destroyed, reassembled, or liberated from grammar.
- Collage — whether with scissors, camera, or chance — is a philosophical act. Cutting up mass media rejects the authority of those sources; recombining them constructs new meaning.
- Semiotics gives artists and viewers tools for understanding how signs work: the difference between indexical, iconic, and symbolic signs explains why the same visual motif can simultaneously represent multiple things.
- The arbitrary nature of the sign — a triangle can be a nose if context trains you to read it that way — is both the vulnerability and the expressive power of visual language.
- Text in art is never neutral. How you set a word — its size, angle, font, relation to other visual elements — is itself an argument.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Do you use text in your work, or do you deliberately avoid it? What is the ideological or aesthetic reason for that choice, and is it conscious?
2. If you were to "cut up" the visual language of your work — its recurring symbols, colors, motifs — and reassemble them in a new order, what new meaning might emerge? What would be lost?
3. Which of your visual elements function as symbolic signs (arbitrary conventions), and which function as iconic signs (resemblances)? Can you exploit the tension between them?
4. Rodchenko treated typography as image and urgency. How does the text you write *about* your art (your artist statement, your titles, your captions) function visually — not just semantically?
5. Magritte used text to make the viewer doubt what they were seeing. Is there a statement you could add to one of your works — on the work itself — that would productively unsettle its meaning?
