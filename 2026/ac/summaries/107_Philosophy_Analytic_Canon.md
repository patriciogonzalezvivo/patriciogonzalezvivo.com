# 107 · Philosophy of Art — What Am I Making? The Analytic Canon

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Dr. Rebecca Millsop (philosopher; specializes in aesthetics and open concept theory; practicing artist)
**Topic:** Ontology of art through the lens of analytic philosophy — four frameworks for defining what makes something art

---

## Overview

This lecture opens the year-long philosophy of art strand of the program, which traces four questions across four quarters: What am I making? (Q1), How does it mean? (Q2), Where do I fit? (Q3), and Why does it matter? (Q4). The Q1 focus — the ontology of art — asks what makes something art rather than something else, and why that categorical question has real-world consequences for how work gets shown, written about, collected, and remembered.

Dr. Millsop presents four frameworks from the analytic tradition as tools, not competing truths to pick among. The lecture is structured as a survey-plus-reflection: after each framework she pauses for three minutes of silent reflection, giving students time to locate themselves within the theory before moving on. The frameworks move from the oldest (mimesis — art as imitation) through formalism (art as aesthetic form), institutional theory (art as socially conferred status), and open concept theory (art as an indefinable but expandable category). Each framework is presented sympathetically and then subjected to its own key problem or breakdown case.

The deeper argument of the lecture is that knowing which framework you intuitively privilege is essential self-knowledge for a practicing artist. Your implicit theory of what makes something art shapes how you justify your choices, how you talk to curators and critics, and what you believe counts as success. These frameworks are not just academic — they are the hidden operating systems of studio practice and the art world alike.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Ontology of art** — The philosophical study of what art is; what kinds of things count as art and why

- **Framework 1 — Art as Imitation (Mimesis)**
  - Plato: art is copies of imperfect copies, twice removed from the ideal Forms; dangerous because it makes us feel strongly about non-real things
  - Aristotle: imitation is how humans learn; art reveals universal truths through particular instances; art is a practice of knowledge, not a deception
  - Jacques Rancière: mimesis is not just copying but a whole "regime of representation" — a system that determines what counts as a proper subject for art and how it should be shown

- **Framework 2 — Art as Aesthetic Form (Formalism)**
  - Immanuel Kant: aesthetic experience is "disinterested pleasure" — contemplating the thing itself without asking what it's for; beauty has "purposiveness without purpose"
  - Clive Bell: "significant form" — the combination of lines, colors, shapes that moves us aesthetically; representational content is irrelevant
  - Clement Greenberg: "medium specificity" — each art form should pursue what only it can do; for painting, that means embracing flatness
  - Rosalind Krauss: "post-medium condition" — medium is no longer a material substance but a recursive logic of practice; your medium might be a question you keep asking

- **Framework 3 — Art as Institutional Recognition**
  - Arthur Danto: Warhol's Brillo Boxes (1964) as the key thought experiment — visually identical to supermarket boxes, yet art; context, theory, and art history determine art status
  - George Dickey: "institutional theory of art" — art is whatever gets conferred art status by representatives of the art world
  - Pierre Bourdieu: "cultural capital" and "field theory" — the art world is a structured social space with its own logic and gatekeepers; taste is not natural but socially constructed

- **Framework 4 — Art as Open Concept**
  - Morris Weitz (1956): art cannot be defined with necessary and sufficient conditions because any definition generates counterexamples; art is an open concept whose nature is to keep changing
  - Ludwig Wittgenstein: "family resemblance" — words like "game" and "art" work because they have overlapping similarities, not one shared essence
  - Boris Groys: art's driving force is newness; the art world has an "archive" (recognized culture) and a "trash heap" (unrecognized); innovation happens when something from the trash heap gets pulled into the archive

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Andy Warhol** — *Brillo Boxes* (1964) — The pivotal thought experiment for institutional theory; visually identical to supermarket boxes but conferred art status through gallery context
- **Marcel Duchamp** — *Fountain* (1917) / *Trois Stoppages Étalon* — Proven that context, not object, makes art
- **Samia Halaby** — Computer-generated abstract painting (since the 1980s) — Palestinian woman working in digital abstraction decades before it was institutionally recognized
- **Tara Donovan** — Large-scale installations from everyday materials (plastic cups, toothpicks)
- **Julie Mehretu** — Large-scale layered paintings — formal choices are part of how the work creates meaning about globalization, migration, and capital
- **Jean-Michel Basquiat** — Street tags (SAMO) later conferred museum status — Institutional theory in action
- **Andrea Fraser** — *Museum Highlights* (1989) — demonstrated that museum context creates art status
- **Henry Darger** — Outsider artist who never participated in the art world; now fully institutionally recognized
- **Hito Steyerl** — *How Not to Be Seen: A Fucking Didactic Educational .MOV File*
- **Cameron Rowland** — *Attica desk / courtroom benches* — objects available for rent, not purchase; the art market itself becomes the medium

## Core Takeaways

- Knowing which ontological framework you intuitively privilege is essential self-knowledge — it shapes how you justify your choices and talk to curators and critics.
- These four frameworks are tools, not competing dogmas. A single work can be analyzed productively through all four.
- The question "Is this art?" has real-world consequences — for how work gets shown, written about, collected, and remembered.
- Rosalind Krauss's "post-medium condition" offers a liberating reframe: your medium might not be a material but the recursive question you keep asking.
- Groys's archive/trash-heap model is useful for thinking about where novelty comes from and what it means to bring something new into art discourse.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Which of the four frameworks — mimesis, formalism, institutional, open concept — do you most instinctively use to justify your work? Has that always been true?
2. What is your medium, understood in Krauss's sense — not a material but a recursive logic, the problem you keep returning to?
3. What forms of cultural capital (knowledge, credentials, taste, connections, vocabulary) do you have, and what do you still need to enter the fields you care about?
4. What are you bringing into the archive that wasn't there before? What is the new thing your work does that art discourse hasn't yet absorbed?
5. When a curator, collector, or critic asks "What makes this art?", which framework underlies your answer? Is that the framework you want to be using?
