## Overview

Having spent the first two sessions gathering raw material (Part 1: *What is it?*; Part 2: *What might it mean?* and *Why does it matter?*, plus free-writing exercises), this session is entirely about **revision** — the step the instructor argues *is* writing. The governing claim, repeated through a chain of writers' quotes, is that real writing is editing: "The writing begins when you've finished" (Mark Twain); "My pencils outlast their erasers" (Nabokov); "I write to find out what I have to say. I edit to figure out how to say it right" (Cheryl Strayed).

The session reframes the statement as a **roadmap**: a map doesn't spell out the journey, it shows viewers where things are so they can navigate the work themselves. It dismantles the myth that writing proceeds linearly from first sentence to last — drawing a parallel to the *Eno* documentary, where Brian Eno describes never writing a song beginning to end, but stitching together bits written at different times. Because you learn what you think *by* writing, the thesis (and therefore the opening paragraph) is often discovered last.

The practical core is two exercises and a toolbox. **Hotspotting** — the instructor's favorite — mines a draft for its few most meaningful sentences and rewrites fresh text around them. A set of **editing hacks** (read aloud, change the font, print it out, cut the preamble, swap words for connotation) all serve one purpose: to make the familiar text strange again so you can see it clearly. The session closes on **writing partners** — recording yourself explaining a piece, swapping statements for mutual revision, and the **Red Star** exercise — and the conviction that, in the end, writing teaches writing; the only universal among successful writers is persistence.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **The statement as a roadmap, not a journey** — A map shows where things are so the viewer can navigate the work themselves. You are equipping a stranger who has no reason to care; treat them as a curious, intelligent person and give them a chart into the work.
- **Writing is revision** — Getting words on the page is the *least* of it. "True writers are people who edit — rigorously and passionately." Seeing how far a finished piece is from its first draft is encouraging, not shameful.
- **Nonlinear composition** — Writers jump around; the middle, the ending, or the thesis may come first, and the opening paragraph is often written last (because you only discover your thesis by writing). The *Eno* documentary frames this across mediums: good work is bits stitched together, not a straight line.
- **Permission to begin badly** — "Nothing begins good, but everything good begins" (Kevin Ashton). The only sin is not beginning. The hardest part — making a start — is already done by the time you reach revision.
- **"We lack words and we have too many of them"** (Susan Sontag) — Bad published writing comes from never working down to the essence; we use too many words to say nothing. Revision is the search for the right words in the right order.
- **One thought per sentence** (William Zinsser) — Readers process one thought at a time. "Be grateful for the period." Give the first set of facts, then the next that builds on it. Resist stringing clauses with commas into a wilderness with no road out.
- **Anchor in the concrete first** — The opening should establish *what the work is* ("these are found objects"; "this is oil on canvas"; "this is a portrait"). Naming the parts anchors the reader; the worst move is to open from abstraction.
- **Style is rhythm** — "Grammar is a piano I play by ear… all I know about grammar is its power" (Didion); "Style is a very simple matter: it is all rhythm" (Woolf). Your ear is tuned by reading good writing; choose your sources well.
- **Variety / "become stretchy"** (Janet Fitch) — Vary sentence length and structure: among long sentences, throw in "threes, fivers, and sevens." Monotony of length makes readers go cross-eyed.
- **Verbs of muscle, adjectives of exactitude** (Mary Oliver; "pick a better verb," Fitch) — Most people lean on ~20 tired verbs for everything. Hunt your own repeated "ruts" and replace them with precise, evocative words.
- **Connotation does the work** — *Odor* vs. *aroma* refer to the same sense but carry opposite feeling. "Bad smell" is nowhere near as punchy as "bad odor." Choose words for what they evoke, not just what they denote.
- **Write for the feel, not only the thought** — Reading is an emotional experience too. Don't only *tell* people about the work; choose words that help them *feel* it.

## The Two Core Revision Exercises

**1. Hotspotting** (the instructor's favorite)
- Go through your draft and mark the few most *meaningful* sentences — the ones that communicate an important idea poetically yet clearly. Be **stingy**; find only the essential ones.
- Copy *one* meaningful sentence onto a fresh page — not a blank page, but one with a good idea already on it.
- Spend ~15 minutes writing new text *around that idea*, without worrying how it flows with the rest.
- Compare the new material to the original. Which is better? Cut large sections of the original and sub in the new. Repeat for a few more key sentences.
- Rationale: the first draft is about *getting to* the good idea; the second draft is about figuring out how to say it best. Hotspotting makes space to find new language for an idea you've already crystallized.

**2. Writing-partner exercises**
- **Record-and-transcribe:** Over coffee, record yourself explaining the piece to a partner (or have them interview you about it). Transcribe and edit the recording into the backbone of a first draft — a verbal alternative to free-writing. Watch their engagement as you talk (good "cocktail-party etiquette") and adjust to keep their attention.
- **Swap-and-revise:** Trade finished second/third drafts and revise each other's statements. **Editing someone else's writing is a shortcut to improving your own** — you notice effective moves to emulate, and your outside perspective can recover the essential thing the writer left out.
- **Red Star Revision:** Print the statement; each time your attention drifts, mark that point with a red star. Best used late in drafting. It flags — on the most basic level — exactly where you lose the reader.

## Editing Hacks Toolbox

All of these work by **dislocating you from the familiar** so you can see the text with fresh eyes:

1. **Read it aloud** (or run it through a text-to-speech app like Natural Reader). Write for the ear, not the eye — clarifies errors and reveals sentences you can't actually say.
2. **Change the font (and size) and reread.** The words land in new places on the page; it feels like someone else wrote it.
3. **Print it out and edit by hand.** Combine with #2 and #1 for maximum effect.
4. **Change where the piece starts.** The first paragraph or two is often disposable preamble. Cut your opening to get to something substantial faster — the highest-risk moment for losing a reader.
5. **Cut the abstractions from the first paragraph.** Once you know what your opening is, hunt for obtuse, pushing-away language and get as concrete and specific as possible.
6. **Anticipate misunderstandings.** List how the work has been misread before; ask what you could say to head off those misreadings now. Not defensive — clear and helpful.
7. **Change words for their connotation.** Circle ~5 words where a connotation-rich choice (aroma vs. odor) would hit harder.
8. **Identify words/phrases that make people *feel* the work** — evocative adjectives, muscular verbs.
9. **Always have an editor.** Trade with a writer friend, hire 30 minutes on Fiverr, or trade services. AI tools (ChatGPT) have their own detectable tics (always opening with a curiosity-baiting question); art-world readers can "smell the odor of AI" and be turned off. Human editors help you get out of your own head.

## Core Takeaways

- **Revision is the real writing.** Getting words down is the beginning; the craft is in editing, rearranging, and wearing out your erasers.
- **Composition is nonlinear.** Don't force yourself to start at the beginning — gather bits, find the thesis through writing, and often write the opening last.
- **Hotspotting** is the engine: find your few most meaningful sentences, then rewrite fresh, sharper text around each one and sub it back in.
- **Make the familiar strange** — read aloud, change the font, print it, edit by hand — so you can see the text clearly enough to fix it.
- **Anchor concretely, then build** — one thought per sentence, vary your rhythm, choose verbs of muscle and words with the right connotation, and write so the reader *feels* the work.
- **Use other people.** Writing partners, swapped drafts, the Red Star exercise, and editors all get you out of your own head — and editing others teaches you to edit yourself.
- **Persistence is the only universal.** Pick the methods that work for you, find a few peers who work the way you do, and keep going. The only sin is not beginning.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Run **hotspotting** on your current statement: which three or four sentences carry the most meaning? Rewrite fresh text around one of them — is the new version better than the original?
2. Read your statement aloud (or change its font and print it). Where does a sentence collapse when you actually have to say it, or look wrong in a new typeface?
3. Where does your statement open from abstraction rather than from a concrete anchor (what the work *is*)? Can you cut the preamble and start somewhere more substantial?
4. List the ways your work has been misunderstood before. Which of those misreadings could you head off with a clearer sentence?
5. Who in your community could be a writing partner? Try the record-and-transcribe exercise, swap statements to revise, or run a Red Star pass on each other's drafts.
