# 150 · Interview with Collector and Patron Benny "Redbeard" Gross

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Benny "Redbeard" Gross (collector, patron, and connector in the technology-based art community) in conversation with Haiver (program instructor)
**Topic:** How a collector-patron actually discovers, evaluates, and sustains relationships with artists — the case for direct one-to-one relationships, the argument that "digital art" is simply the future of art, patronage as legacy rather than transaction, and why artists should experiment freely and reach out directly

---

## Overview

Benny Gross — "Redbeard" — is one of the most visible collectors of technology-based art, a fixture on stages at Art Basel and Christie's, and the patron behind studio-visit programs, museum acquisition committees, and the Basel 0.10 digital-art section. He is emphatic that he is *not* an art expert: his wife holds a master's in art history and he "still doesn't understand what chiaroscuro is." His authority comes instead from conviction, proximity to artists, and a background running a school. He got into Bitcoin roughly sixteen years ago (funding it, he says plainly, by "buying weed on the dark web"), stepped away during the 2018 crash while rebuilding his school after a fire, and returned wanting to collect rather than trade. He hangs $20–50k pieces in his school hallways so that five-year-olds and 1,500 students grow up simply *used to* this work — the same logic by which he lets his own kids use AI: the thing you forbid entirely becomes the candy the kid throws up on at the sleepover; moderation, not abstinence, teaches a healthy relationship to technology.

The through-line of the interview is a single practical claim aimed directly at artists: **the future of this whole movement runs on one-to-one relationships**, and artists have never had a better moment to build them. Art was always relationship-driven; the gallery era tapered that off; social platforms and direct messaging have brought it back. Benny bought almost no art through galleries — he DM'd the artists. He describes an hour-and-a-half phone call with a young artist that his wife found baffling ("you don't realize how lucky you are that you can talk to artists — collectors never had that"). The three artists he watched sell out recently all did the same unglamorous thing: they personally called every collector they'd ever had, one at a time, and said "I'd love to show you what I'm working on." That is a pitch, and there is nothing wrong with a pitch. Even when it doesn't sell, it earns a retweet, a post, a mention — and in an attention economy, those compound.

The rest of the conversation orbits that core. Benny defends "digital art" as just the next iteration of art (plein-air easels and photography were once decried as cheating too), and predicts contemporary art's market is dying while the movement merely changes and gets renamed by a future generation. He reframes the NFT stigma ("say 'on chain,' it's the same thing — the technology works; the speculation was a narrative"). He counsels fearless experimentation (Picasso's pottery, his juvenile beach drawings), addresses AI fear head-on ("I'm sorry you're scared, but it's happening — AI could be your next paintbrush"), and closes on the stakes that actually move him: legacy, memory, and the refusal to let artists be forgotten. He wants to be remembered "not for what I collected, but for what I made possible" — and his parting instruction to the cohort is: don't stop making, because "if you stop, I have no reason to go forward."

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Moderation over abstinence** — The way to build a healthy relationship to any technology (devices, Snapchat, AI, digital art) is exposure in moderation, not prohibition. Forbid it completely and it becomes the candy the sleepover kid binges until he throws up. Applies to how he raises his kids, runs his school, and normalizes new art.
- **Normalization by exposure** — Hanging serious work where children pass it daily isn't about "inspiration" ("everything should be fucking inspiration; that's a dumb answer"). The goal is that they're *used to it* — so that one day in a museum they think "I've seen that before." The audience of the future is being acclimatized now.
- **"Digital art" is just the future of art** — Not synonymous with NFTs or blockchain; that's one small part. Almost everything artists make going forward will have some digital component (even a digital study for a physical work). The movement is "so amorphous right now" it doesn't have a name yet; someone 20–30 years out will name this period. Contemporary art, by the numbers, is "dying" — the movement isn't dead, it's changing.
- **The technology-is-cheating fallacy** — Every tool that expanded art was once called cheating: the portable easel that let painters work outdoors (a piece of tech "some guy created to make money"), digital vs. film photography, artists staffing their own booths at Basel. "Most of the most famous artists in our lives were at some point considered hacks because they were using a piece of technology."
- **There are no rules — and artists need to be reminded of that** — Benny disagrees that artists already know this. The default script ("go to a gallery, sell my soul for 60%, hope they believe in me") has convinced artists they *can't* break rules. They need reminding that they can build their own spaces, networks, and micro-economies. He invokes Warhol's Factory as the kind of open studio-space the scene now lacks.
- **The attention economy as opportunity** — "There's never been a time, ever, that you can build your own space." Direct access to individuals means you can build your own micro-economies (or "micro-religions, micro-terrorist groups — whatever the fuck you want"). Artists no longer need a gallery, fair, or physical location to reach the people who'd support them.
- **You don't have to be an expert to have conviction** — From roughly 1990–2005 the message was: to know if art is great, you must be brilliant — or defer to someone who is. Benny rejects this. "Joe Schmoe could buy anything they want." His only rule for new buyers: **you have to love it.** Buy for investment and "you're probably going to lose all your money." Conviction, not credentials.
- **Patronage as legacy, not transaction** — What collecting does for him, watching his 86-year-old father decline with dementia and raising young children, is offer a way to leave a mark. On-chain and social work is "there forever" — the artist becomes "part of history, no matter what anyone wants to tell you." He wants to "live forever through helping create these things."

## How a Collector Actually Discovers and Chooses Artists

- **Direct message, not gallery** — Benny "barely bought art through a gallery." His method: "I just DM them — yo, what do you do? what is this? I do that all the time." He still supports galleries he knows because he believes they matter, but the relationships that built his collection were direct.
- **The olive branch signal** — If an artist takes the call, the Zoom, the dinner, the lunch — "if they're giving that olive branch just a little bit, they're interested." Taking the meeting doesn't mean buying; but interest is real, and even a non-buyer will support you with a retweet, a post, a message.
- **Love first, value never** — His first instruction to anyone about to buy: you have to love it. He's indifferent to whether a piece is physical or digital (he owns more digital but "doesn't particularly care"); he'll collect an artist's work "any day of the week" regardless of medium if he loves it.
- **He decides with his wife (and trusted experts)** — She's "the brains," gives perspective, and vets pieces; the ones they both love get the best spots at home. He also leans on curators and friends (Marcus Fox at Christie's, writer Georgina Adams, Regina at MoMA) so he doesn't need to be the expert himself.
- **Conviction over knowledge** — His confrontation with a gallerist in Saudi Arabia who mocked him for not being able to name every Picasso: "I don't need to know that... but I sold more art than you did in 10 years. That's my conviction." Georgina Adams told him what mattered wasn't art-history mastery but that "you care about what you do. That's all that matters."

## What Builds Trust (and What the Artist Should Actually Do)

- **Call your collectors individually** — The single behavior common to the artists he watched sell out: they personally, one at a time, called every collector they'd ever had and said "I'd love to show you my art." Benny saw the lists. It is a pitch; that's fine.
- **Lead with the work, not the sale** — Strong openers that aren't a hard sell: "I'd love to have a conversation." "I'd love to show you what I put my heart and soul into." "I'd love to show you what I'm doing next." "I'm not even selling anything right now — I just want your advice on something." All of these "make a huge difference."
- **You're an artist, not a car salesman** — Artists are *allowed* to do this direct outreach. The alternative is paying someone a lot of money to do it for you (not a bad idea either) — but why give up being the first point of contact, the person who explains what your work is and makes the human connection?
- **Bypass the toxic timeline** — Community platforms (X especially) can be disheartening — for a few days the feed was "all this art," now hate rises first. The way past it is the direct one-on-one conversation. "I'm a big believer of that."
- **He is genuinely reachable** — "Talking to artists is pretty much what I do all day." He invites the whole cohort to DM him on X, Instagram, or Telegram, offers to help artists plan their outreach ("test it out on me — here's a good person to call"), and to connect them to others.

## Reframing the NFT / Digital Stigma

- **Separate the technology from the speculation** — "The idea that there was speculation has nothing to do with the actual technology." When Mt. Gox was hacked in 2015, headlines said "Bitcoin got hacked" — but *Mt. Gox* got hacked; the technology still worked. He put in nearly everything he and his wife had. NFTs are the same: "the technology worked — why? because they speculated off of it, so clearly it works."
- **"Say 'on chain'"** — The word "NFT" now triggers people, so he's stopped using it — not because the substance changed. If provenance, on-chain ownership, generative/AI work, or community connection genuinely fit your practice, use the technology and "don't worry about it. People will forget." An NFT is just a token.
- **Hypocrisy of the stigma** — A stuffed shark sold for ~$20M and "nobody said boo for years because everybody made money off of it." "It's all narrative. It's all bullshit." The biggest platform for scams in the world "is called the dollar bill." Scams and grifts exist in every medium (the "big eyes" painter whose wife made the work) — that's not an indictment of any one technology.
- **Use what makes sense for you** — "Use technology that makes sense for you." Black-and-white cookie: some like black, some white, some both. "It's art. There's no answer."

## On Experimentation, Fear, and AI

- **This is the time to experiment** — "This idea that I just need to put my best work out to sell — fuck that." Picasso made pottery ("why the fuck are you making pottery?" — now everyone wants it), juvenile centaur beach drawings, early charcoals. "It doesn't matter that you experiment. There's no better time." He backed an artist (Shlombs) whose early project smashed a toilet into pieces and sold the shards.
- **You don't have to love everything an artist makes** — "You can love an artist who's creating, and then all of a sudden — and I like that, okay, wait till the next thing." A permission that frees artists to make uneven, exploratory work.
- **Confront fear rather than give up** — On AI and data-center anxiety: "I'm sorry you're scared, but it's happening." Legislation and protections for lower-income communities: "a thousand percent." But AI isn't going away; some jobs (e.g. accountants) will go; the Industrial Revolution provoked identical panic and "we survived — we're human beings, we'll figure it out." Fire has bad parts too; we harness it. His daughter's monster-in-the-closet: you go into the room and show her there's nothing there. "AI could be your next paintbrush if you use it the right way."

## Institution-Building: Basel 0.10 and Acquisition Committees

- **0.10's origin** — Credit to Noah Horowitz, Basel's CEO, "who took the risk." Benny, "a little too emotional," wrote a passionate letter to a chain not realizing Horowitz was on it; they became friends and Horowitz eventually put digital art on the main floor. It grew from Miami (10 galleries) to Hong Kong to Basel proper (20 galleries, second curator Trevor Paglen joining Eli Scheinman).
- **Its real success would be its own disappearance** — "If I had to say what the biggest success of 0.10 would be, is that 0.10 doesn't exist anymore" — because all of Basel simply *has* digital art, not taboo, not a weird new thing, just part of everything. "We're definitely not there yet, but the conversations have started."
- **The Gertrude Stein / salon model** — Sitting in a Paris park where Stein once received and funded artists, he saw the arc: the salon started down the street, then next door, then took over the whole building. That's his vision for how digital art enters the institutions.
- **"The barbarians are at the gate"** — An Artnet CEO reportedly said of the 0.10 crowd: "I don't like the art, I don't like the people... but the barbarians are at the gate. Either we let them in, or they break the doors down." Benny: "I don't mind being a barbarian... that's how every movement starts. We'll work with you — but you've got to play in our sandbox too."
- **Building acquisition committees** — He sits on committees (Whitney, Toledo) and works with Pompidou, M+, MoMA. His current main push: get a digital-art acquisition line into most major museums. Ten museums each spending $150–200k = ~$2M of institutional support — "it changes the narrative." The movement, he stresses, is not five or six years old but sixty (per Michael Spalter): "I've laid the seeds in a desert, and now the trees are blooming."

## Core Takeaways

- **Relationships are the whole game.** Art was always relationship-driven; the tools now let artists reach collectors directly. "You have the best opportunity to struggle less than any artist has in history."
- **Reach out directly and personally.** The artists who sell out call every collector one at a time. It's a pitch; that's fine. Even a non-buyer becomes a supporter — a retweet, a post, a connection.
- **You don't have to be an expert — you have to have conviction, and you have to love it.** For collectors and artists alike, credentials matter less than care. Buy for love, not investment; make what you actually love.
- **"Digital art" is just the future of art, and the technology stigma is a narrative.** Separate the tool from the speculation; call it "on chain" if "NFT" triggers people; use whatever medium genuinely fits your practice.
- **Experiment relentlessly and don't fear the new.** This is the moment to make the pottery, the toilet shards, the AI experiment. Fear (of AI, of the market) is real but is not a reason to give up — confront it, harness it, adapt.
- **Patronage is about legacy.** The point isn't what you buy; it's what you make possible. On-chain and social work is permanent — it makes artists "part of history." Don't stop making: "if you stop, I have no reason to go forward."

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Who are the people who have already given you an "olive branch" — taken a studio visit, a call, a DM, replied warmly on social — and when did you last personally reach out to each of them, not to sell but to show what you're working on next?
2. Could you write, today, the list of every person who has ever bought, supported, or engaged with your work — plotter drawings, paintings, shaders, on-chain pieces — and call or message them one at a time the way Benny's sold-out artists did?
3. Where in your practice are you still "putting only my best work out to sell" instead of experimenting freely? What is your equivalent of Picasso's pottery — the odd, risky direction you've been withholding because it isn't a safe bet?
4. Your work already spans "physical" (oil, plotter, ash) and "on-chain" (NFT/blockchain) — do you present that range as a coherent whole, or do you let the NFT stigma make you hide half of it? What would it mean to say simply "I use whatever technology fits the work"?
5. Beyond selling, what do you want a collector or patron to make *possible* through your work — and can you name the legacy or long-horizon stake (family, migration, presence, teaching) that a relationship-driven patron like Benny would actually connect to?
