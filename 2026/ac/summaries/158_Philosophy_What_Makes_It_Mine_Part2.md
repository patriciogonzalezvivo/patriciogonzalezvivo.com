# 158 · Philosophy of Art — What Makes It Mine, Part 2: Authorship

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Dr. Rebecca Millsop (philosophy of art lecturer; holds both a studio practice and a doctorate in philosophy)
**Topic:** The second of two Q3 lectures on **originality and authorship**. Part 1 ([[146_Philosophy_What_Makes_It_Mine|Lesson 146]]) asked *is anything original?* and answered with inheritance (Kant, Eliot, Bloom, Krauss). Part 2 turns to the companion question: *if your work inherits and depends on so much outside you, how are you still the author of it?* Four frameworks — Foucault on the author as function, Haraway on situated knowledge, Becker on collective production, Benjamin on the author as producer — reframe authorship as something built through relationships and arrangements rather than sole origination.

---

## Overview

Millsop opens by connecting directly back to Part 1: last time the cohort accepted that a work is never original from nowhere — it inherits constantly. This raises the natural follow-up: *if a fabricator makes part of your work, if you use existing material, if you don't personally make what you make at all — how are you still the author?* The lecture reframes authorship not as sole origination but as **a specific set of decisions, positions, and relations that can be named and owned even inside heavy dependence on other people, materials, and systems.**

She reintroduces Sturtevant's *Warhol Flowers* (from Part 1) to open the question freshly: the image is visually identical to Warhol's, made from his own silkscreens — so what does attaching a name to it actually *do*? Placing "Warhol" next to it connects it to soup cans and Marilyns; placing "Sturtevant" next to it connects it to her practice of exact repetition of other artists' work (Duchamp, Jasper Johns). Same image, different questions asked of it, purely because of attribution. That's the lecture's throughline: **authorship is a function that organizes interpretation, not a guarantee of sole creation.**

Four frameworks, each answering a different question about authorship:
1. **Foucault** — what does naming an author actually *do*? (the author as function)
2. **Haraway** — what does the artist's own position/experience contribute? (situated knowledge)
3. **Becker** — how does authorship work when many people's activity makes the work possible? (collective production)
4. **Benjamin** — can the artist's contribution include *how the work is made and circulated*, not just what it says? (the author as producer)

As in Part 1, the format pairs each canonical (largely white, male, European) thinker with contemporary artist examples and a three-minute silent studio-reflection prompt, and Millsop repeatedly stresses these are tools to take what resonates and leave what doesn't — not a debate to resolve.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

### 1. The Author as Function (Michel Foucault, "What Is an Author?", 1969)

- **The person vs. the author.** The *person* lives an actual life — makes decisions, tries things, changes their mind. The *author* is an entity constructed through the body of work, exhibitions, statements, and what others say — "you can tell they're an artist because of the purple beret." Attribution doesn't mean everything originated with the person; it means something is being *recognized*, with real consequences.
- **The author function does three things:** (1) connects a work to other works by the same name, building a legible *body of work*; (2) guides interpretation — knowing whose work it is supplies questions and expectations the viewer brings to it; (3) has practical, institutional consequences — museums, catalogs, and critics decide what "belongs" to an artist's practice for exhibition, cataloguing, and posterity.
- **Sturtevant's *Warhol Flowers* revisited.** Once attributed to Sturtevant, the interesting question shifts from "how closely does this copy Warhol?" to "why does she keep returning to other artists' work?" — attribution changes which question is even worth asking.
- **The trap of a settled author function.** Once an audience's expectation of an artist ("known for work about grief") calcializes, a genuinely new direction can get read *through* the old expectation rather than encountered fresh — "the artist supplies the answer before the new work has had much of a chance to do anything new." The artist alone can't fix this by writing a new statement; exhibition texts, reviews, interviews, and earlier work all participate in resetting expectation.
- **Studio use:** the author function is something you can *use as material* — deciding what counts as part of "your work" and what stays private, showing work with or without context to see how that shifts critique, experimenting with a persona, pseudonym, or invented biography. You can't control everything your name comes to mean, but you can experiment with what you connect it to and what expectations you invite or interrupt.
- **Example: Martine Gutierrez**, *Indigenous Woman* (2018) — a 124-page artist-made magazine built to look exactly like a glossy commercial publication, in which Gutierrez occupies every normally-distributed role (model, photographer, stylist, writer, editor) to explore Mayan heritage and contemporary indigeneity. Encountered without attribution it reads as "a weird fashion magazine"; attributed to Gutierrez, it becomes legible as an artist working the entire apparatus of commercial image production as her subject and medium at once.

### 2. Situated Authorship (Donna Haraway, "Situated Knowledges," 1988)

- **If the author-function doesn't guarantee control over meaning, what does the artist's own position contribute?** Haraway (better known for *A Cyborg Manifesto*) asks how we can know the world when we always encounter it from a particular position — and why some accounts get to stand for "knowledge in general" while others (in her case, women's experience) get dismissed as merely partial.
- **The "God trick."** The fantasy of a neutral, position-less view — someone who "just sees things as they are." A critic who says "you have a perspective" (about the artist) while claiming "I just see things as they are" (about themselves) is performing the God trick: exempting their own training and position from account while holding the artist's position against them. Haraway rejects the possibility of that view entirely — everyone's knowledge is situated, without exception.
- **Situated authorship, applied:** your body, access, relationships, and experience shape what you're even able to notice and make — not a limitation to overcome, but the actual source of what only you could bring to the work.
- **Does situated authorship mean all work must be autobiographical?** No — Millsop explicitly rejects this as a restrictive misreading. Being situated doesn't require the artist's own life be the *subject*; even researching unfamiliar territory, inventing characters, or working with historical material is still approached *from* a situated position (questions asked, relationships built, way of learning) that shapes what gets discovered and made. **An artwork should not be treated as a transparent expression of its maker's identity** — an artist can work from a position while using the work to interrogate, question, or imagine beyond that position.
- **Example: LaToya Ruby Frazier**, *The Landscape of a Body: Epilepsy Test* (2011) — juxtaposes her mother undergoing medical testing with the demolished Braddock, PA hospital her family depended on; the visual echo (medical wires / demolition cables) only becomes visible through Frazier's specific relationship to that place and that dependency.
- **Example: Sin Wai Kin** (b. 1991, Toronto, based in London) — drag, film, and performance drawing on Chinese opera, Taoist philosophy, boy-band and TV conventions to construct recurring characters that unsettle how gender and identity get *learned* to be recognized (*A Dream of Wholeness in Parts*, 2021). Haraway's framework applies on **both sides**: Sin's situated experience of drag and Chinese opera shapes the choices that build the character; the viewer's own situated position (familiar with Chinese opera or not) shapes what they can recognize in it — applying Haraway to making *and* viewing at once.
- **The grandparents'-house example** (Millsop's own illustrative scenario): photographing a childhood room you remember as welcoming; a cousin who grew up scolded for touching the same shelves remembers something entirely different. Your memory isn't wrong, but your account was incomplete — situated knowledge doesn't require abandoning your account, but does require checking it against others' access to the same space.

### 3. Art as Collective Production (Howard Becker, *Art Worlds*, 1982)

- **Becker (American sociologist and jazz pianist)** asks how people learn to work together and what they need from one another to make something — starting point: **art is a collective activity**, full stop, even when a single hand makes every mark. Paint manufacturers, technique lineages, and exhibition conventions all shape what gets made even in total studio solitude.
- **"Art world"** = the network of people whose cooperative activities produce works of a particular kind — extending well beyond people the artist personally knows.
- **Conventions make cooperation possible without re-explaining everything from scratch:** a musician's count-in, chord symbols that leave room for improvisation, standard paper sizes and file formats between an artist and printer, lighting cues in theater, how a painting is framed or an edition numbered. Departing from a convention is possible, but something else usually has to change to accommodate it (needing new equipment, a new process).
- **Not every activity counts equally as "the artist's work."** Art worlds distinguish *defining* activities from *supporting* activities, and that distinction is learned through participating in a practice — it determines who gets recognized as author and what they're expected to have decided. Example: a photographer selects the subject and frames/selects the image; a specialist printer chooses paper, contrast, and how much shadow detail survives — decisions that visibly affect the final print — yet convention still names the photographer "the artist" and the printer "technical assistance." Becker's framework doesn't overturn that convention, but asks the artist to **examine it deliberately**: which decisions did each person actually make, and what does the standard division of labor let us see or overlook?
- **Collective production ≠ equal authorship.** Cooperation does not mean equal power, equal agreement, or equal benefit — describing everything as "collaboration" can hide real differences in contribution just as easily as claiming sole credit can. The honest question is: what is the actual arrangement, and should it change?
- **For authorship:** the artist's contribution is located precisely by asking what they actually decided, versus what the collective activity around them supplied — not by denying the collective activity exists.
- **Example: Mierle Laderman Ukeles**, *Touch Sanitation* (1979–80) — over 11 months, across all 59 NYC sanitation districts, Ukeles personally shook hands with 8,500 sanitation workers, saying "Thank you for keeping New York City alive," developing the route in direct collaboration with the department's operations chiefs. Becker's framework explains why this counts as authored art: Ukeles conceived and carried the action across an entire department, but doing so required the department's operational knowledge, the workers' participation, and city-scale logistics she didn't personally control — her authorship is legible *as* a specific set of decisions inside a much larger collective structure, not despite it.
- **Studio use:** notice which conditions you work within even in solitude (materials, learned techniques, exhibition conventions) as part of a larger "art world"; when involving other people, decide deliberately *which* expertise you need them for and *which* decisions you want to keep making yourself.

### 4. The Author as Producer (Walter Benjamin, "The Author as Producer," 1934)

- **Benjamin** (German-Jewish philosopher, writing in Paris exile after fleeing Nazi Germany, working explicitly within Marxism and anti-fascism) asks a further question: can the artist's authorship include *changing the actual arrangement through which the work is made and reaches people* — not only what the work says?
- **Four (informally identified) stages of "producing":** making (material/tool/skill/resource choices) → selecting and preparing (editing, choosing what's shared, for what context) → presenting and circulating (whoever controls this route has real influence over who can encounter the work and how) → encountering/using (the audience's own engagement, which can itself feed back into further production).
- **Benjamin's political demand:** a work can politically *oppose* an institution in its content while supplying that same institution with exactly the commercial product it already knows how to sell — opposition can be quietly absorbed as consumption. Example: *The World Is Beautiful* (Albert Renger-Patzsch, 1928) — beautifully composed photographs of nature, objects, and industry that make a factory visually fascinating *without* revealing who controls production or under what conditions people work there. You can admire the form while the actual relations of power stay invisible.
- **Three examples used to test the framework, deliberately left unresolved rather than sorted into "good" vs. "bad" images:**
  - *The World Is Beautiful* — form without disclosed relations of power.
  - **Dorothea Lange**, *Migrant Mother* (1936) — made within an FSA government documentary project, circulated through newspaper reporting that connected the image to food-relief policy — Benjamin's framework directs attention to the *institutional arrangement* (caption, publication, agency) around the image, not just the image alone.
  - **The Abu Ghraib hooded-prisoner photograph** (2003) — taken by a participant in the abuse, not a photojournalist making a statement, yet later circulated by editors and journalists to become evidence used to challenge that abuse — extending "author as producer" beyond the original maker to everyone who shapes an image's circulation and political use, sometimes against the original maker's intent.
- **Technique (in Benjamin's sense) includes caption, editing, juxtaposition, staging, and sequence** — decisions that change what a viewer can *do* with an image, not just what it depicts. His example: Brecht's **epic theater** — revealing a scene's outcome via a projected caption *before* it plays out shifts audience attention from "what happens next?" to "why did this happen, and could it have been otherwise?" — using interruption to produce active, examining spectators rather than passive, absorbed ones.
- **A crucial distinction:** *inviting someone to respond* to your work is different from *giving them a greater capacity to produce work themselves* (the stronger, more demanding version of Benjamin's political goal). Millsop is explicit that not every valuable artwork has to transform its audience into producers to count as authored, political, or important — but the question of what a work's mode of production and circulation *makes possible for others* is a real, extra axis of authorship most artists don't examine.
- **Example: Joana Moll**, *The Hidden Life of an Amazon User* (2019) — Moll bought a book about Jeff Bezos on Amazon and traced everything her browser loaded during that purchase, then rebuilt the transaction as a scrollable online work: an ordinary storefront gives way to an overwhelming, screen-filling field of the actual code and estimated energy cost behind one "buy" click. Moll's authorship is entirely in research, selection, and arrangement of material she didn't originate — she doesn't hand the viewer control over Amazon's infrastructure, but she does force an ordinary, invisible action into something the viewer has to spend time inside and reckon with.
- **Studio-level questions Benjamin opens up:** How does the work direct attention (sequence, caption, juxtaposition)? Where and in what format is it shown — gallery, publication, downloadable file — and how does that choice determine who can encounter it and on what terms (price, location, permission)? Could you share a method, a score, or enough detail that someone else could adapt or produce from it, and is that a goal for this particular work or not?

## Core Takeaways

- **Calling a work "yours" never has to mean everything in it began with you.** You can name what you shaped, what you decided, and what you made possible — that is what authorship actually consists of across all four frameworks.
- **An author's name is a function, not a guarantee.** It organizes interpretation, connects work into a body, and carries real institutional weight — and, once settled, can also trap new work inside old expectations that only exhibition context, critical writing, and time can loosen.
- **Being situated is not the same as being autobiographical.** Your position shapes what you can notice and make even when the subject is unfamiliar territory, invented characters, or historical material — the artwork is not required to be a transparent self-portrait to be authentically, situationally yours.
- **Dependence on other people's activity does not make your contribution unreal — it makes it locatable.** Becker's framework asks precisely *which* decisions were the artist's, inside real cooperative structures that also include materials, technique lineages, and institutional conventions the artist didn't invent.
- **Authorship can include how a work is made and how it reaches people, not only what it says.** Benjamin's producer framework — caption, sequence, format, price, access, and whether the work extends anyone else's capacity to produce — is a real and underused axis most artists never examine deliberately.
- **These four frameworks are relational, not self-focused.** Together they answer *what does the name do, what does your position bring, what do others contribute, and what does the arrangement make possible* — none of which requires proving sole origination.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. **(Foucault)** Choose a current body of work. What does someone assume about it purely because it's associated with your name and prior practice — and would you want to reinforce that expectation, or deliberately interrupt it with this next piece?
2. **(Haraway)** For a work drawing on unfamiliar territory (a research subject, an instrument's data, a place you didn't grow up in), what does your specific position let you notice that someone else would miss — and what might a different vantage point (a collaborator, a subject, a critic) surface that you can't currently see?
3. **(Becker)** For a machine-assisted or collaboratively fabricated work (plotter output, cast or printed elements, code someone else wrote a library for), which decisions are unambiguously yours, which are the collaborator's or the tool's, and does your current framing of "who made this" actually reflect that division — or flatten it in one direction?
4. **(Benjamin)** Beyond what a work depicts or means, what does its actual mode of production and circulation let someone do — does showing it only in a gallery, versus as an open-source tool, a publication, or a downloadable file, change who can encounter it and what they can do with that encounter?
5. Given that this practice already builds open tools for others (Book of Shaders, PixelSpirit, LYGIA, Vera) — where Kant's exemplarity (Part 1) and Benjamin's producer question (Part 2) directly overlap — is there a current work where you are only *inviting response* when you could instead be *extending someone else's capacity to produce*, and is that distinction one you want to act on for this piece?
