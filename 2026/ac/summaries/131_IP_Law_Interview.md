## Overview

David Shein, a copyright and entertainment lawyer whose practice focuses on representing artists, walks through the fundamentals of US intellectual property law with particular attention to visual artists — painters, photographers, muralists, and illustrators. The conversation is deliberately practical rather than theoretical, aimed at artists who create valuable work but rarely understand the legal framework protecting it. The central message is that copyright is automatic at the moment of creation, but registration with the US Copyright Office is what transforms that abstract right into an enforceable tool with real financial teeth.

The interview moves from first principles (what copyright is, what "the bundle of sticks" means, how originality is defined in law) through to concrete scenarios: what happens when a photo ends up on a T-shirt sold at Target, how murals in ad shoots constitute infringement, and what an artist should actually say — and not say — when sending a cease-and-desist letter. Shein is consistently candid about the gap between legal theory and practical litigation, including the role of contingency-fee lawyers in making enforcement realistic for working artists.

A recurring thread is the idea/expression dichotomy: you cannot copyright an idea, a style, or a natural subject — only your specific expression of it. Shein illustrates this through the sunset example, the glass octopus case (*Satava*), and a real-world anecdote about mirror-covered cactus sculptures. The conversation closes on the registration process itself — step-by-step guidance on copyright.gov — and a real case (muralist Mad Steez vs. Sharp Electronics) that demonstrates how rights can be unexpectedly complicated by third-party agreements and metadata chains.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Bundle of sticks** — Copyright is not a single right but a cluster: reproduction, derivative works, distribution, public performance, public display, and licensing. Each stick can be conveyed separately.
- **Copyright subsistence at creation** — US copyright attaches the moment a work is fixed in a tangible medium, regardless of registration.
- **Registration benefits (US)** — Unlocks three specific advantages: (1) statutory damages ($750–$150,000 per willful infringement); (2) profit disgorgement; (3) recovery of attorney's fees, which makes contingency-fee representation viable.
- **Statutory damages vs. actual damages** — Statutory damages are especially valuable when the infringer made little money but acted willfully; actual damages depend on licensing history, which young artists often lack.
- **Idea/expression dichotomy** — Ideas, styles, natural subjects, and common concepts are not protectable. Only the specific fixed expression is protectable. Rooted in *Baker v. Selden* (1880).
- **Originality threshold** — Very low in US law; novelty is not required. Even a split-second iPhone photo qualifies. The copyright office will reject only the most elemental geometric forms.
- **Work for hire** — Works made by employees in the scope of employment belong to the employer. Independent contractors retain ownership unless there is a signed written transfer.
- **Implied license** — When a creator is paid to produce work for a specific purpose and delivers it without a written assignment, courts may find an implied license (*Effects v. Cohen*).
- **Publication vs. unpublished status** — Posting to Instagram is arguably not publication. Works in studio that have not been licensed or offered for sale are most cleanly registered as unpublished.
- **Registration timing and the 90-day grace period** — To access statutory damages and attorney's fees, a work must be registered before the infringement *or* within 90 days of first publication.
- **Access requirement** — To prove copyright infringement, you must establish that the alleged infringer had access to the work, or that the works are virtually identical (making independent creation implausible).
- **DMCA takedown notices** — Still available but weakened by *Cox Communications*; ISPs now have near-immunity from secondary liability.
- **Cease-and-desist letter discipline** — Keep it factual, professional, and non-specific about damages to preserve legal options.

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Mad Steez (Mark)** — Self-portrait photograph in front of his own mural — cited as a real case Shein litigated against Sharp Electronics involving both likeness rights and copyright in a photograph of copyrighted artwork
- **Dua Lipa** — Used as a parallel example of combined likeness rights and photographic copyright claims against a consumer electronics company (Samsung)
- **Beeple** — Work shown at Art Basel Miami — cited as an example of artwork widely reproduced by news organizations without permission
- **Keith Haring** — Cited as an example of an iconic visual style that, despite being highly recognizable, cannot be copyrighted as a style; discussed in the context of where trademark law begins
- **OK Go** — Cited in discussion of the idea/expression dichotomy; their in-camera optical illusion style was allegedly replicated by an Apple ad campaign, which is not a legal violation but may be an ethical one
- **Drake** — Cited repeatedly as a real subject of Shein's firm's copyright litigation involving music (beat copying), illustrating the access requirement

## Core Takeaways

- Copyright is automatic and exists at the moment of creation in a fixed medium — no registration, no mail-to-self needed.
- Registration with the US Copyright Office is strongly advisable *before* publication or within 90 days of publication; it is what enables statutory damages and attorney's fee recovery, making enforcement financially viable.
- You can copyright your specific expression; you cannot copyright an idea, a style, a natural subject, or a concept.
- As an independent contractor you always own the copyright in work you make unless you sign a written transfer — verbal agreements and payment alone are not enough to transfer copyright ownership.
- When infringed, consult a lawyer before sending any communication. Keep cease-and-desist language simple, professional, and damage-neutral to avoid inadvertently capping your claims.
- Metadata and digital timestamps are the modern equivalent of the postmark envelope — document your work with photographs and dated files.
- Non-US artists can register with the US Copyright Office and can sue in US courts without registration if their work was first published outside the US (though without the same damage benefits).

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Have you registered your most publicly visible or commercially significant works with the US Copyright Office? If not, what is your plan and timeline to do so?
2. Do you have signed, written agreements with every person or organization that has ever commissioned work from you? Are those agreements explicit about who owns the copyright?
3. If you collaborate with videographers, photographers, or other artists to document your work, have you clarified in writing who owns the resulting images?
4. Where does your work live publicly — on social media, a website, in galleries? What is your exposure, and who has had access to it? Is there a paper trail that would support an access argument?
5. When you see your style or a concept you originated appearing in someone else's work, how do you distinguish between legitimate inspiration (the idea) and actionable copying (the expression)?
