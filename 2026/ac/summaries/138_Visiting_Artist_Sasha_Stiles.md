# 138 · Visiting Artist Process Lecture: Sasha Stiles — Crafting Systems

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Sasha Stiles, visiting artist in residence — first-generation Kalmyk-American poet, language artist, and AI researcher working at the nexus of text and technology (honored by Ars Electronica, the Lumen Prize, the Future Art Awards; featured by MoMA, Christie's, Artforum, NPR)
**Topic:** A process lecture and generative-poetry workshop on building **systems-based artwork** — how an intuition becomes an archive, a structure, a generative system, and finally a physical or screen-based manifestation

---

## Overview

This is the second of Stiles's residency sessions — billed as a hybrid between a craft talk and a generative-poetry workshop. Where the first session took a broad view of "poetry as a technology," this one digs beneath the surface to reveal *how* and *why* she works, not just *what* she makes. Her governing theme is **systems**: not narrowly as code, algorithms, or machines, but as the much older human structures for making meaning — language, poetry, writing, memory, archives, libraries — all of which emerged from a single need: to preserve, transform, and transmit meaning across time.

Stiles frames a historical arc for the artwork itself: from **object** (a container that holds the meaning an artist gives it), through the conceptual turn of the 20th century (Duchamp, Fluxus, Sol LeWitt — meaning as idea, instruction, or proposition), to the contemporary moment of **systems thinking**, where meaning is an *emergent property* arising through relationships — between artist and audience, language and memory, instruction and interpretation, one moment and the next. The artwork becomes less a container for meaning and more a *site* for meaning-making.

The session is structured around two embodied exercises and one extended case study. A spoken-word memory exercise (reading an excerpt of her poem "The E-Wasteland") demonstrates firsthand that memory is not storage but transformation — everyone hears a different poem. The case study of her project **Heart Mantras** lays out a repeatable five-stage methodology — **intuition → archive → structure → transformation → manifestation** — and she closes by turning that framework back on the participants, prompting them to sketch a latent system in their own practice.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **The artwork as a site for meaning, not a container for it** — Meaning does not reside in objects alone, or even in ideas alone. It emerges through relationships. The contemporary artist's craft lies not only in making objects but in *crafting the conditions through which meaning becomes possible*.
- **Systems are older than computers** — Language, poetry, writing, memory, and archives are all meaning-making systems. Reaching for "systems" need not mean reaching for technology; all artists already work inside cultural, linguistic, and social systems whether they acknowledge it or not.
- **Rules and constraints as artistic materials** — Working in systems does not abandon craft; it recognizes that prompts, rules, structures, procedures, and conditions are themselves the medium. Understanding how systems shape meaning may be one of the most important literacies available to artists today.
- **"I write poets, not poems"** (from *Ars Autopoetica*) — The shift from authoring discrete artifacts to authoring the *structures that generate artifacts*. The artist's role moves from making the total, finished vision to designing an environment in which something will happen — informed by the artist's voice but partly beyond their control.
- **Memory is transformation, not storage** — The spoken-word exercise proves that meaning is never transmitted intact. A poem is a site where meaning is continuously made and remade through encounter; no two readings are the same even when the text is unchanging. This is systems thinking experienced in the body, before any technology enters.
- **Data sets as living archives** — A data set is not a dead record of the past but a collection that can be *activated and thought-with in real time*. Often the idea is lurking inside the materials; gathering an archive can generate the idea rather than the reverse.
- **The durational problem of generative work** — A live, browser-based generative work shown on a monitor may be mistaken for a video or a fixed loop. Each encounter is a different artwork; the work is never repeated. The artist must decide how much of the system (the code, the prompt architecture, the data set) to make legible to the viewer.

## The Heart Mantras Methodology — Five Stages

A repeatable framework for moving from a feeling to a finished, world-facing artwork. Stiles stresses it is **not linear** — she constantly loops back, especially into the archive.

1. **Intuition** — The first nagging inkling: an unresolved question, an obsession, a tension, a feeling that won't leave you alone. For *Heart Mantras*, this was the pandemic paradox of being physically isolated yet connected through technology — and her fascination with the machine phrase "keep alive."
2. **Archive** — Gather the materials: drafts, iterations, notes, research, AI-generated variations. The archive *is* the data set — the baseline from which the system grows. (She landed on ~50–51 variations of the original poem.)
3. **Structure** — Impose relationships on the archive: prompt architectures, categories, "language buckets," diagrams. Look for what repeats, what connects, what wants to interact. This is where the system starts to emerge.
4. **Transformation** — The system runs. Stiles built a P5.js text generator drawing on her data set plus a structured prompt, calling GPT-4 Turbo to rewrite the poem endlessly, each output unique. The questions here: what persists, what changes, what survives?
5. **Manifestation** — How the system enters the world (if it needs to): live URL on monitors, exported still frames, WebM video captures, hardbound books of iterations, published prompt architecture, and engraved **mirror sculptures** (the mirrored surface as a material metaphor for input/output and the reader-text relationship).

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Sasha Stiles** — *Ars Autopoetica* (generative poem/video; source of "I write poets, not poems"); *Heart Mantra* / *Heart Mantras* (a 2020 pandemic poem grown into a generative ecosystem of text generators, videos, installations, and mirror sculptures; exhibited in Vienna and Turkey); *A Living Poem* (a dynamic grid where the work emerges from relationships between mutating fragments, never a single text)

- **Marcel Duchamp, Fluxus, Sol LeWitt** — the 20th-century conceptual turn that expanded the artwork from object to idea, instruction, score, and proposition
- **Kate Crawford & Vladan Joler** — *Calculating Empires* — a visual manifesto mapping technology and power across five centuries; cited as an artwork that engages systems critically and makes invisible systems legible
- **Tools:** P5.js (her preferred "playground" for text generators), GPT-4 Turbo (the generative co-author behind the *Heart Mantras* generator)

## Core Takeaways

- The craft of contemporary art increasingly lies in **designing conditions** rather than producing fixed objects. Rather than expressing an idea, you can build a framework that generates many possible statements.
- Begin with **intuition, not technology** — the obsession or question that won't leave you alone. *Heart Mantras* began with a short, human, deliberately simple poem written in a lonely moment, not with code.
- **Go to the archive when you need energy.** Materials at your fingertips are often where the idea is hiding. Treat data sets as living collections to think with, not as records of the past.
- **Structure is where systems emerge** — by looking for what repeats, connects, and wants to interact across your materials, you begin to organize them into something that can do more than the sum of its parts.
- A single system can have **many manifestations** — live, printed, sculptural, performative — and you can productively resist the assumption that a generative work must always *look* digital or dynamic.
- The process is **non-linear and alive.** You constantly return to earlier stages — adding to the archive, excising a sample, tweaking the prompt. Yet sometimes code behaves like "wet paint": you change it so much you can't return to yesterday's version, and you must decide whether to preserve versions or move forward irrevocably.

## Workshop Exercises (Turning the Framework on Your Own Practice)

After walking through *Heart Mantras*, Stiles prompts participants to sketch a latent system in their own work — moving through the same five stages:

1. **Intuition** — What won't leave you alone? What recurring question, tension, memory, or process do you keep returning to?
2. **Archive** — What material already exists around that intuition? (Texts, images, sketches, emails, conversations, dream journals, family stories.)
3. **Structure** — What relationships arise within that archive? What speaks to what? What categories, motifs, or associations emerge?
4. **Transformation** — Imagine the material moving through a system. What changes, repeats, survives, mutates? (Can be literal — a generator — or analog, like a painter returning to the same landscape across seasons.)
5. **Manifestation** — How does the system become an experience — or does it even need to? Poem, performance, publication, installation, generator, video, sculpture, or something else?

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Where are there systems *already implicit* in your practice — recurring procedures, rule sets, or constraints you follow without naming them? What would change if you named and designed them deliberately?
2. Is your work a container for a meaning you've fixed, or a site where meaning emerges through the viewer's encounter? Which do you want it to be?
3. What is the archive your work draws on — and could gathering that archive more deliberately *generate* your next idea rather than merely document the last one?
4. If your work is durational or generative, how legible do you make the underlying system to the viewer? What is gained or lost by revealing the prompt, the code, or the data set?
5. Stiles treats some code "like wet paint" and preserves versions of other work. In your own practice, when do you want a fallback and when do you want to commit irreversibly to the next state?
