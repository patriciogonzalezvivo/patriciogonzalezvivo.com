# 105 · Machines Learn to See

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Dr. Paulina Rood (art historian)
**Topic:** How the mechanisms of vision have changed across history — from linear perspective to photography, film, television, and digital image-making — and how artists have responded at each shift

---

## Overview

Rather than following a strict chronological timeline, this lecture moves across what Dr. Rood calls "regimes of vision" — distinct cultural and technological moments that each reorganized how humans see, what counts as real, and who holds the power to define both. The through-line is that seeing is never neutral: every era has its own optical order, and artists have consistently positioned themselves either to reinforce or expose that order.

The lecture begins in the Renaissance, where Brunelleschi's invention of linear perspective placed the individual human observer at the center of a calculable, frameable world — a shift that enabled not only artistic representation but also cartography, colonization, and capitalist spatial logic. It then moves into the 19th and 20th centuries, where the mechanical eye of photography and film shattered the monopoly of the human eye by revealing what is too fast, too small, too hidden, or too distant for unaided perception. Artists from Muybridge to Man Ray to Harold Edgerton exploited this "optical unconscious" (Walter Benjamin's term) to expose hidden structures in reality.

The third movement covers photography's philosophical paradox (Roland Barthes's "this-has-been," the frozen moment that is also already a death), the overproduction of images, and Guy Debord's "society of the spectacle," where images no longer record events but produce them. The lecture culminates in the digital age and Lev Manovich's principles of new media — numerical representation, modularity, automation, variability, and transcoding — showing how images have become generative, modular data rather than fixed records, and proposing that the task of artists today is not to produce images but to interrupt the regimes of vision that produce us.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Optical order** — The culturally specific logic that governs how seeing operates in a given era; not biological but constructed
- **Linear perspective (Brunelleschi, c.1420)** — Mathematical system converging to a vanishing point; placed the viewer at the center of a measurable, ownable world; enabled surveillance, cartography, colonial domination
- **View from above as domination** — Leonardo da Vinci's Imola map (1502); seeing from above as a form of power over terrain and people
- **Optical unconscious (Walter Benjamin)** — Photography exposes structures within vision that the human eye cannot consciously perceive
- **The Kino-Eye (Dziga Vertov)** — The camera as a more perfect eye than the human eye; capable of revealing hidden truths
- **Photography's paradox (Roland Barthes)** — Every photograph is simultaneously "here it is" (present) and "this has been" (past, already lost); photography's essence is death
- **Society of the spectacle (Guy Debord)** — A world in which everything directly lived has migrated into representation; the image no longer records the event, it is the event
- **Montage / Intellectual montage (Sergei Eisenstein)** — Meaning emerges not from individual images but from the collision between them
- **Simulacra (Jean Baudrillard)** — Copies that no longer refer to originals; reality is replaced by representation
- **Database logic (Lev Manovich)** — In the digital era, images are modular units that can be stored, sorted, recombined, and generated; meaning arises from structure and correlation
- **Manovich's five principles of new media** — Numerical representation, modularity, automation, variability, transcoding
- **Medium is the message (Marshall McLuhan)** — Media are not neutral channels; they are environments that shape perception

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Filippo Brunelleschi** — Florence Baptistery perspective demonstration (c.1420) — Origins of linear perspective
- **Leonardo da Vinci** — Imola map (1502) — Bird's-eye cartography as a tool of military control
- **Eadweard Muybridge** — Horse-in-motion photographs (1878) — First proof that the human eye cannot resolve fast motion
- **Andreas Gursky** — Stock exchange / factory / landscape photographs — Adopts the elevated viewpoint of capitalist power to expose it
- **Mark Lombardi** — Hand-drawn political/financial network diagrams — Visibility as political weapon
- **David Hockney** — Photographic collages / joiners — Rejects single-point perspective
- **Dziga Vertov** — *Man with a Movie Camera* — Coined "kino-eye"
- **Man Ray** — Rayographs — Objects placed directly on light-sensitive paper; image-making without a lens
- **Harold Edgerton** — Stroboscopic flash photography — Froze events faster than human perception
- **Michael Wesely** — Open-shutter photography (exposures lasting years) — Buildings rise and vanish in a single frame
- **Trevor Paglen** — Photographs of secret satellite bases, undersea internet cables — Points the camera at what surveillance cameras try to hide
- **Erik Kessels** — *24 Hours in Photos* — 350,000 Flickr images printed and poured into a gallery
- **Thomas Demand** — Reconstructs crime scenes from cardboard, photographs them, destroys the model
- **Richard Prince** — Re-photographed Marlboro cowboy advertisements
- **Sergei Eisenstein** — *Battleship Potemkin* / Odessa Steps sequence — Intellectual montage
- **Christian Marclay** — *The Clock* (2010, MoMA) — 24-hour video composed of film clips featuring clocks
- **Andy Warhol** — *Empire* (1964) — Eight-hour static shot of the Empire State Building
- **Nam June Paik** — *Magnet TV*, *TV Buddha*, *Electronic Superhighway* — Revealed broadcast image as fragile electronic code
- **Richard Serra** — *Television Delivers People* (1973) — Text: television delivers audiences to advertisers
- **Nancy Burson** — Digital face-morphing work including *Warhead I* — Merged Reagan/Brezhnev/Mitterrand into a collective portrait of nuclear power
- **Lev Manovich** — *Soft Cinema* — Database logic applied to film
- **Cory Arcangel** — *Super Mario Clouds* — Hacked Super Mario cartridge to show only the scrolling sky

## Core Takeaways

- Seeing is never neutral: every era has its own optical order, and artists consistently position themselves to reinforce or expose it.
- Photography's "optical unconscious" revealed structures within reality the human eye cannot perceive — making machines, from the start, different kinds of seers.
- Images no longer simply record events — in the society of the spectacle, they produce them. The question is no longer what images show but what they do.
- Database logic has replaced sequential narrative as the dominant structure of digital images — modularity, recombination, and generation are now the operative logic.
- The artist's task today is not simply to produce images but to interrupt the regimes of vision that produce us.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. What "optical order" does your work inhabit — whose vantage point, whose frame, whose capacity to see does it assume or question?
2. Does your practice reveal something that ordinary vision cannot? What is your equivalent of Edgerton's strobe light or Muybridge's camera array?
3. Are you producing images or interrupting image regimes? What is the difference in your specific practice?
4. How does database logic — modularity, recombination, variability — operate in your work, intentionally or not?
5. What would it mean to point your practice at what is being systematically hidden rather than at what is already visible?
