# 144 · Interview with Gallerist Stephen Santoro

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Haiver (program instructor) in conversation with Stephen Santoro (gallerist and curator, Virtue Fine Art, Boca Raton, FL)
**Topic:** How a small secondary-market gallery actually operates — collecting as a discipline, the economics of a boutique gallery, authentication and pricing of prints and editions, the mechanics of art fairs, and what it takes for a gallerist to champion a living artist

---

## Overview

Stephen Santoro is 28 and has spent roughly eleven years in the art world, the last five or six of them professionally, at his family's gallery, Virtue Fine Art in Boca Raton — a two-person, boutique operation that deals primarily in prints and multiples on the secondary market (Warhol, Lichtenstein, Katz, Stella, Basquiat, Ruscha) and, more recently, in younger living artists. The interview is recorded from the Hamptons, where Santoro is about to walk onto an art-fair floor for a full weekend. What makes the conversation valuable to artists is that it is told entirely from the *other side of the table*: the person who buys, prices, frames, ships, hangs, and sells the work explains what he is actually thinking, and where artists help or hurt themselves in that process.

Santoro's origin story is collecting. As a kid he collected anything — baseball cards, toys — and fell in love with the way a collection "marks a moment in time." That gamified, resource-constrained instinct ("I don't have unlimited money, I don't have unlimited time … how do I create this niche that becomes my collection?") becomes his whole professional philosophy. He credits keeping "the inner child" — asking the naïve question *why do I like this?* — as the thing that keeps his eye honest and his bias in check. From studying the archived CVs of dead artists he became "obsessed with the progression of an artist," and with the strange fact that a *career* in art is treated as odd where a career in sports or tech is not. That oddness, he says, "is the beauty of art."

The through-line of everything he says is **trust built through straightforwardness over a long time horizon.** He hates the "games" of the old art world — inquiry-only prices, forced bundling, gatekeeping for access — and runs against a "country club mentality" in favor of education, access, and plain answers. His central promise to every artist he takes on is deliberately, almost radically, modest: "I can't promise you sales. I can't promise you awareness. I can't promise you discovery … But what I can promise is that I will attempt at trying to make you 1% more successful than you are now." The gallery's job, as he frames it, is the enormous, underappreciated labor *around* the object — buying, framing, provenance, presentation, marketing, logistics — that "gives the work its justice."

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Collecting as marking a moment in time** — A collection is a record of who you were thinking like, what you discovered, and who you met at a given point. Taste evolves; the collection freezes the state of that taste. This is a lens for artists too: the work you make and place now is a snapshot someone can later "follow the arc" of.
- **Keep the inner child; ask "why do I like this?"** — Santoro's method for training the eye is to let the naïve, pre-educated reaction surface first, then interrogate it with sophistication. "Keeping my bias kind of away from me" comes from being honest that sometimes "I like it because of X, Y, and Z" isn't the same as "this is actually good."
- **Why *you* care beats consensus** — "What makes art interesting is why I care about it. Not because why a bunch of people care about it … more importantly, why the artist cares about it." If the artist has put real thought, effort, and time into *why* they are doing this and embodied that energy in the object, "it's going to be a lot easier to then market and sell." Conviction in the object is the precondition for the sale, not the other way around.
- **Consensus is "really not anything"** — Art history is full of artists ignored "for a decade or two" and later resurfaced (the feminist recovery of overlooked women painters is Haiver's example). Don't confine ideas to what's currently popular or saleable; make work available in a format that can be *rediscovered by the right person at the right moment.*
- **The boutique model** — Virtue is two people, so choices are forced. Their answer: 3–4 art fairs a year instead of a permanent exhibition program, a low-cost home base (Boca) instead of NY/LA/London, and a hedge-your-bets group-show approach instead of solo booths.
- **Straightforwardness as competitive advantage** — "A lot of people in the art world like to play games. I hate it personally. I just want information." Plain answers, no hoops, no forced bundling. Over time this "strengthens relationships with people who buy from you" — and they come back to *sell* through you too.
- **"What do you want for it?"** — Rather than negotiating percentages on secondary work, Santoro asks the seller to name their number. If it's fair, he pays it or marks it up and pays it on sale. Percentages make people feel cheated after the fact ("you sold it for this, I deserve X"); a clear number keeps the relationship clean.
- **Both parties educated = long relationship** — "We advise every person we're doing a deal with to be educated." Ripping people off ends the business; mutual knowledge lets him "nurture the relationship for a long time." He tells the Alex Katz story: a print sold for $10–15k, worth $60k five years later, resold for the original owner at $50k — "a long-term, fun, beautiful thing."
- **Abundance as a positive** — Editions and multiples are "all the same," which the art world reads as a negative; Santoro reads it as an advantage. Sameness makes authentication trivial (you compare the thing in front of you to the thing you know) and keeps a healthy, liquid market where the same image resurfaces and resells. More fairs, more artists, more shows = more intrigue and opportunity, not less.
- **The 1% promise** — Santoro's explicit pitch to living artists: no promises of sales, awareness, or discovery, only that he will try to make them "1% more successful than you are now," and grow together over the long run so the work is "mutually beneficial."

## How the Gallery Actually Works (Practical Notes for Artists)

- **Why Boca, not New York:** (1) cost of operating is far lower in a capital-intensive business; (2) a real local base of collectors with second homes and appetite; (3) little competition, so a region to "hone in on." Bonus: they get to *install in homes*, see what else a collector owns, and thereby learn the collector's taste well enough to target future works to them.
- **Physical space vs. fairs vs. online:** For Virtue, art fairs *are* the core practice — ephemeral, curated, face-to-face, a reason to invite people and deepen existing relationships. With high rents and only two people, fairs beat a permanent gallery program. The website is a "constant tinker."
- **Buying is the fun part — and the dangerous part.** "It can screw you if you buy wrong." Every piece you buy occupies a wall and a show — an *opportunity cost* against what else you could have shown — plus framing and sometimes restoration (many prints are from the 60s–80s). There is "a whole philosophy to buying" that sets up the sell.
- **Authentication is pattern recognition earned over time.** Years of looking teach you where a work "should be coming from," what the COAs (certificates of authenticity) look like, what a signature should be, whether a date is missing where one should be. When unsure, "snap it over to the foundation or to a gallerist that specializes in that artist." Editions make this easier: you compare against a known copy. (Haiver ties this to Malcolm Gladwell's *Blink* — expert snap-judgment as trained intuition.)
- **~80% of offered work is declined** — usually not because it's bad but because it's outside their expertise/scope. Declining fast and redirecting the seller ("bring it to this auction house") respects everyone's time. "Time is the most valuable thing" and "there are a lot of time-wasters in the art world."
- **Curating a booth:** Groups of artists, not solos — "we want to appeal to as many people as we can" and hedge the bet. Grouped by wall, in "sections or sets" (a few pieces per artist) so collectors can envision the work in their own kitchen or living room. Pair sizing, medium, and artist to booth layout. A single eclectic showstopper (a Frank Stella wall sculpture that fits nothing) is fine — "isn't it freaking amazing?" beats forced cohesion.
- **Sold work stays on the wall.** Leaving a sold piece up is a "high signal" that the thing is desirable and sparks conversation; taking it down feels like "taking back what we once saw." Their diverse, flexible inventory lets them do this — a solo-artist booth can't.
- **Logistics: "there should be no art emergencies."** With nine months' lead time and proper planning (dates, signed contracts, framing deadlines, shipping windows), last-minute crises are inexcusable. Two-day installs; a couple of trusted workers; keep original boxes so packing reverses cleanly; "art bags" you slide work into are his single biggest unlock. Installing is harder than de-installing — everything straight, plexi polished, hung 60" to center. Unsold work goes back to the gallery between shows.

## Do / Don't for Artists (From the Gallerist's Side)

- **Don't pitch your work at a fair booth.** "A fair is not a good opportunity for an artist to pitch their work." Exhibitors are there to sell what's on the wall; a 15-minute unsolicited pitch makes them "cringe." **Do** drop a card or send a short email after ("I stopped in, I love what you put on") — that reads as professional.
- **Do find your one sentence.** Respond to *their* curiosity rather than leading with a monologue. Haiver's model: a single sentence that generates "tell me more" — used only when the gallerist isn't busy and the booth isn't mobbed. Good things (a residency invite, an introduction) come from short, curious exchanges, not audition-style pitches.
- **Do put your name on the wall.** From the visitor side, findable artist names (label on the wall, salon-style with white space) let people photograph and remember the work later — a small presentation decision with real professional consequence.
- **Do treat the frame as part of the artwork.** Presentation — frame, spacing, lighting, what's written about it, who's brought to see it — is a huge, "underrepresented and underappreciated" part of what makes an object read. Don't simply surrender that decision.
- **Do put in the reps at fairs.** Even without sales, doing (or being in) a fair teaches how people interpret your work, what a good frame looks like, how presentation changes reading. That experience is "invaluable" and hard to fake.
- **Do build a shipping paper trail** (Haiver's advice, endorsed): photograph the work going into packaging, packaged from all sides, on receipt, after opening, and again when re-wrapping — timestamped — so damage can be located to the fair wall vs. the shipper and restitution sought. Protect corners; mark which way is up.
- **Do hold up your word.** Santoro's clearest emotional note: people increasingly "are not holding up their word," and it "saddens" him because shows take months and artworks take months or years. Reliability is what makes people want to work with you again.

## Core Takeaways

- **Conviction is the engine of the market.** A gallerist sells the *why* — his own and the artist's. Work made from genuine, thought-through reasons is easier to represent than work chasing what's saleable.
- **Straightforwardness compounds.** Plain answers, fair numbers, mutual education, and holding your word are not niceties — they are what create a decades-long, mutually profitable relationship. Games win the transaction and lose the relationship.
- **Consensus is not the verdict.** Overlooked work resurfaces when the right person finds it. Make the work; make it available and legible; don't confine your ideas to this quarter's taste.
- **The labor around the object gives the work its justice.** Buying, provenance, framing, presentation, logistics, and marketing are an enormous, underappreciated craft — and the frame and presentation are part of the artwork's meaning, not an afterthought to surrender.
- **A fair is a selling floor, not a pitch floor.** Read the room, find your one curiosity-generating sentence, follow up afterward, and put your name where it can be found. Put in the reps even when nothing sells.
- **"No art emergencies."** Professionalism is planning backward from the opening. Reliability and communication are what a gallerist is actually evaluating in a long-term partner.
- **Championing new work is lonely — and that's the job.** Representing "unfamiliar, rejected, or misunderstood" artists means being "out there and lonely," believing before others do, and hoping the contribution is canonized by "future generations," not you. The honest promise is 1% better, growing together over the long run.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Santoro sells the *why* — the artist's own reason for making the work. For a body like Hybrids or Santos, can you state, in one plain sentence a gallerist could repeat to a collector, *why* you make it — not what it's about, but why it matters enough that you keep doing it?
2. Your practice spans oil painting, plotter drawings, shader/generative code, astronomical instruments, and NFT work. From a boutique gallerist's "hedge the bet, group by wall" logic, which two or three of these read as a coherent *set* a dealer could place together — and which one is the eclectic Frank Stella piece that only earns its wall by being undeniable?
3. Editions and multiples let Santoro turn "sameness" into a liquid, resurfacing market. Your plotter and generative work can exist as editions or uniques. What does treating a series as an *edition* — priced, numbered, authenticatable — do to how a secondary market could carry it, versus insisting every output is a unique?
4. "There should be no art emergencies." Take your most recent show or fair deadline and reconstruct the backward schedule (framing, shipping, contracts). Where did the real emergencies come from, and which were avoidable with earlier planning?
5. Santoro's honest promise is "1% more successful than you are now," grown over the long run. If you were pitching your own practice to a gallerist, what is the single realistic, non-hype claim you could make about where you are and where a partnership could take you in five years — and does your current body of work back it up?
