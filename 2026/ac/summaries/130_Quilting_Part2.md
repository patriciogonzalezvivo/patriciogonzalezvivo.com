# 130 · Art History Lecture: Quilting Part 2 — Quilting as Idea

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Dr. Cristin McKnight Sethi
**Topic:** Quilting as concept, raw material, portraiture, memorial, and activism in contemporary art practice

---

## Overview

Where Part 1 established the history and technique of quilting as a tradition, Part 2 pivots to "quilting as idea" — exploring how contemporary artists use quilting not as a craft form but as a conceptual framework, a raw material, a form of portraiture, a memorial structure, and an instrument of social critique. The central move of the lecture is a shift from quilt as noun to quilt as verb: from the object to the logic, the process, the political weight, and the conceptual possibilities that quilting carries.

The lecture opens with Robert Rauschenberg's *Bed* (1955), in which a friend's log cabin quilt becomes the canvas for gestural abstraction. This sets up the essential question: when a quilt is taken out of its domestic context and placed on a gallery wall, it becomes a new kind of object — still carrying the memory and residue of its previous life, but now doing entirely different work. Pasita Abad's trapuntos — stuffed, quilted, mixed-media paintings that collapse the boundary between canvas and textile — and Sanford Biggers's interventions into antique quilts (cutting them, mounting them on plywood, inscribing constellations onto them) both embody this tension. Biggers explicitly treats quilts as palimpsests: documents that have been written, erased, and written over again.

The lecture's middle section examines quilting as portraiture through Bisa Butler's vibrantly colored fabric portraits of Black historical figures, then extends into Faith Ringgold's story quilts, which add text to the visual field to construct elaborate narratives that critique Western art history and center Black women as protagonists. The final section covers quilting as memorial (the AIDS Quilt; Hmong story cloths as records of displacement) and as activism (LaMerci Frazier's kente-inspired quilt honoring Dr. King; Dr. Carolyn Mazloomi's politically charged quilts).

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Quilting as idea vs. quilting as object:** Artists use quilting conceptually — as a motif, a thematic frame, or a political vocabulary — without necessarily making a quilt in the traditional sense.
- **Quilt as noun → quilt as verb:** A shift from a thing to put on a bed toward a practice, a logic, an approach to combining disparate elements with stitching and layers.
- **Combines (Rauschenberg):** Attaching found objects (including quilts, bedding, taxidermy, photographs) to a canvas to collapse the boundary between art and everyday life.
- **Trapunto:** A quilting technique involving stuffed, raised areas; Pasita Abad uses it as the basis for mixed-media paintings incorporating beads, buttons, shells, and paint.
- **Palimpsest (Biggers):** A document written over but retaining traces of previous writing. Biggers uses antique quilts as palimpsests — objects that carry the histories of previous owners and makers.
- **Social life of things (Arjun Appadurai):** Material objects have biographies and a degree of agency, carrying the histories of their previous uses, owners, and contexts.
- **Quilting as portraiture:** Using the quilt form to render human figures with more emotional and symbolic resonance than photography alone.
- **Story quilts (Ringgold):** Quilts that combine visual imagery with text panels to create complex, often fictional narratives; the border is a traditional quilt block pattern; the interior tells the story.
- **Quilting as memorial:** The AIDS Quilt (NAMES Project, 1985) as the paradigmatic example — collaborative, diverse in technique, each panel a biography.
- **Quilting as activism:** Using the quilt format to make political arguments; challenging dominant historical narratives.

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Robert Rauschenberg** — *Bed* (1955) — friend's log cabin quilt used as canvas for gestural painting; quilt as raw material and ground
- **Pasita Abad** (b. 1946, Philippines; d. 2004) — *Bakongo* series; *Cross Cultural Dressing* (1993); *Lady Liberty* (1992) — trapunto paintings: stuffed, quilted, mixed-media canvases; retrospective at Walker Art Center 2023
- **Sanford Biggers** — *Hidden Cities* quilts (2009, Philadelphia) — antique quilts with constellation/map inscriptions linking Underground Railroad stops; called quilts "portals" that hold material memory
- **Bisa Butler** — *The Storm, the Whirlwind, and the Earthquake* (Frederick Douglass portrait, 2020); *Don't Tread on Me, God Damn, Let's Go, the Harlem Hellfighters* (2021) — pieced and painted fabric portraits using symbolically chosen color and pattern
- **Faith Ringgold** — *Dancing at the Louvre* (French Collection series); *Picasso's Studio* quilt; *The Sunflowers Quilting Bee at Arles* — story quilts combining text and image to place Black women at the center of art history
- **NAMES Project / AIDS Quilt** (initiated 1985 by Cleve Jones, first displayed 1987 National Mall) — now nearly 50,000 panels, 54 tons; paradigmatic collaborative memorial quilt
- **Pali Tao** — Hmong story cloth made in Thai refugee camp (1983) — memorializes village of Puglia, Laos; the act of stitching as the memorial act
- **LaMerci Frazier** — *From a Birmingham Jail* — kente-inspired quilted panels with printed photographs of Dr. King; displayed at Renwick Gallery, Smithsonian, 2024
- **Dr. Carolyn Mazloomi** — *Ruby's Courage* (Ruby Bridges, 1960 school integration); *Strange Fruit No. 3* (Billie Holiday, lynching); *Harriet Powers* (homage) — politically charged quilts; founder of Women of Color Quilters Network (1985)

## Core Takeaways

- A quilt is not only an object — it is a conceptual logic: layering, piecing, stitching disparate elements into a unified whole. This logic can be applied to any medium.
- Fiber holds memory, residue, and history in a way that paint on canvas does not. Artists who work with textiles as raw material are working with objects that already have lives.
- Fabric, pattern, and color choices in contemporary quilt-based work are never neutral — they carry symbolic, historical, and cultural weight that artists use intentionally.
- Quilting has been a vehicle for political and social critique, collective memory, and the reclamation of erased histories — especially within Black American and diasporic communities.
- The collaborative nature of quilting (the quilting bee, the NAMES Project) is intrinsic to the form and continues in contemporary practice.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Is there a domestic or craft-based material or process that you could bring into your fine art practice and use conceptually rather than literally?
2. What would it mean to treat the materials you work with as palimpsests — objects that carry prior lives? How might that change how you source or select them?
3. Are there absent or erased figures, communities, or histories that your practice is positioned to center? What format or form best suits that act of reclamation?
4. How do you think about fabric, color, and material choice as symbolic? Are there materials in your practice whose symbolic freight you have not yet fully activated?
5. What is the difference between art that documents social history and art that actively intervenes in it? Where does your work fall on that spectrum?
