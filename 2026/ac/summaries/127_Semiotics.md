**Topic:** Semiotics of art in the analytic philosophical tradition — four frameworks for understanding how artworks create meaning

## Overview

Dr. Millsop's lecture addresses a core anxiety for artists: not knowing how — or whether — their work communicates. She reframes this as a question of craft rather than chance: instead of hoping viewers "get it," artists can understand the conditions under which meaning is produced and build those conditions intentionally. The lecture introduces four analytic frameworks she calls "meaning levers," each offering a different diagnostic lens for the same work. These frameworks are not competing theories but complementary tools that can be applied simultaneously.

The session begins with Charles Sanders Peirce's theory of signs and semiosis, which maps how meaning travels from a sign vehicle through its object to an interpretant — and crucially, that this process requires an encounter: meaning is not sitting inside an object waiting to be unlocked. Peirce's three sign modes (icon, index, symbol) give artists a vocabulary for asking what kind of meaning work a given element is doing. Nelson Goodman then shifts the question from how signs point outward to how artworks can refer inward — to their own properties — through denotation, exemplification, and expression. This reframes formal choices (material, texture, scale, color) as meaning choices, not just aesthetic ones.

The third framework introduces the philosophical debate around artistic intention and framing (Wimsatt and Beardsley, E.D. Hirsch, and Noël Carroll's "moderate actual intentionalism"), landing on the practical point that intention matters when the work can support it — and that artists are responsible for building the frame (title, statement, installation, sequencing) that gives viewers an on-ramp to that intention. The fourth framework, Kendall Walton's theory of categories, argues that the category a viewer brings to a work changes which features become aesthetically meaningful — making category a lever artists can clarify, complicate, or deliberately destabilize.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Semiosis (Peirce):** The three-part process of meaning-making: sign vehicle (the word, image, or object) → object (what it stands for) → interpretant (the understanding or effect it produces). Meaning happens in the encounter, not in the object alone.
- **Icon:** A sign that means through resemblance. Not limited to realistic depiction — maps and diagrams are iconic because they preserve structural relationships.
- **Index:** A sign that means through trace, connection, or causation. A footprint, a scar, a photograph made by light bouncing off the subject — all indexical. Carries the force of evidence and residue.
- **Symbol:** A sign that means through convention and learned code. Words, flags, traffic lights. Requires the viewer to share the code; carries social memory and ideological weight.
- **Denotation (Goodman):** Reference that points outward — depicting, labeling, or standing for something external.
- **Exemplification (Goodman):** Reference that points inward — a work has a property and makes it significant. A textile that exemplifies labor; a sculpture that exemplifies fragility.
- **Expression (Goodman):** A special kind of exemplification that works metaphorically. A work does not feel sad; it metaphorically exemplifies sadness through color, rhythm, material, and composition.
- **Intentional Fallacy (Wimsatt and Beardsley):** The artist's private intention does not automatically determine meaning; the work must be able to stand on its own.
- **Moderate Actual Intentionalism (Noël Carroll):** Intention matters for meaning, but only when the work itself can support that intention. The frame — title, statement, installation, context — is how intention becomes accessible.
- **Category (Walton):** We perceive artworks as belonging to categories. Standard features are what we expect; variable features create style; contra-standard features create friction or reclassification. The category changes which features matter aesthetically.

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Morehshin Allahyari** — *Material Speculation: ISIS* (2015–16) — 3D-printed reconstructions of artifacts destroyed by ISIS, each containing an archive on a flash drive; analyzed through icon (resembles lost artifacts), index (points to actual destruction), and symbol (heritage, colonial collecting)
- **Kenyatta A.C. Hinkle** — *Ruins of Empire II* (Whitney Biennial 2024) — White House form made from earth; iconic (resembles the White House), indexical (material points to land, extraction, collapse), symbolic (presidency, national myth)
- **Diedrick Brackens** — *Bitter Attendance Drown Jubilee* (2018) — hand-woven textile; exemplifies labor, cotton (symbolically charged with Black labor and slavery), and expresses intimacy and vulnerability
- **Mark Bradford** — large-scale multimedia collage painting — urban materials exemplify density, accumulation, and pressure; denotation of networks
- **Amelia Ulman** — *Excellences and Perfections* (2014) — staged Instagram performance revealed as scripted; meaning depends entirely on which frame the viewer brings
- **Sonia Almeida** — *Search Engines* (2021) — mobile freestanding panels; refuses stable category (painting? sculpture? performance? furniture?); category instability as the meaning

## Core Takeaways

- Meaning is not inside an object; it happens in an encounter between sign vehicle, object, and interpretant.
- Most artworks operate across all three sign modes simultaneously — ask what is doing the most meaning work, not which single mode applies.
- Formal choices are meaning choices: material, texture, scale, color, and process are not neutral carriers but active meaning-makers through exemplification and expression.
- An artist's intention matters, but only when the work and its frame support it. Build the on-ramp — title, statement, installation — so viewers can access your intention.
- The category a viewer brings changes everything they see. You can clarify your category, complicate it, or make its instability the meaning itself.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. In a recent work, what is doing the most meaning work — resemblance (icon), trace or connection (index), or convention (symbol)?
2. What properties does your work make meaningful — and which are just along for the ride? Are there properties (material, scale, process, texture) you are not yet activating?
3. Where does your intention live in the work itself, and what frame have you built to make it accessible to a viewer who has no access to your head?
4. What category does a first-time viewer bring to your work? Is that the category you want?
5. Are you working between categories on purpose? Could that in-between space be where the meaning actually lives?
