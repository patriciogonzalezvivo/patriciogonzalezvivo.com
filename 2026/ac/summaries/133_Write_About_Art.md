# 133 · How to Write About Your Art — Part 1

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Haiver (program instructor)
**Topic:** Writing the artist statement — unlocking voice, overcoming resistance, and building the foundational "What is it?" paragraph

---

## Overview

This lecture-workshop hybrid treats writing not as an administrative obligation but as an artistic practice in its own right — one that can be developed through repetition, ritual, and structured exercises. The instructor begins by dismantling two myths: that art speaks for itself without words, and that artist statements require a specialized, academic register. The real argument is that writing is how other people gain access to your work, and resisting it is a form of self-imposed insularity.

The lecture is organized around a framework borrowed from Gilda Williams's *How to Write About Contemporary Art* (2014): every piece of art writing must answer three questions — What is it? What might this mean? Why does this matter? Part 1 focuses almost entirely on the first question, building toward a "What is it?" opening paragraph. This is grounded in a Pavlovian approach to creative ritual (playlist-triggered writing sessions), a word-list brainstorming method, and the principle that specificity and concreteness are the primary virtues of good art writing.

The workshop portions use timed exercises with music to move students past blank-page paralysis. The instructor models what good and bad art writing looks like, uses a real artist statement (Brit Ransom's *Layered Sites*, shown at New Museum/New Ink in 2024) as a positive example, and contrasts it with anonymized curatorial jargon. The session closes by pointing toward Part 2, which will cover the "What might this mean?" and "Why does this matter?" questions, and introduces the concept of the thesis statement as the marker of artistic maturity.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **The three-question framework** (from Gilda Williams, *How to Write About Contemporary Art*, 2014) — (1) What is it? What does it look like? How is it made? (2) What might this mean? How does form carry meaning? (3) Why does this matter? So what?
- **Pavlovian writing ritual** — Deliberately associating a specific trigger (a playlist, a time of day, a cup of tea) with the act of writing to lower the activation energy required to start.
- **Word list / phrase list brainstorming** — Before drafting sentences, generate categories of traits and themes that apply to the work, then list 15+ words and phrases under each. This fertilizes the ground and pushes past clichés without self-censorship.
- **Quantity produces quality** (Ray Bradbury) — Volume of output in early drafts is more important than precision; editing comes later.
- **The "grandmother test"** for the "how" — Explain process and technique at the level accessible to an interested non-practitioner. The "how" is only one-third of the "What is it?" question.
- **Specificity and concreteness as primary virtues** — Vague writing is boring, confusing, and fails to generate the next sentence. Every sentence should be traceable to this specific artist and not copy-pasteable into anyone else's statement.
- **Audience calibration** — The writer must decide in advance who the audience is (curator, general public, fellow practitioners, collectors) and tune language accordingly. Career level and career goals determine the appropriate audience.
- **The "latch point"** — A phrase or sentence in the statement that gives a viewer a foothold to enter the work and begin their own engagement with it.
- **Collectors invest in people and stories, not objects** — The artist statement is part of the narrative infrastructure that makes collecting meaningful.
- **Art writing as hospitality** — An artist statement is an open door, not a fence. "You tell me what it is" is an act of exclusion.

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Brit Ransom** — *Layered Sites* (2024, New Museum / New Ink Demo festival) — cited as a model of clear, concrete "What is it?" writing; the statement immediately clarifies that the work consists of acrylic scale models of three specific abolitionist historic sites
- **Helen Frankenthaler** — Mentioned as an example of a formative visual influence an artist might trace in their own work history
- **Edgar Degas** — Quoted: "Art is not what you see, but what you make others see" — cited to reinforce the communicative purpose of the artist statement
- **Pablo Neruda** — Quoted: "All paths lead to the same goal: to convey what we are" — cited as the emotional foundation of all writing
- **Jerry Saltz** — *How to Be an Artist* (2020, based on a New York Magazine essay) — cited multiple times for plain-language advice: don't use art jargon, write how you talk, don't try to write smart
- **Vicky Krohn-Ambrose** — *Art Write: The Writing Guide for Visual Artists* — cited for: "Just as an artist toils with powers of perception to create a work of art, the viewer toils with their own powers of perception to see it"
- **Cormac McCarthy** — Quoted: "Teaching writing is a hustle" — used to open the lecture with irreverence about prescriptive writing advice
- **Carl Sandburg** — Quoted: "Beware of advice, even this" — used as a caveat that every artist's process is different
- **Mary Karr** — Quoted: "People who say they write for themselves write for no one" — cited to establish that audience is essential
- **Michael Chabon** — Cited as an example of a writer with a distinctive process (writing 11pm–3am) that resonated personally with the instructor
- **John Falk** — *Museum Visitor Experience* — cited for the statistic that practitioners represent only one-fifth of museum visitors; therefore most viewers are non-practitioners with different needs

## Core Takeaways

- An artist statement is not optional vanity — curators, journalists, collectors, museum educators, and social media users all rely on your text to represent your work in your absence.
- The three questions every piece of art writing must answer: What is it? What might it mean? Why does it matter? Part 1 focuses on the first.
- Start with a word list and phrase list, push past clichés, never self-censor in the gathering phase. Editing is a separate activity.
- The "What is it?" opening paragraph should be specific, concrete, sensory, and sequenced by importance — the most essential element first. It should stand alone well enough that a reader who stops halfway still leaves with useful clarity.
- Write in your own voice, at the level you would use to explain the work to someone smart but outside your field. If you bore yourself, you bore your audience.
- Avoid art jargon, over-explaining the "how," and impersonal academic register. These are the three most common failure modes of bad art writing.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Who is the specific audience you are writing for right now, given where your career is and where you want it to go? How does that audience shape the vocabulary, tone, and length of your statement?
2. Take a work you have been struggling to write about — what are the five most specific, non-generic words you would use to describe just what it looks like? Can you build a sentence from those?
3. When you read your current artist statement aloud, which sentences sound like you and which ones sound like someone else? What would it take to rewrite the latter?
4. What is the earliest visual or emotional influence you can trace in your current body of work? Does your writing acknowledge it?
5. If a museum educator had to give a 5-minute gallery talk about one of your works using only your written text, what would they say? Is that what you want them to say?
