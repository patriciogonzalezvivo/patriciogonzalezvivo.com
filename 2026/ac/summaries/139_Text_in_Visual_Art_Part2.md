## Overview

Picking up where Part 1 left off (Dada, Cubism, Constructivism, Futurism, Surrealism), Dr. Raffel moves into the contemporary period, organized around three overlapping currents that all use text: **conceptual art**, **feminist art**, and **AIDS-activist art**. She frames the whole period under the umbrella of **postmodernism** — a useful if imperfect catch-all for the Western movements (conceptual, pop, feminist, Fluxus) that emerged from the 1960s to the early 1990s as a backlash against **Abstract Expressionism** and its myth of the white-male artist-genius (epitomized by Jackson Pollock).

Two postmodern strategies recur throughout: **appropriation** (borrowing, copying, or remixing existing imagery and distribution networks to question authorship and blur high/low culture) and **pluralism** (rejecting the idea of one correct way to make art, expanding a narrowly white-male history to represent a multiplicity of voices). These parallel the feminist, civil rights, environmental, and LGBTQ+ movements of the era, and the art world's postwar center of gravity shifts from Paris to New York.

The lecture's through-line is that text in art is never neutral — its size, font, placement, and especially its *location* (gallery wall, magazine ad space, Coca-Cola bottle, billboard, subway car) are themselves arguments. A second through-line is the conceptual fascination with **delegating the artwork to a third party** — Weiner's patrons, Baldessari's students, Kaltenbach's institutions, Meireles's thousands of anonymous distributors — which dematerializes the object and redistributes authorship.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Postmodernism** — A catch-all for Western art c. 1960–early 1990s reacting against Abstract Expressionism's rigid hierarchies. Centered in but not limited to New York City.
- **Appropriation** — Borrowing, copying, or remixing existing imagery or distribution systems from art history or popular culture; questions authorship and the "genius" of the artist; blurs high and low culture.
- **Pluralism** — The rejection of any single correct way to make art; an effort to expand and revise a white, male art history to include more races, genders, and voices.
- **Dematerialization of the object** (conceptual art) — Prioritizing the idea over the physical object; at the extreme, making a work that exists in no physical form at all. Leads to questioning authorship and "the hand of the artist."
- **The work made by a preset system or third party** — From Minimalism's industrial fabrication (Donald Judd) to conceptualism's delegation of execution; the *idea* and the *choice*, not the making, are what matter.
- **Sign types applied to objects** (Peirce, carried from Part 1) — A photograph of a chair is an *iconic* sign; a dictionary definition is a *symbolic* sign; the chair itself is the thing. All sign systems are inherently imprecise.
- **The artist's book** — Working against art as a one-of-a-kind precious object: a more-or-less mass-printed limited edition rather than a singular painting or sculpture.
- **Public distribution as strategy** — Bypassing the gallery to reach a broad, non-initiated audience via mass media: magazine ads, billboards, buses, subway cars, currency, consumer products. Decentralized, repeated, and hard to censor.
- **Operating within systems to work against them** — Raffel's own critical lens: the most effective activist artists co-opt existing commercial and ideological distribution systems (advertising, currency, product circulation) to subvert them from inside.

## Artists & Works Referenced

**Conceptual art (mid-1960s–mid-1970s)**
- **Sol LeWitt** — coined the public prominence of "conceptual art" in a 1967 article; a major figure in the movement
- **Joseph Kosuth** — *One and Three Chairs* (1965) — an actual chair, a full-scale photograph (iconic sign), and a blown-up dictionary definition (symbolic sign); demonstrates that all sign systems are imprecise. Also definition paintings (e.g., on "meaning"). Sold to MoMA when he was 20.
- **Lawrence Weiner** — *Earth to Earth, Ashes to Ashes, Dust to Dust* — text-only, site-specific; sold the *rights* to reconstruct; his 1968 declaration of three equally valid options (the artist may construct it; it may be fabricated; it need not be built). "Once you know about a work of mine, you own it."
- **Kay Rosen** — bold text-based wall works treating language as a found object (*ADD → AND → END*; *Something Happened* with an extra "O" making "coroner"); more in the driver's seat over typography and placement than Weiner
- **John Baldessari** — the *Cremation Project* (burning his paintings from 1953–66); text-only paintings (*What Is Painting*; *Everything Is Purged…*); the artist's book *Ingres and Other Parables* ("historical mispronounced sounds like hysterical"); *I Will Not Make Any More Boring Art* (Nova Scotia students execute the instruction)
- **Stephen Kaltenbach** — *Time Capsules* (industrial, minimalist, engraved with handling instructions; the three famous ones gifted to Bruce Nauman, critic Barbara Rose, and MoMA — "please open this capsule when… I have attained national prominence"); *Art Forum* ad-space text pieces ("Tell a lie, perpetuate a hoax, start a rumor"; "Build a reputation, become a legend"), purposely obtuse, subverting commercial space
- **Cildo Meireles** — *Insertions into Ideological Circuits* (1970, MoMA's *Information* show) — Coca-Cola bottles silkscreened with "Yankees Go Home" and instructions, exploiting Brazil's returnable-bottle program to evade the military dictatorship's censors; also stamped currency. Decentralized, anonymous, hard to suppress; a bridge from conceptualism to activism.

**Feminist art (1960s–1980s)**
- **Linda Nochlin** — *Why Have There Been No Great Women Artists?* (1971) — locates the absence in institutional exclusion, not talent
- **Guerrilla Girls** — billboard appropriating Ingres's *Grande Odalisque* with a gorilla head and a museum-statistics caption; rejected by the Public Art Fund and a bus company, censorship amplifying its fame
- **Carolee Schneemann** — *Interior Scroll* (1975) — nude performance reading a defiant feminist manifesto from a scroll drawn from her body; "the vagina itself is reporting sexism" (Jeannie Forte)

**AIDS activism & the 1980s**
- **Keith Haring** — populist, accessible art with text; *New York Post* cut-up collages (against Murdoch's rightward shift); 5,000+ subway chalk drawings (1980–85); the Pop Shop; AIDS work for ACT UP ("Silence = Death"), safe-sex imagery, and murals (*Together We Can Stop AIDS*, Barcelona 1989). Revealed his own HIV status in *Rolling Stone* to humanize the disease.
- **General Idea** (AA Bronson, Felix Partz, Jorge Zontal) — the *AIDS* logo appropriating Robert Indiana's *LOVE*; spread "like a virus" across posters, stamps, wallpaper, billboards, and an Absolut-Vodka-style print campaign
- **Gran Fury** — *Kissing Doesn't Kill: Greed and Indifference Do* — appropriating Benetton's "All the Colors of the World" ad on buses and posters
- **Felix Gonzalez-Torres** — the Stonewall-anniversary billboard in Sheridan Square (dates, names, and places of the gay-rights movement as a memorial, not an ad); later public/private billboard works

**Contemporary coda**
- **Jonathan Allen** — ~400 *interruptions* inserting Trump and right-wing figures (and later overlaid text) into existing advertisements; reaching an international audience through Instagram rather than physical ubiquity

## Core Takeaways

- Conceptual artists used text to **prioritize the idea over the object**, pushing toward the dematerialization of the artwork and questioning authorship, the hand of the artist, and what makes art "art."
- **Language is loaded and imprecise.** Kosuth and Weiner both expose that no sign system delivers a meaning with full certainty; there is always wiggle room for the viewer's interpretation — and many of these artists deliberately hand that interpretive freedom (and the execution itself) to a third party.
- **Where you put the text is part of the meaning.** Moving text out of the gallery and into ad space, currency, product circulation, billboards, buses, and subways reaches a broader, non-initiated audience — and, when decentralized and anonymous, becomes very difficult to censor.
- **Feminist artists used text and the body** to reclaim a history written by and about white men, to subvert the male gaze, and to revalue craft and performance as serious art.
- **AIDS-activist artists borrowed the tools of advertising** — repetition, recognizable logos, public placement, image-plus-call-to-action — because a painting in a gallery could not compete with mass-media misinformation. Visibility itself was the political act.
- The most durable strategy across all three currents is to **operate within existing systems in order to work against them** — appropriating the look, the networks, and the channels of commerce and authority to subvert them from inside.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. When you use text in a work, what does its *placement and context* argue — independent of what the words say? Would the same words mean something different on a gallery wall, a product, or a billboard?
2. Conceptual artists delegated execution to a third party. Is there a part of your work you could hand to someone else — a fabricator, a viewer, an institution — and would that delegation change its meaning productively?
3. Which existing system (a distribution channel, a commercial format, a familiar image) could you *appropriate and subvert* rather than building something wholly new?
4. Is your work preaching to the choir inside the art world, or could a public, decentralized format reach an audience that has no reason to seek it out?
5. The AIDS-activist artists made visibility itself the goal. For the issues your work engages, is the most important act to *explain*, or simply to make something *visible and unavoidable*?
