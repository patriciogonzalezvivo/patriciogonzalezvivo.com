# 125 · Art History Lecture: Quilting, Part 1

**Program:** Artist Commons
**Instructor/Guest:** Dr. Kristin Sethi McKnight (curator, educator, textile historian; MCAD)
**Topic:** The practice of quilting — techniques, cross-cultural traditions, and recurring conceptual themes

---

## Overview

Dr. McKnight opens by grounding quilting etymologically: the word descends from Latin and Old French terms for a stuffed mattress or sack, with "bed covering" usage dating to the 1590s. From the start, quilts are defined by two defining characteristics — the layering and stitching together of multiple pieces of fabric, and their deep association with domestic space, comfort, and the home. These characteristics, she argues, are not fixed: artists across traditions both honor and push against them.

The lecture surveys American quilting traditions first — pieced (patchwork) quilts, whole-cloth quilts, and combined techniques — paying special attention to African-American quilting histories, the collaborative social practices of quilting bees and "quiltons," and the storytelling quilts of Harriet Powers. Dr. McKnight then moves through three Asian traditions: Bengali Kantha (running-stitched, recycled-sari quilts dense with Hindu and Buddhist symbolism), Korean Bojagi/Jogakpo (geometric pieced wrapping cloths rooted in the Joseon Dynasty), and Japanese Sashiko/Boro (functional running-stitch reinforcement on indigo-dyed farmers' garments, connected to wabi-sabi aesthetics). Each tradition is illustrated through both historical objects and contemporary artists who extend the tradition.

The Q&A deepens the themes of material memory, the migration of stitches, the durability of pieced cloth, and the philosophical resonances between Kantha's use of worn saris and the Japanese concepts of mottainai (regret over waste) and wabi-sabi (beauty in the imperfect and time-worn).

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Piecing / patchwork / appliqué** — bringing together fragments of fabric (often recycled or remnant) to construct a larger whole; making something new from collected pieces of the past
- **Layering and quilting stitches** — the physical sandwiching of top fabric, batting, and backing, held together by stitching; the stitching is simultaneously functional (structural) and aesthetic (decorative)
- **Improvisation vs. pattern** — American quilting tradition contains both highly planned geometric patterns and loose, improvisational piecing, especially in African-American traditions (Gee's Bend)
- **Upcycling / economy of material** — quilts across all traditions share a commitment to reusing worn or remnant fabric; this is simultaneously practical, ecological, and symbolically rich
- **Collaborative making** — quilting bee (American), quilton/frolic (African-American) — social gatherings as both creative and communal practices; quilts as products of multiple hands across time
- **Quilts as storytelling and identity** — especially prominent in African-American traditions; quilts as tactile repositories of history
- **Kantha** (Bengali, literally "rags") — running-stitch quilting on recycled saris/dhotis; dense with regional symbolism (lotus, snake, peacocks, fish)
- **Bojagi / Jogakpo** (Korean) — pieced wrapping cloths with roots in the Joseon Dynasty; characterized by geometric shapes and a distinctive reverse-seam technique
- **Sashiko** (Japanese) — dense, rhythmic running-stitch patterns applied to indigo cotton; simultaneously functional (reinforcing worn cloth) and decorative
- **Boro** (Japanese, literally "rags/tatters") — the practice of mending, patching, and continuously repairing cloth over years and generations; philosophical equivalent of wabi-sabi in textile practice
- **Wabi-sabi** — Japanese aesthetic philosophy valuing imperfection, transience, and the beauty of the time-worn; wabi = simple/rustic beauty; sabi = silence/time-worn/irregular
- **Mottainai** — Japanese concept: regret over wasting something; a driver of Boro practice
- **Stitches as markers of time** — a quilt made over months or years records duration in its material; multiple dye lots, varying stitch density, and the evidence of different hands all make time visible in cloth

## Artists & Works Referenced

- **Addie Pearl Nicholson** — *Nine Block Half Log Cabin Variation*, 1974 — Gee's Bend, Alabama; improvisation within the log cabin structure
- **Harriet Powers** — Bible quilt, 1885–86 — scenes from Scripture rendered in appliquéd cotton; considered a grandmother of African-American quilting; introduced narrative, metonymy, and simplified form as adequate storytelling device
- **Ekta Kahl** — Kantha maps of London and Riverside Walk — contemporary London-based artist using Kantha as personal memory repository and geographical mapping practice
- **Do Ho Suh** — fabric sculptures of remembered apartments and *348 West 22nd Street* installation — South Korean-born artist using Jogakpo stitching and diaphanous polyester to explore memory, displacement, and home
- **Atsushi Futatsuya** — contemporary Sashiko/Boro practitioner; meticulous preservation of traditional needle technique; mending practice applied to blue jeans

## Core Takeaways

- Quilting is defined by two core operations — piecing (collecting fragments into a whole) and layering/stitching (holding multiple planes together) — and both operations carry conceptual weight beyond their technical function.
- Across every tradition surveyed, reuse and recycling of worn material is fundamental; the quilt is a technology for transforming what would have been discarded into something of renewed beauty and function.
- Quilts are objects that record time: they are made over long durations, by multiple hands, and carry the material traces of those different moments and makers.
- Wabi-sabi (Japan), mottainai (Japan), and the "baraka" of worn fibers (South Asia) all point to a cross-cultural idea: that imperfect, time-worn, reused material carries meaning that new material cannot.
- Contemporary artists (Kahl, Suh, Futatsuya) use these stitching traditions as entry points into questions of memory, mapping, displacement, and material ethics.

## Questions for Your Own Practice

1. Do you work with recycled, remnant, or worn materials? What would change conceptually if you introduced the logic of upcycling?
2. Where in your practice do you work with fragments or pieces assembled into a larger whole? What is the relationship between the individual fragment and the whole it joins?
3. How do you think about duration and accumulation in your work? Is there a way to make time — the time of making, the time of use, the time of repair — visible in the object itself?
4. What is your relationship to imperfection in your work? Does your practice have an equivalent of wabi-sabi — a philosophy that finds value in the irregular, the time-worn, and the visibly mended?
5. Are there stitching, mapping, or layering techniques from a tradition connected to your own cultural background or geography that you have not yet explored?
