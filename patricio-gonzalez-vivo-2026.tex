\markright{Bio}
\textbf{Patricio González Vivo} (b. 1982, Buenos Aires) is a multidisciplinary artist whose research-driven practice follows non-linear experiences such as memory, awareness and self-expression. Each project is another iteration of that inquiry, bridging analog and digital languages as ways of knowing.

\vspace{-\intextsep}

FILE Festival, São Paulo

Originally trained in clinical psychology and arts therapy, his two main mediums are computer code and oil paint, his early interactive installations in Buenos Aires explored how collective gestures create shared meaning. That work led to his first international exhibition, at \textbf{FILE Festival} São Paulo, and to an MFA at \textbf{Parsons School of Design} in New York, where his thesis introduced him to the drawing machines that anchor his practice today: custom plotters that translate perception into material trace.

Alongside his studio work, an ongoing commitment to open pedagogy led him to author the \href{https://thebookofshaders.com/}{\textbf{Book of Shaders}}, now a foundational resource for learning GLSL and shader programming used by artists and educators worldwide who work with computational art. In addition to his current career as a digital toolmaker, that same commitment has taken him into classrooms at \textbf{Parsons School of Design}'s graduate program, \textbf{ITP NYU}, and the \textbf{School for Poetic Computation}, and most recently into mentoring the \textbf{Processing Foundation}'s Lead Developer and serving as a dedicated mentor at \textbf{NEW INC}. He has also been invited to give talks for \textbf{STUDIO for Creative Inquiry} at \textbf{Carnegie Mellon University} and \textbf{Future Sketches} at the \textbf{MIT Media Lab}.

His work has been exhibited internationally at \textbf{Espacio Fundación Telefónica} in Buenos Aires, \textbf{FlyingTokyo} in Japan, the \textbf{Museum of Moving Image} in Queens, \textbf{EYEO} in Minneapolis, \textbf{Resonate} in Belgrade, \textbf{BrightMoments} in Buenos Aires, \textbf{FILE Festival} in São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro, and Petrópolis.

These are oil portraits extended into living images which hold the elusive essence of singularity. This dual nature has let me explore what is deliberate and what is unconscious. Pigments and pixels. Code and brushstrokes. Subjective and objective.

Each digital portrait is first the material trace of an intimate dialog, and then, when animated, into a recurring reenactment of the psychological exercise of recognition.

The sitters are mostly men, and the portraits take up that imbalance as their subject: the construction of masculinity at a moment when its crisis is hard to miss. What is being shaped from its pieces? What needs to be preserved? What transformed? What shed?

The portraits hold all these questions open by refusing to settle on an answer.

\noindent{\huge\textbf{\href{https://patriciogonzalezvivo.com/2026/santos/}{SANTOS}}}\hfill{\large \href{https://patriciogonzalezvivo.com/2025-2026/}{\textcolor{red}{2025-2026}}}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.400\textwidth}
\vspace{-\intextsep}
\noindent\hfill \href{https://patriciogonzalezvivo.com/2026/santos/}{\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=0.750\textheight,keepaspectratio]{2026/santos/thumbnail.jpg}}

In Latin America, it is common to receive the name of the saint whose feast day coincides with one's birth. To ask for someone's santo is, in effect, to ask for their birthday. This custom reflects a deep cultural belief: that the saint linked to your day of birth becomes a protector, a guide, and a source of inspiration. Saints, historical figures revered for their moral virtue and spiritual significance, are understood to transcend linear time. Through their acts of compassion and faith, they enter a more circular, mythological temporality, becoming enduring presences within the collective imagination of Christianity.

I was raised in a Catholic family. My grandfather used to read to me from a red book in his study, a compendium of saints' lives. Alongside brief narratives, there were illustrations, each saint marked by a halo, captured at the moment of transformation when the ordinary gave way to the transcendent. Again and again, these stories returned to a common thread: self-sacrifice, service, and devotion to others. The halo functioned as a visual language for this metaphysical shift.

That ethos of service extended into my family's expectations. Professions such as law, medicine, and engineering were valued as pathways toward a life of usefulness and contribution. In my lineage, I cannot trace a single artist. There is one fragile exception, my maternal great-grandmother, a poet who reportedly burned all her writings upon marriage, as though creativity were a deviation to be corrected, a path to be relinquished in favor of duty.

This series of portraits emerges from a personal search for an alternative lineage, one composed of figures whose service to humanity is enacted through creation. These are individuals committed to the discipline of imagination, capable of translating the invisible into form, and of reshaping the world through beauty, wonder, and possibility.

If there is a creator who invites us into existence as co-creators, then this lineage of artists stands as a kind of secular sainthood. They are not exemplars of moral virtue in the traditional sense, but they are, for me, figures of devotion, visionaries with whom I seek communion and dialogue.

These portraits continue an exploration of drawing machines begun with \href{https://patriciogonzalezvivo.com/2014/skylines/skylines.php?v=01}{\textbf{Skylines in 2014}}, for which I constructed a wall plotter that slowly reveals an image through custom software translating photographs into lines. In 2025, with the \href{https://patriciogonzalezvivo.com/2025/hybrids/}{\textbf{Hybrids series}}, I began combining plotting with alla prima oil painting, bringing together the forces of machine and hand, code and gesture. In Santos, I use that same approach: converting portrait images into vector paths layered with symbolic elements singular to each subject, plotting the result with acrylic paint on a primed canvas, then completing each portrait by hand with oil.

Behind every portrait in this series stands the absence of my great-grandmother, the poet who burned her writing. Santos is the lineage she was not permitted to enter.

\markright{\href{https://patriciogonzalezvivo.com/2026/weaver2/}{Weaver}, 2025-2026}
\noindent{\huge\textbf{\href{https://patriciogonzalezvivo.com/2026/weaver2/}{WEAVER}}}\hfill{\large \href{https://patriciogonzalezvivo.com/2025-2026/}{\textcolor{red}{2025-2026}}}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.400\textwidth}
\vspace{-\intextsep}
\noindent\hfill \href{https://patriciogonzalezvivo.com/2026/weaver2/}{\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=0.750\textheight,keepaspectratio]{2026/weaver2/thumbnail.jpg}}
\vspace{-\intextsep}
\end{wrapfigure}
Weaver is an interactive work that invites two people to meet across time and space through a shared fragment of the night sky. Like a prayer or a ritual, it offers a passage beyond immediate experience into a quiet awareness of what connects us, not despite distance, but through it.

In the middle of the nineteenth century, during the Great Potato Famine, Dudley Sarsfield Brennan and Mary Colcough left Ireland and crossed the Atlantic to Argentina. I imagine them on the deck at night, breathing the cold ocean air as familiar constellations disappeared and unknown stars emerged above them. I imagine a mixture of grief and hope, an awareness of being small beneath the immensity of the universe: a ship crossing a dark sea on a planet drifting through space. They eventually settled in Magdalena, where their son Patricio was born in 1870. 

\begin{wrapfigure}{l}{0.16\textwidth}
\vspace{-\intextsep}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,keepaspectratio]{temp_portfolio/html_renders/looom_000_light_f0.png}
\vspace{-\intextsep}
\end{wrapfigure}


I stand at the far end of their horizon, beneath a different sky, with only fragments of their stories and the sky itself. From this desire to connect across time, I created Weaver: a way of threading my present to their future and my past to their present, a way of seeing what they saw and allowing future generations to see what I see.

Two celestial maps overlap: one for each observer. Each is rendered in a distinct color, reflecting the night sky from a specific place and moment. In the region where the maps intersect lie the constellations both participants share, a common sky that exists beyond distance or time.

Each map uses a polar projection. Stars near the outer edge rest close to the horizon (marked by the cardinal directions), while those at the center hover directly overhead. This perspective situates each observer within their own sky, while revealing where their visions align.

Beside each map, a rotating globe marks the observer's location. Both the celestial maps and the globes are fully interactive. By dragging the sky, you can shift the date and time; by rotating the globe, you can reposition the observer anywhere on Earth. Mounted as a site-specific installation, one map can be fixed to the present location, anchoring the visitor's own sky, while the second opens onto a single chosen place and time, or sequences through several.

\markright{Artist Statement}

\section*{Artist Statement}

In my grandfather's book of saints, a halo marked the moment the ordinary gave way to the transcendent. I think of art making as that moment set into matter: what I see and what I carry inside, pressed into a material that can hold it after the hand is gone.

I work in two materials, oil and code. A plotter draws the geometry of a face onto a primed canvas in acrylic, sixteen by twelve inches, and I return with oil until the face looks back. The same portraits also live as code, strokes that repaint a sitter again and again, so each viewing is a first meeting and a repetition. In other works the material is the sky: two star maps, drawn for two places and two moments, overlapping where both observers would have seen the same stars.

Oil keeps the trace of my heartbeat, the gesture of my breathing. Code can return to it in an ever expanding loop. I keep both because each carries time in its own way, and because meaning arrives where they meet.

Time in my work does not run in a line. The people I paint, Baldwin and Butler among them, keep arriving, the way old light arrives from a star. My great-grandmother, a poet, reportedly burned everything she wrote when she married, and I am building the lineage that was cut. In the family I come from there is a burned page. In the work there is a face that looks back, and then past me, toward whoever comes next.
